Search **Jagalchi** in English and you will get Busan. Wikipedia, guidebooks, a dedicated tourism domain, a hundred blog posts about Korea's largest seafood market and its famous rows of tanks and knives.

You will not get the snack. Which is a shame, because **자갈치** is also a bag of ridged, octopus-shaped crackers that Nongshim has been making since **1983**, that is a household name in Korea, that is a genuine phenomenon in Russia, and that is sold in the United States under a completely different name.

It is also not made of octopus.

## The Name Is Three Steps Away From a Fish

![Inside Jagalchi fish market in Busan, South Korea, with Korean shop signage above the stalls.](/assets/images/posts/414/jagalchi-market-busan.jpg)
*Jagalchi market in Busan, the place the snack is named after. Korean stall signage overhead; the market is the reference the brand assumes you already have. Photo by Bruna Santos via Pexels.*

![Chart showing the chain 자갈 pebbles to 자갈치시장 the Busan fish market to 자갈치 the Nongshim snack launched 5 August 1983, with a panel noting the snack is not named after the real fish called 자갈치.](/assets/images/posts/414/jagalchi-name-chain.jpg)
*EpicKor chart.*

Start at the end and work backwards.

**자갈** means *pebbles*. The stretch of Busan waterfront where the market grew up was covered in them, and that is the entire origin of the place name — **자갈치시장**, the pebble market.

That market became Korea's most famous seafood market, and in **1983 Nongshim borrowed its name for a snack**, because in Korean the word instantly says *seafood* the way "Billingsgate" or "Fulton" might to an English speaker.

**There is also a real fish called 자갈치**, and the snack is not named after it. Korean sources are explicit about this, and the reason is practical: it is a deep-sea species most Koreans will never see, so it carries no association at all. The market does.

So the chain is pebbles → market → snack, and English-language search has only ever known about the middle one.

## Abroad It Is Called Something Else Entirely

![Nongshim Tako Chips retail bag: pink packaging with 자갈치 in Korean above the words TAKO CHIPS in yellow, a cartoon octopus in a chef's hat, seafood flavor, big size, 10.09 oz / 286 g.](/assets/images/posts/414/jagalchi-tako-chips-pack.jpg)
*The US retail bag. 자갈치 is kept in Korean; the English name is Tako Chips. Image: Nongshim USA official product page.*

The American bag is worth reading carefully, because it makes three separate naming decisions at once.

**자갈치 stays**, in Korean, above everything else — so the Korean shopper still finds the product they know.

**The English name is Tako Chips.** *Tako* (たこ) is the Japanese word for octopus. Nongshim did not translate 자갈치, did not romanise it, and did not use an English word: it reached for a **Japanese** one, presumably because Western consumers already knew it from takoyaki.

**The descriptor is "seafood flavor"**, not octopus flavour. That turns out to be a very carefully chosen phrase.

There is a further twist recorded in Korean sources. At launch, Korea marketed the flavour as *octopus* because **takoyaki** — which is what it actually tastes like — was an unfamiliar food in Korea in 1983, so the ingredient was easier to name than the dish. Forty years later the export name went to the dish's own language.

## Read the Allergen Line

![Chart of the Nongshim USA ingredient list for Tako Chips: corvina, anchovy, cutlassfish, pollock and shrimp present, octopus and squid absent, alongside wheat flour, corn starch, rice bran oil, beef bone extract and black pepper.](/assets/images/posts/414/jagalchi-ingredients.jpg)
*EpicKor chart, from Nongshim USA's published ingredient and allergen statement.*

Nongshim USA lists the ingredients for Tako Chips as wheat flour, corn starch, rice bran oil, **minced corvina**, palm oil, rice flour and sugar, plus under 2% each of anchovy extract, artificial flavour, beef bone extract, black pepper and fish paste.

The allergen line reads: **contains wheat, fish (corvina, anchovy, cutlassfish, pollock), shrimp, soy and milk.**

**There is no octopus and no squid in it.** Four fish and a crustacean, and the octopus is the shape.

Nongshim's own product copy does not pretend otherwise. It describes a snack *"crafted from a blend of fish and flour"* with a *"playful octopus shape."* Read closely, the company is being exact — it is the English brand name that does the implying.

Two practical notes fall out of that list. **Corvina is 조기**, a croaker, and **cutlassfish is 갈치** — both completely ordinary Korean fish that almost never appear on a Western label, which is a good short description of what this snack tastes of. And there is **beef bone extract** in it, so this is not a fish-only or pescatarian product, plus **shrimp**, which matters if you avoid shellfish.

**The Korean bag makes the opposite claim, in the largest type on the front.** It is sold domestically as **자갈치 문어맛** — octopus flavour — under the headline **맛도 모양도 문어!**, "both the taste and the shape are octopus." Korean-language sources agree the domestic formulation does contain some octopus.

So the two markets are not saying the same thing about the same product. Korea says octopus twice on the front of the bag; the American bag says *seafood flavor* and its ingredient list has no cephalopod in it. Formulations differ by market and change over time, and the packet in your hand is always the authority — but it is worth knowing that the honest version of the claim is the export one.

## It Is Bigger in Russia Than You Would Guess

자갈치 launched on **5 August 1983** as Nongshim's major follow-up to 새우깡, and it settled into the small group of Korean snacks — with 바나나킥, 새우깡, 양파링 and 포스틱 — that Koreans call **국민과자**, national snacks. A 2006 poll of Korean internet users in their twenties and thirties put it fourth among long-running snacks.

The more surprising fact is where else it took hold. Korean sources describe 자갈치 as a **major hit in Russia**, alongside 꽃게랑 and Choco Pie, with the usual explanation being that inland regions have little access to seafood flavours and these fill the gap.

The reception in English-speaking markets has been less warm. The American YouTube duo Smosh tried it and hated it — their summary was that they had hoped it would not taste like octopus, and it did. **That is a fair description of the risk.** This is a savoury, briny, faintly spicy snack with a distinct fish-paste base, and it is not trying to be mild.

There is one more layer to the name. In **2025 Nongshim's parent group opened a seafood-focused supermarket in the United States** using the same name. So *Jagalchi* now denotes a Busan market, a Nongshim snack, and an American grocery chain.

## What It Looks Like, and Where It Came From

The crackers are small, ridged and unmistakably shaped like a little octopus — a body and splayed legs, deep-scored so they stay rigid.

Korean sources note that the form closely resembles **타이아라레 (鯛あられ)**, a traditional Japanese arare moulded in the shape of a sea bream, and suggest Nongshim looked at it when designing the product. The joke that follows is a real one: Korean students in Japan buy 타이아라레 expecting 자갈치 and get a shock, **because the Japanese version is sweet.**

Korean descriptions of 자갈치's own shape vary between an octopus and a **쭈꾸미**, the webfoot octopus — which is smaller, and which Koreans eat far more often than they eat octopus.

## What It Costs and How to Eat It

A Korean record from **2023 puts the 90 g bag at ₩1,700**, which is standard territory for a Nongshim snack bag and has not moved dramatically since. The US export bag is a **286 g "BIG SIZE" zipper pack**, which is roughly three times the Korean one — worth knowing before you assume the sizes are comparable.

**Eat it with beer.** This is the standard Korean use and it is not a throwaway line: the snack is salty and savoury enough that it works as 안주, drinking food, in a way sweet snacks do not. Korean commentary about 자갈치 mentions beer more often than it mentions children.

**Buy the smallest bag first if you are unsure.** The fish-paste flavour is the entire product. If you like Korean fish cake, dried anchovy stock or 어묵, you will probably like this. If those are not for you, the size of the bag is not going to help.

**One cultural footnote worth having.** The late President Roh Moo-hyun was known to be fond of it, and photographs of him eating 자갈치 with his granddaughter circulate in Korea. It is that kind of snack — unglamorous, specific, and attached to people's memories rather than to marketing.

## Honest Notes

**The English name oversells the octopus.** Tako Chips means octopus chips. The US ingredient list has no octopus. Nongshim's own descriptive copy is accurate about this, but the brand name on the front is doing something else.

**It is a fish snack, not a seafood-flavoured potato snack.** The base is wheat and starch bound with minced corvina and fish paste. That is a fundamentally different thing from a potato chip with seasoning powder, and it is why reactions to it are so strongly split.

**It has shrimp and beef bone extract in it.** Neither is implied by the name. Check the packet if either matters to you.

**The 자갈치 you find in a search engine is a market, not this.** If you are trying to buy the snack online, search *Tako Chips* or *Nongshim 자갈치*; searching *Jagalchi* will send you to Busan travel guides and now, in the US, to a supermarket.

**Formulations differ by market.** Korean sources describe the domestic version as containing some octopus while the published US ingredient list does not. Do not treat one bag as evidence about the other.

## FAQ

**Q: Does 자갈치 contain octopus?**
**A:** Not in the US version. Nongshim USA's allergen statement lists fish (corvina, anchovy, cutlassfish, pollock) and shrimp, with no cephalopod. Korean sources say the domestic formulation includes some octopus. The shape is an octopus in both.

**Q: Why is it called Jagalchi?**
**A:** After Busan's 자갈치시장 seafood market, whose own name comes from 자갈, the pebbles on that stretch of shore. It is not named after the fish that shares the name.

**Q: What is it called in English?**
**A:** **Tako Chips**, from the Japanese word for octopus. The Korean name stays on the bag in Hangul.

**Q: What does it taste like?**
**A:** Salty, savoury and faintly spicy, with a clear fish-paste base — closer to takoyaki than to plain octopus. Korean sources say the original 1983 marketing called it octopus flavour only because takoyaki was unfamiliar in Korea at the time.

**Q: How much does it cost?**
**A:** About ₩1,700 for a 90 g bag as recorded in 2023. The US export bag is a 286 g resealable size.
