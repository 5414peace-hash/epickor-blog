You are staying at a Korean relative's apartment, or a countryside guesthouse, and the bed in the spare room is a slab of rock. Not a firm mattress. Actual polished stone, set into a wooden frame, with a small temperature controller hanging off the side.

Nothing is broken and nothing is missing. That is the bed. It is called a **Korean stone bed** — 돌침대, *dolchimdae* — and the entry-level single costs **₩3,470,000**, roughly **$2,510**. The king configurations pass ₩7 million.

That price is the part almost nobody outside Korea knows, and it changes how you read the object. This is not a hard mattress somebody cheaped out on. It is one of the more expensive pieces of furniture in the apartment.

## Why This Exists At All

Korea heated its floors for centuries before it heated its rooms. **온돌** — ondol — ran flue gas from the kitchen fire under a thick masonry floor, so the floor itself was the radiator. People sat on it, ate on it, and slept on it, on a thin 요 mattress laid down at night and folded away in the morning. Our [Korean floor culture guide](/blog/229) covers what that still means for shoes, slippers and low tables in a modern apartment.

Modern Korean apartments still have heated floors. What changed is the bodies. **Getting down to the floor and back up again is the part that stops working**, and it stops working exactly at the age when a warm hard surface feels best.

The stone bed is the answer to that specific problem. It is not an attempt to build a better mattress. It is an attempt to keep the warm hard floor while removing the part where you have to get down onto it. Read that way, everything else about the object makes sense — the height, the hardness, the heating element, and the customer.

## Stone Bed vs Clay Bed: The Comparison Koreans Actually Make

If you search this in Korean, the question that comes up is not "stone bed or mattress." It is **흙침대와 돌침대** — clay bed or stone bed. They are two products in the same category and Koreans choose between them on one property: how fast the surface changes temperature.

<div class="table-scroll">

| | 돌침대 Stone bed | 흙침대 Clay bed |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Natural stone — 맥반석, 게르마늄, granite, marble | **황토**, Korean loess clay |
| Heat-up | **Fast.** High thermal conductivity | Slow |
| Cool-down after power off | **Fast** | Slow — holds warmth for a long time |
| Feel | Harder | Slightly more give |
| Suits | People who want heat now, and a cool surface in summer | People sensitive to temperature swings |

</div>

The practical read: **stone is responsive and clay is stable**. A stone bed reaches your set temperature quickly and drops just as quickly when you switch it off, which is also why Koreans describe it as cool in summer — bare stone at room temperature pulls heat out of you. A clay bed takes longer to warm and then stays warm well past the switch, which is better if you are the kind of sleeper who wakes up when the temperature moves.

Both are heated the same way, and both are sold by the same companies, often in the same showroom.

## The Company That Turned This Into a Category

**장수돌침대** is to stone beds what Hoover was to vacuum cleaners, and the story is worth knowing because it explains why the product looks the way it does.

최창환 founded the company in **1992**. The stated motivation was his wife's pain after childbirth, and the idea came from ondol — radiant heat from a hard warm surface, at bed height.

**Department stores and furniture retailers refused to stock it**, on the reasonable grounds that a slab of rock was not a recognised furniture category. So he took it to television home shopping instead, which was then a new channel. The first broadcast did **₩700 million**.

That worked well enough that imitators arrived — at one point **sixteen brands** were using the 장수 name — and the founder's response is the reason most Koreans can quote this company at you. He appeared in his own commercials and delivered the line **"별이 다섯 개"** — *five stars* — with an earnestness that turned it into a national catchphrase. It is the kind of ad Koreans describe as 촌스럽다, unfashionable, in the affectionate way people say that about something that has outlasted the fashion. The clip passed a million views when it was re-uploaded in 2020.

## Is It Good For Your Back? Korean Sources Say: Not On Its Own

This is the part where an English article about Korean wellness usually starts listing benefits. The Korean-language material is more sceptical than the English-language material is, and it is worth reporting accurately.

The argument against a bare stone surface is mechanical, not mystical. **Your spine holds an S-curve.** Lying flat on something that does not compress at all, your shoulders and buttocks take the contact and your lumbar region is left spanning a gap with nothing under it. The muscles and ligaments around those vertebrae then spend the night holding the unsupported section in place instead of resting.

The described result is morning stiffness and pain, and Korean writing on the subject goes further — sustained over years, it is presented as capable of **inducing or worsening a disc herniation or spinal stenosis** rather than relieving one. That is the exact opposite of the reason most buyers say they bought it.

**The remedy is not to throw the bed away.** The standard Korean recommendation is a **3–5cm topper** — latex or memory foam — laid over the stone. That restores enough compression for the lumbar curve while the heat still comes through, which was the point of the bed in the first place. If you are sleeping on one in someone's spare room and your back hurts in the morning, this is the thing to ask for, and most Korean households already have one folded in a closet.

## Low-Temperature Burns Are the Real Safety Issue

Not electrical safety. Heat, applied gently, for hours.

**저온화상** — low-temperature burn — is the injury you get from a surface that never feels dangerous. Skin held against something at **40–50°C** long enough will take heat deep into the tissue, and around **45°C** sustained is enough to burn. You do not flinch away because it does not hurt while it is happening, and the classic case is exactly this one: a high setting, bare skin, and a full night asleep.

Three practical rules follow, and they are the same rules Koreans apply to heated floors, electric blankets and 핫팩 hand warmers:

- **Do not sleep on bare stone at a high setting.** Bedding between you and the surface is not optional.
- **Turn it down, not up, before sleeping.** The bed keeps delivering heat for hours after you stop noticing it.
- **Be more careful, not less, if you have reduced sensation** — diabetic neuropathy in particular, and after drinking. Both remove the feedback that would otherwise move you.

Korea's consumer agency issues the same warning every winter about hand warmers, which are a far smaller heat source than a bed.

## Should You Actually Buy One

For most readers of this article, honestly, no — and the useful outcome is understanding what you are looking at rather than buying it.

**Reasons it might genuinely suit you:** you already prefer very firm sleeping surfaces; you like heat and want it in the surface rather than in the room; you are furnishing a home in Korea long-term and will use it for a decade.

**Reasons to walk away:** you are a side sleeper, in which case the hardness is working against your hip and shoulder; you rent and will move; you want it for a health claim, because the honest reading of Korean sources is that a bare slab is a back-pain risk rather than a back-pain cure. And the shipping arithmetic ends the conversation for anyone outside Korea — these weigh what a slab of stone weighs.

## What Koreans Use Instead, and What Travellers Meet

The stone bed is the expensive, permanent end of a spectrum, and most Korean homes are somewhere else on it.

The mass-market answer is the heated mat laid over an ordinary mattress — **온수매트** circulating warm water, or **전기장판** with an electric element. ## FAQ

**Q: How much does a Korean stone bed cost?**

**A:** ₩3,470,000 for the entry single from 장수돌침대, about $2,510, rising to ₩6,190,000–₩7,160,000 for king configurations. Those are official-shop list prices checked in August 2026; dealer and home-shopping prices run lower, and there is an active second-hand and rental market.

**Q: Is a Korean stone bed good for back pain?**

**A:** Not as a bare slab. Korean orthopaedic writing describes the opposite mechanism — an incompressible surface leaves the lumbar curve unsupported, so the muscles around it stay tense all night, and sustained over years it is presented as a risk factor for disc problems rather than a treatment. The standard Korean fix is a 3–5cm latex or memory foam topper over the stone, which keeps the heat and restores the support.

**Q: What is the difference between 돌침대 and 흙침대?**

**A:** Material and thermal behaviour. 돌침대 uses natural stone and conducts heat quickly, so it warms fast and cools fast. 흙침대 uses 황토 loess clay, warms slowly, and holds heat long after you switch it off. Koreans choose stone for responsiveness and a cool summer surface, clay for temperature stability.

**Q: Can you buy a Korean stone bed outside Korea?**

**A:** Practically, no. They weigh what a stone slab weighs and are sold through Korean dealer networks. The exportable version of the idea is a heated jade or tourmaline mat, or the water-circulating and electric mats Korean households use over ordinary mattresses.

**Q: Is it safe to sleep on a heated stone bed all night?**

**A:** With bedding between you and the surface and a moderate setting, yes — it is a mainstream Korean product. The risk to know about is 저온화상, low-temperature burns, which happen at 40–50°C over long exposure because the heat never feels dangerous enough to make you move. Do not sleep on bare stone at a high setting, and be more cautious if you have reduced sensation in your skin.

**Q: Why does the bed have a glass panel over the stone?**

**A:** Tempered glass over the slab is standard on many models. It keeps the stone surface sealed and easy to clean while still conducting the heat through, and it protects the stone from chipping.

## The One Thing to Remember

A Korean stone bed is not a mattress somebody made too hard. It is an **온돌 floor lifted to bed height** for people whose knees stopped agreeing to floor life, and it is priced like the serious furniture it is — ₩3.47 million to start.

If you meet one in a spare room, the useful move is not to admire it or endure it. It is to ask whether the household has a topper.
