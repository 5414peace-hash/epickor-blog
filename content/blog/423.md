## Korea Built a Category in December 1996

![A CJ Hetbahn 210 g bowl, cream plastic with a red band, the 햇반 logo, the English line Cooked White Rice, and Korean label text across the sealed film lid.](/assets/images/posts/423/hetbahn-210g-bowl.jpg)
*The standard 210 g bowl. The lid carries the brand, the English line, the weight and the energy figure — 310 kcal on this version, 315 on the current one. Image: CJ Newsroom.*

Shelf-stable rice existed before 1996. What did not exist was rice that tasted enough like rice for a country that eats it three times a day.

In **March 1996**, CheilJedang put about **₩10 billion** into a facility for **aseptic packaging** — 무균화 포장. **Hetbahn launched in December of that year** and became the company's best-selling product.

The technique is the interesting part, and it borrows its standard from an unrelated industry. Retort food is sealed and then heated, which cooks the contents a second time and costs you texture. Aseptic packaging inverts that: the rice is **sterilised with high-temperature, high-pressure steam**, cooked in purified water, and then **sealed inside a clean room** — one CJ describes as cleaner than a hospital operating theatre and comparable to a semiconductor fab.

Nothing is cooked after sealing. That is why it tastes like rice and not like a boil-in-bag.

The container carries the rest of the load. **The bowl is a three-layer structure and the lid film is four layers**, which is what keeps air, temperature and humidity out for nine months on a warm shelf.

**One consequence matters if you are buying:** the seal is the entire preservation system. A bowl with a punctured or lifted film is not a bargain, it is a spoiled product waiting to happen. CJ's own consumer explainer makes exactly this point — mould gets in through a hole in the film. **Check the lid before you pay.**

## What Is Actually In It

This is the question the label raises and no English page answers, because the answer is printed in Korean.

**햇반 is 99.9% Korean rice and water.** The remaining **0.1% is 미강추출물** — rice bran extract, which functions as an acidity regulator.

That single ingredient caused a genuine controversy in Korea in 2021, and the shape of it is worth knowing.

The extract was **imported from Japan**, because Japanese producers had the higher-purity extraction technology. When Korean consumers noticed 산도조절제 and a Japanese origin on a product marketed on Korean rice, it became a news story. CJ's response at the time was that the extract came from a plant roughly **800 km from Fukushima** and that it constituted **0.1% of the product**.

**Then the company changed the answer instead of repeating it.** CJ developed a domestic extraction process for Hetbahn, applied it first to the multigrain line, then to about 20% of total volume, then targeted 50% within the quarter and **100% domestic by the end of that year**.

Two things follow for a reader deciding whether to buy it.

**First, "acidity regulator" on a Korean instant-rice label is not a preservative in the sense people fear.** The preservation is done by the sterile packaging, not by chemistry. The extract is there to hold the rice at the right acidity.

**Second, the ingredient list really is that short.** Rice, water, and a rice-derived extract at one part in a thousand. If you have been avoiding instant rice on the assumption that it must be full of additives, the Korean label does not support that assumption.

## How to Cook It, Including Without a Microwave

![Chart of the Hetbahn 210 g cooking instructions: microwave one bowl for 2 minutes, microwave two bowls for 3 minutes, or boiling water for 10 minutes with the film left on and the pan lid closed.](/assets/images/posts/423/hetbahn-cooking-methods.jpg)
*EpicKor chart, from the Korean instruction panel printed on the lid.*

The Korean lid prints **three** numbers. English-language listings print one.

- **Microwave, one bowl — 2 minutes.**
- **Microwave, two bowls — 3 minutes.**
- **Boiling water — 10 minutes.**

**The third line is why this article exists.** If you are in a guesthouse kitchen, a dorm, a hotel room with a kettle and no microwave, or camping, you do not need a microwave to eat Hetbahn. Put the sealed bowl in a pan of boiling water, **leave the film on**, put the pan lid on, and give it about 10 minutes. Korean cooking sites put the practical range at 10 to 12 minutes and report the rice comes out firmer and more separated than the microwave version, which is a fair description.

Two things to get right with the boiling method:

- **Do not peel the film.** The sealed bowl is what steams the rice. Open it and you are boiling rice in water, which is a different and worse outcome.
- **Keep the pan lid closed.** It is the difference between 10 minutes and considerably longer.

One discrepancy worth flagging honestly: the **US Amazon listing for Hetbahn says 90 seconds** in the microwave, while the **Korean lid says 2 minutes**. Neither is wrong — they assume different oven wattages. If 90 seconds leaves cool patches in the middle, that is what the extra 30 seconds is for.

## The Lid Is the Part People Get Wrong

![Top-down view of a Hetbahn 210 g bowl on a wooden table, showing the sealed lid film with the Korean cooking-instruction panel, from CJ's own consumer explainer video with Korean subtitles.](/assets/images/posts/423/hetbahn-lid-cooking-panel.jpg)
*The instruction panel sits on the right of the lid: one bowl 2 minutes, two bowls 3, boiling water 10. The subtitle is from CJ's own explainer, warning that mould gets in through a damaged film. Image: CJ Newsroom.*

For the microwave, the correct move is neither of the two obvious ones.

**Tear the lid film only as far as the dotted line (점선).** That is what the printed instruction says, and there is a reason on each side of it:

- **Leave it fully sealed** and pressure builds inside. The bowl swells, and in the worst case it pops.
- **Peel it fully off** and too much moisture escapes. The rice dries and hardens at the edges.

Opening to the dotted line vents the steam at the rate the container was designed for. It is a small thing that changes the result, and it is printed in Korean only, which is why so many first-time users abroad get a bowl that is either bulging or dry.

## The Rest of the Family

The brand now covers **eight product lines**, according to CJ's own brand page, and they are not variations on one idea.

- **밥 (cooked rice)** — the plain 210 g bowl, and the size ladder above.
- **잡곡밥 (multigrain)** — added in **2003**. Black rice, brown rice, mixed grains.
- **햇반 컵반 (Cupbahn)** — rice plus a stew or topping in a cup. This is the one that turns instant rice into a meal.
- **햇반 솥반 (Sotbahn)** — the premium line, styled after 솥밥, stone-pot rice.
- **곤약밥 (konjac rice)** — rice cut with konjac for lower energy density.
- **볶음밥 (fried rice)**, **주먹밥 (rice balls)**, **죽 (porridge)** — brought under the Hetbahn name in **2022**.

For a visitor, the practical shortlist is short. **Plain white 210 g** if you want rice to go with something. **Cupbahn** if you want a whole meal from a convenience store shelf. **Multigrain** if you would eat brown rice at home anyway.

## When Not to Buy It

Here is the argument against, which the brand pages will not make for you.

**If you cook rice regularly, a rice cooker is cheaper by a wide margin.** A 210 g bowl of Hetbahn costs ₩1,079 at the best online price. The same weight of cooked rice from a bag of Korean rice costs a fraction of that. The premium buys you shelf stability and two minutes, not rice.

**If you only need rice and do not care whose it is, the convenience-store own brands are cheaper.** CU's HEYROO instant rice is **₩990**, roughly ₩700 less than a branded bowl at the same counter. Korean coverage has framed it as instant rice priced below a pack of gum. If you are buying one bowl to eat right now in a GS25 or a CU, the own brand does the same job.

**And there is packaging.** Each bowl is a multilayer plastic container plus a multilayer film lid, per portion. CJ has published on the recyclability of the bowl, but the honest framing is that a household eating instant rice daily generates a meaningful amount of packaging that a rice cooker does not.

**Where Hetbahn genuinely wins:** one person cooking for one, a household where nobody wants to run a cooker for a single portion, a camping trip, a hotel room, a desk drawer, or the specific Korean situation of eating at 11pm after work. That is not a small set of cases — it is why the category exists, and why 66.9% of it belongs to one brand.

## FAQ

**Q: Can you cook Hetbahn without a microwave?**
**A:** Yes, and the method is printed on the Korean lid. Put the sealed bowl into boiling water with the film left on, cover the pan, and heat for about 10 minutes — Korean sources put the practical range at 10 to 12 minutes. Do not peel the film; the sealed bowl is what steams the rice.

**Q: Do you peel the lid before microwaving?**
**A:** Only to the dotted line. Fully sealed builds pressure and the bowl can swell or burst. Fully peeled lets too much moisture escape and the rice dries out. Tearing to the printed 점선 vents it at the designed rate.

**Q: What is actually in Hetbahn?**
**A:** 99.9% Korean rice and water, plus 0.1% rice bran extract (미강추출물) acting as an acidity regulator. There is no preservative doing the shelf-life work — that is done by sterilising and sealing the bowl in a clean room, with nothing reheated after sealing.

**Q: Why does it last nine months without a fridge?**
**A:** Aseptic packaging. The rice is sterilised with high-temperature, high-pressure steam, cooked in purified water and sealed in a clean room CJ compares to a semiconductor plant. The bowl is a three-layer structure and the lid film four layers. If the film is damaged, none of that holds — check the lid before buying.

**Q: How much does it cost in Korea?**
**A:** About ₩1,700 for a single 210 g bowl in a convenience store as of August 2026, falling to roughly ₩1,079 per bowl buying 24 online. Convenience-store own brands such as CU's HEYROO sell instant rice at ₩990.

**Q: What is the difference between Hetbahn and Cupbahn?**
**A:** 햇반 is rice alone in a bowl. 햇반 컵반 is rice plus a stew, sauce or topping in a cup, so it is a complete meal rather than a side. Sotbahn is the premium line styled after Korean stone-pot rice.

## Final Take

Korea did not invent shelf-stable rice. It invented shelf-stable rice that a rice-eating country would accept, by borrowing a clean-room standard from electronics manufacturing and refusing to reheat anything after the seal.

What is in the bowl is short enough to read in one line: **rice, water, and a rice-derived extract at one part in a thousand.** What is on the lid is three cooking times, and the third one — **10 minutes in boiling water, film on** — means you never actually needed the microwave.

And if you are standing in a Korean convenience store with ₩1,700 in your hand, that is the right price for the situation you are in. The bulk maths is for people who live here.
