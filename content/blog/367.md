*바나나킥. The bag says **바나나맛 그대로** — "banana taste, just as it is." What is inside is corn. Official brand image, Nongshim.*

A twelve-month-old reached into a bag of **바나나킥**, and her mother said *안 되는데* — "you shouldn't." The baby froze, looked at the bag, and scratched the back of her head.

That clip has **25.8 million views**, 1.48 million likes and about 12,000 comments. This week Nongshim responded by manufacturing a one-off pack with the baby's face on it and the words **바나나킥 베이비**, in a bag taller than she is.

Nobody outside Korea has written about what is actually in that bag. Here is the short version: **it is not a banana snack.** It is a corn puff with 1.6% banana powder in it, and the reason a one-year-old can eat it is the same reason it has survived on Korean shelves since 1978.

## It Was the First Thing Nongshim Made as Nongshim

This is the fact that gives the bag its position in Korea, and it is not in English anywhere.

Until 1978 the company was called **롯데공업** — Lotte Industries. In May of that year it renamed itself **농심**, and Korean-language reference sources record Banana Kick as **the first snack released under the new name**. Nongshim's own brand page puts it plainly: *바나나킥은 1978년 출시 후… 현재까지 많은 사람들에게 사랑받고 있는 장수제품* — a long-life product, still loved.

So when a Korean adult picks up a yellow bag, they are not picking up a novelty. They are picking up something that has been in the same shape, in the same colour, for nearly fifty years. That matters for how the viral baby clip landed at home: the joke was not "look at the funny snack," it was "look at her, doing what all of us did."

## Why a One-Year-Old Can Eat It

Open a bag and the pieces look solid. They are not. Each puff is **hollow and porous**, and it collapses against the roof of the mouth almost immediately — closer to a prawn cracker than to a corn chip.

Korean sources describe the intended eaters directly: children, **people with weak teeth, and elderly denture wearers**. That is an unusual thing for a snack company to design around, and it explains the entire product. There is nothing to bite through.

It also explains something you will notice at the end of the bag. Because the coating is a fine sugar dust on a very light puff, a lot of it shakes loose in transit and **collects in the bottom corner**. Koreans tip that last inch straight into their mouth. It is intensely sweet and it is the best part, and no one abroad is told about it.

## The 1.6% Number, and Why It Is Not a Scandal

The original bag contains **1.6% banana powder**. Variants run lower — between roughly 0.2% and 0.9% depending on the version.

If you come to this expecting fruit, that number will annoy you. It should not. Banana Kick is not competing with a banana; it is competing with the memory of one. The flavour is deliberately closer to **banana candy** than to the fruit, which is exactly what Jennie of BLACKPINK described when she introduced it on American television in March 2025: not the typical banana taste you would expect.

That appearance had a measurable effect. Korean reference sources put the resulting jump in Nongshim's market capitalisation at roughly **₩264 billion**, and Banana Kick, shrimp chips and whale crackers all spiked in search interest afterwards.

Worth being precise about what happened there, because the English-language internet has this backwards: **Jennie did not make Banana Kick popular in Korea.** It was already a fifty-year-old staple. She made it visible outside Korea, which is a different thing.

## What to Compare It To, If You Have Never Had One

The most common mistake made abroad is filing this next to **banana chips** — the dried, sliced, faintly oily kind sold in health-food aisles. They have nothing in common. Dried banana chips are dense, chewy and genuinely fruit. Banana Kick is airy, sweet and genuinely corn.

The closer references are things you already know:

<div class="table-scroll">

| If you know this | Banana Kick is… |
|---|---|
| **Cheese puffs** (Cheetos-style) | The same puffed-corn body and the same dissolve, sweet instead of savoury, and not greasy — nothing comes off on your fingers but sugar dust |
| **Banana Runts or banana taffy** | The same artificial-banana note, but carried on air instead of sugar glass, so it is far less aggressive |
| **Prawn crackers** | The closest texture match in the world, minus the seafood |
| **Dried banana chips** | Nothing alike. Do not use this as your reference |

</div>

That "not greasy" point is worth dwelling on, because it is the practical difference at a Korean table. Puffed corn snacks in Korea are largely eaten with clean hands and passed around; Banana Kick leaves nothing on a car seat or a keyboard. It is the snack Korean parents hand to a child in the back seat for exactly that reason, and it is why it turns up at desks, on trains and in hospital rooms rather than only in front of a television.

The other thing English-language listings never mention: **the pieces are not uniform.** Some are near-straight, some are curled almost into a ring. Korean children sort them. There is no official rare shape and no prize — it is simply what you do with a bag of them at age six, and it is another small reason the snack keeps a generation of adults attached to it.

## Melon Kick and Mango Kick Are New

If you last looked at this shelf a few years ago, it has changed. The **-킥** line is now three products:

| Banana Kick | 바나나킥 | **May 1978** |
| Melon Kick | 메론킥 | **April 2025** |
| Mango Kick | 망고킥 | **May 2026** |

Mango Kick is three months old at the time of writing. Melon Kick is barely a year older. Both use the same puffed-corn base and the same collapse-in-the-mouth texture, with different fruit powders and different coloured bags — green for melon, orange for mango.

There have also been a chocolate version (October 2016) and a mini format (August 2019), plus collaborations that ran through ice cream, milk, cereal and popcorn. The chocolate one is worth a warning: **Korean consumers criticised it fairly bluntly on appearance.** A brown, lumpy version of a snack whose entire identity is a clean pale yellow did not land.

## What Nongshim Actually Sent the Baby

Because this is the part that made the news, and the detail is specific.

The video went up on Instagram in **March 2026** and kept climbing until it passed 25.8 million views. In **August 2026**, Nongshim produced a custom set — Banana Kick, Melon Kick and Mango Kick — with the child's real face printed on the packaging next to the phrase **바나나킥 베이비**.

The oversized bag is **taller than the baby**, who is 80 cm. Inside it were roughly twenty normal retail bags. Korean press was clear on one point: **it is not going on sale.** There is no Banana Kick Baby edition to hunt for. It was made once, for one child.

## What It Costs, and Where to Buy It

As of **August 2026**, a standard 75 g bag runs about **₩1,360–1,390** through Korean online grocers. Convenience stores sit higher, as they do for every snack in Korea, and they run 1+1 and 2+1 promotions on the -킥 line often enough that it is worth checking the shelf tag before you pay.

Outside Korea it is one of the easier Korean snacks to find. It is stocked by Korean grocers, and Banana Kick and the strawberry version have appeared in mainstream US retail as well as the usual online routes.

One practical note for travellers: it is 75 g of mostly air in a large bag, so it is a bad use of suitcase volume and an excellent use of the gap around your shoes. It also crushes. If you want it to arrive intact, it goes in the middle of soft clothing or not at all.

## Who Should Skip It

Honest version, because the bag is cheap but your suitcase is not.

**Skip it if you want fruit.** At 1.6% banana powder, this is a sweet corn puff with a banana idea attached. If you want actual Korean fruit flavour, the pear and citrus drinks aisle is a better use of your money.

**Skip it if you buy snacks by weight.** A 75 g bag is enormous and nearly weightless. Measured per gram it is poor value next to a potato chip, and if that arithmetic bothers you it will bother you here.

**Skip the chocolate variant** unless you are curious for its own sake. Koreans were not kind about it.

**Do not skip it** if you have ever wondered what Korean adults mean when they call a snack *추억의 과자* — a snack of memory. This is the clearest example on the shelf, and it costs about a dollar.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is Banana Kick actually made from bananas?**
**A:** Barely. The original contains **1.6% banana powder**; the base is puffed corn. Some variants go as low as 0.2%. It is designed to taste like banana candy, not like fruit.

**Q: Why is Banana Kick suitable for babies and older people?**
**A:** The puffs are **hollow and porous** and dissolve rather than needing to be chewed. Korean sources describe the intended eaters as children, people with weak teeth and denture wearers. That texture is why the viral clip of a twelve-month-old eating it did not alarm anyone in Korea.

**Q: What is the Banana Kick Baby packaging and can I buy it?**
**A:** **No.** Nongshim made a single custom set in August 2026 for the child in the 25.8-million-view video, printed with her face and the phrase 바나나킥 베이비. Korean press reported there are no plans to sell it.

**Q: How much does Banana Kick cost in Korea?**
**A:** Around **₩1,360–1,390** for a 75 g bag through Korean online grocers as of August 2026. Convenience stores charge more but frequently run 1+1 or 2+1 deals on it.

**Q: What are Melon Kick and Mango Kick?**
**A:** The same puffed-corn base in different fruit flavours. **메론킥 launched April 2025** and **망고킥 in May 2026**, so both are recent and may not have reached export markets yet.

**Q: Did BLACKPINK's Jennie make Banana Kick famous?**
**A:** Outside Korea, largely yes — she named it on a US talk show in March 2025 and Korean sources put the resulting rise in Nongshim's market value at about ₩264 billion. Inside Korea it had already been a staple for 47 years.
