**Yakgwa (약과)** is a Korean honey cookie: wheat dough kneaded with honey and sesame oil, pressed in a wooden mould, deep-fried, then soaked in syrup.

Two things about it are worth knowing before anything else.

**It was banned.** Twice, by royal order — in 1192 and again in 1353 — because making it consumed so much grain, honey and oil that prices rose and ordinary people suffered. In Joseon Korea, using it outside three permitted occasions was punishable by **60 strokes of the cane**.

**And its name is now the Korean phrase for "that's nothing."** When a Korean says **"이 정도면 약과다"**, they mean it could be much worse. A word for one of the most expensive things you could eat became the word for trivial.

![A stack of traditional flower-shaped yakgwa cookies.](/assets/images/posts/128/128_01.jpg)

Today it is back, and the most famous maker sells **one box per person, three afternoons a week.** Koreans call trying to get it **약케팅** — yakgwa plus ticketing.

## The Cookie That Was Too Expensive to Allow

Yakgwa belongs to a category called **유밀과** — literally oil-honey confection. The method has barely changed: wheat flour kneaded with honey and sesame oil, pressed into a carved wooden **약과판** mould, fried in vegetable oil, then steeped in syrup.

Read that ingredient list against a pre-modern economy. Wheat flour, honey and cooking oil were all costly, and yakgwa uses them lavishly. It was not a treat that scaled.

The Korean historical record is blunt about the consequence. The *Goryeosa* legal prohibitions record that in **1192** an order went out to use tree fruits and nuts **instead of** 유밀과. In **1353** the ban was issued again. The stated reason both times was that consumption of grain, honey and oil was driving prices up and hurting people's livelihoods.

Joseon kept restricting it. Using 유밀과 outside of **헌수** (a birthday toast to elders), **혼인** (weddings) and **제향** (ancestral rites) was punishable by **장 60대** — 60 strokes of the cane.

So the vague thing English writing says about yakgwa — that it "carries a memory of a time when sweetness felt precious" — is an understatement of a documented fact. This was a dessert the state legislated against.

## Why "Yakgwa" Came to Mean "No Big Deal"

Here is the turn that makes the whole story worth telling.

Because yakgwa was so valuable, it circulated as a **bribe**. Then bribery in Korea escalated — wild ginseng, deer antler velvet, things far costlier than a box of cookies. Faced with those, people started waving yakgwa off: *이건 약과네.* This one's just yakgwa.

The dismissal stuck and outlived the context. **"이 정도면 약과다"** is now ordinary Korean for "this is nothing," "it could be worse," roughly what English does with *a piece of cake*.

Which means the language contains a fossil. Modern Korean uses the name of a luxury item to say something is trivial, because the luxury item was once out-bribed.

You will see this pun used commercially. An ice cream launched during the boom is called **이정도는약과지** — "this much is just yakgwa" — and reportedly grew sales about elevenfold in a month.

## 약케팅: How Buying a Cookie Became a Sport

The revival rides the **할매니얼** trend — 할매 (grandmother) plus millennial — the same wave that brought back yanggaeng, mugwort desserts, grain drinks and rice cakes.

But yakgwa did something the others did not: it became genuinely hard to buy. Koreans coined **약케팅**, yakgwa + ticketing, for the scramble.

The shop that produced the word is **장인약과 (Jangin Yakgwa)** in **Uijeongbu, Gyeonggi Province**. Its signature is not the pretty version — it is **파지약과**, the broken pieces, sold cheaper because they did not survive intact. The shop bills it as the best-selling yakgwa in the country.

**How it actually sells:** through the website, **Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays at 5pm KST**, limited to **one box per person.**

Two other makers worth knowing, both regional and both doing something specific:

- **버들골수제약과** in **Chuncheon, Gangwon** — a pastry-style yakgwa, less sugar and more **조청** (grain syrup). Thin, crisp, and notably not sticky, which makes it the easier one to eat with an iced americano.
- **타래약과** in **Damyang, Jeollanam-do** — hand-twisted rather than moulded, made with Korean wheat and domestic rice syrup, seasoned with ginger and cinnamon.

The mass-market end moved too. **CU reported yakgwa product sales up 158.6% year on year** in a January during the boom, which is what turned a heritage sweet into a convenience-store category.

## What Yakgwa Actually Tastes Like

Yakgwa is sweet, but the better versions are not only sweet. You may notice honey, grain syrup, toasted sesame oil, ginger, a cinnamon-like warmth, and a faint fried-dough richness.

Texture is the real divider. Think syrup-soaked pastry bite, not crunchy biscuit:

- firm on the outside;
- moist or fudgy inside;
- fragrant from sesame oil or spices;
- sticky enough to slow you down;
- rich enough that one small piece makes sense.

This is why it pairs with bitter or clean drinks — unsweetened tea, barley tea, iced americano, black coffee. Put it next to a sweet drink and the whole thing turns heavy.

**개성약과**, the Kaesong style, is the one to look for if you dislike density. Repeated folding and pressing gives it a layered, pastry-like structure closer to a flaky biscuit than a syrup brick.

## Traditional vs Modern

Not every yakgwa is doing the same job. Some are for ritual and holiday trays, some are cafe desserts, some are convenience-store snacks.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Type | Where you see it | Texture | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional 약과 | Markets, hangwa shops, holiday trays | Dense, syrupy, fragrant | Tea, gifts, cultural tasting |
| 개성약과 | Specialty makers, premium boxes | Layered, flaky | People who find standard yakgwa too dense |
| Mini yakgwa | Snack aisles, souvenir packs | Small, easy to share | Travel snacks, office gifts |
| 파지약과 (broken) | Direct from makers online | Same taste, irregular shape | Eating it yourself, cheaper per gram |
| Yakgwa cookie / ice cream | Cafes, convenience stores, collabs | Cookie or cream base plus sticky topping | Trend tasting |

</div>

If you are new to it, start with a small traditional piece, then try a modern cafe version. The contrast is the lesson: one shows why it lasted, the other why it came back.

![A modern yakgwa-topped ice cream dessert in a trendy cafe.](/assets/images/posts/128/128_03.jpg)

A tip that circulated with the ice cream trend and genuinely works: **microwave a yakgwa for about ten seconds** before putting it on ice cream. The syrup loosens and the cold cream stops it from setting hard.

## How to Buy Without Regret

Yakgwa is small, but bad buying decisions are easy. A giant cheap pack can taste flat. A beautiful premium box may be too sweet for the recipient. Choose by use case.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Use case | Best choice | What to check | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Souvenir for many people | Individually wrapped mini yakgwa | Expiry date, packaging strength | Loose sticky pieces in a warm suitcase |
| Gift for one person | Premium box or specialty-shop set | Sweetness, allergens, storage | Choosing by box design alone |
| First taste | Small traditional piece plus a cafe version | The texture gap between them | Judging the category from one convenience-store item |
| Eating it yourself | 파지약과 broken pieces | Weight per won | Paying extra for a shape you are about to bite |
| Food content | Modern yakgwa dessert | Whether it actually tastes balanced | Ignoring taste for plating |

</div>

For travel, choose sealed packaging. Yakgwa is sticky and dense, and loose pieces do not belong in a hot suitcase.

## Where to Find It Without the Queue

Traditional markets, department-store food halls, museum shops, convenience stores and Seoul cafes all carry it. **Insadong** is the natural starting point because the neighbourhood already leans traditional. Department stores are better for a clean gift box.

You do not need 약케팅 to eat good yakgwa. The queue is for one specific maker's broken pieces. A hangwa shop in a market will sell you something excellent, immediately, at a normal price.

## Is It Healthy?

No. The **약** in the name means medicine, and older Korean food culture did treat honey and sesame oil as restorative, but this is deep-fried dough soaked in syrup. It is dense, sugary and oily by design.

That is not a reason to avoid it — it is a reason to eat one piece with tea rather than a handful in front of a screen. The traditional serving context, small amounts with unsweetened tea, is also the sensible one.

## Common First-Timer Mistakes

**Expecting a crunchy cookie.** It is a syrup-soaked bite. Judge it as pastry.

**Eating it alone without a drink.** The tea is not decoration; it is what makes the sweetness work.

**Buying the biggest cheapest bag first.** Industrial mini yakgwa is fine as a snack but is not what the fuss is about.

**Assuming the queue means it is the only good one.** 약케팅 is about one shop's supply, not about quality being scarce nationwide.

**Judging the whole category from a convenience-store piece.** That version exists because the trend went mass-market, not because it is the reference point.

## FAQ

**Q: Why was yakgwa banned in Korea?**

**A:** Because it consumed too much of three expensive things at once — grain, honey and oil. Goryeo ordered tree fruits used in place of 유밀과 in **1192**, and banned it again in **1353**, both times citing rising prices and hardship for ordinary people. Joseon restricted it to three occasions — elders' birthday toasts, weddings and ancestral rites — with **60 strokes of the cane** for using it otherwise.

**Q: What does "이 정도면 약과" mean?**

**A:** "This is nothing" or "it could be worse," close to the English *a piece of cake*. The origin is bribery: yakgwa was valuable enough to be given as one, and when costlier bribes like wild ginseng and deer antler velvet became common, people dismissed yakgwa as trivial by comparison. The phrase outlived the practice.

**Q: What is 약케팅?**

**A:** 약과 (yakgwa) + 티케팅 (ticketing) — Korean slang for the scramble to buy yakgwa from a maker that sells out instantly. It was coined around **장인약과** in Uijeongbu, which sells through its website on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays at 5pm KST, one box per person.

**Q: How much does yakgwa cost in Korea?**

**A:** From 장인약과's own shop in August 2026, a 480g bag of 장인 미니약과 is **₩10,900**, a two-pack of 못난이 파지약과 is **₩16,500**, and a full limited box is **₩149,600**. Market and hangwa-shop prices are usually lower; convenience-store mini yakgwa is cheaper still and is a different product in practice.

**Q: What is 파지약과?**

**A:** Broken yakgwa — pieces that cracked in production. Same recipe and taste, irregular shape, lower price. It is the product that made 장인약과 famous, which is unusual: the seconds became the headline item.

**Q: Is yakgwa healthy despite the name?**

**A:** No. 약 means medicine and older Korean food culture treated honey and sesame oil as restorative, but yakgwa is fried dough soaked in syrup. Eat one with unsweetened tea, which is how it was served in the first place.

**Q: What should a first-timer try?**

**A:** A small traditional piece from a market or hangwa shop, then a modern cafe version, so you can feel the gap. If dense and sticky is not your thing, look for **개성약과**, whose repeated folding gives it a layered, flaky structure instead.
