Most foreigners get **Korean fried chicken brands** completely wrong: they think it's all one thing. Walk down almost any street in Seoul and you'll pass three different chicken chains within a block of each other, each one convinced its version is the correct one — and Koreans genuinely have brand loyalties here the way some people defend a favorite pizza chain. If you order blind, you might land on a cheese-powder-coated wing when you actually wanted something closer to Nashville hot, or a delicate soy-garlic piece when you were craving something bolder.

This guide breaks down Korea's "big three" — BBQ, Kyochon, and BHC — what actually makes each one different, plus a fast note on where Goobne fits in, and how to order the way a local would instead of just pointing at the menu. If you're brand-new to the category, EpicKor's [chimaek and ordering basics guide](/blog/018) covers the crunch styles and etiquette this comparison builds on.

## BBQ: The Olive Oil Original

BBQ (technically "Genesis BBQ," not related to the American barbecue concept) launched in 1995 and built its entire identity around a single ingredient decision: frying in 100% olive oil instead of the vegetable or palm oils most competitors use. That choice is still the brand's core pitch nearly three decades later, and it does produce a noticeably lighter, less heavy fried texture than some competitors — the kind of chicken that doesn't sit in your stomach like a brick afterward.

The signature order is **Golden Olive Chicken**, a plain-fried style that lets the olive oil frying speak for itself rather than burying the chicken under sauce. If you want to actually taste what "Korean fried chicken done simply" tastes like — thin, crackling batter, clean chicken flavor — BBQ's original preparation is the most honest baseline of the big three. It's a reasonable starting point if you're not sure what style you like yet, since everything else on this list is a flavor variation on the same fried foundation BBQ built its name on.

## Kyochon: The Soy-Garlic Purist

Kyochon (branded in some markets as "Kyochon 1991," referencing its founding year) built its reputation on precision rather than boldness. Its defining style is a soy-garlic glaze — savory, faintly sweet, with none of the intense heat or sugar-forward glaze some other brands lean on — applied over an unusually thin, crackly-light batter that's closer to a delicate shell than a thick crust.

The two entry-point orders are **Kyochon Original** (soy-garlic) and **Kyochon Honey**, a sweeter variation of the same base. Both are frequently recommended as the safest first Korean fried chicken order for visitors who aren't sure how spicy Korean food generally runs — nothing on Kyochon's core menu is aggressively hot, and the flavor profile reads as more "refined restaurant dish" than "greasy takeout," which is very much the brand's intention. If BBQ is the honest baseline, Kyochon is the polished version of the same fried chicken, dressed up with a sauce technique most competitors don't bother matching.

## BHC: The Cheese Powder Phenomenon

BHC is the brand most likely to surprise a first-time visitor, mostly because of one product: **Bburinkle (뿌링클)**, a fried chicken tossed in a sweet-savory powder blend built around cheese and dried vegetable seasoning. It doesn't map cleanly onto any Western fried chicken flavor — it's not "cheesy" the way ranch-dusted wings are cheesy, and it's not purely sweet either. It reads as something closer to a snack-food seasoning applied to a full chicken order, and it became enough of a cultural phenomenon in Korea that "Bburinkle-flavored" versions of unrelated snack products have followed in its wake for years.

BHC (originally launched as "Bunny's Chicken" before rebranding) tends to sit at a slightly higher price point than Kyochon in most head-to-head comparisons, positioning itself as a premium option. If you're ordering for a group and want one dramatically different flavor on the table alongside a more classic style from BBQ or Kyochon, Bburinkle is the pick that guarantees nobody at the table has tasted anything quite like it before — which, depending on your group, is either the best or the riskiest recommendation in this whole guide.

## Beyond the Big Three: Where Goobne Fits In

If you spend more than a few days in Korea, you'll also run into **Goobne Chicken**, whose storefronts market themselves explicitly as "No.1 Oven Roasted Chicken" — a genuinely different cooking method from the fried big three. Goobne roasts rather than deep-fries, which makes it the go-to recommendation for travelers avoiding fried food or looking for a lighter option without giving up the Korean chicken experience entirely. It's not usually grouped with BBQ, Kyochon, and BHC in "the big three" conversation, but it's common enough, and different enough, to be worth knowing before you assume every Korean chicken chain fries.

## How to Order Like a Local

A few habits separate a tourist order from a local one. First, **"반반" (ban-ban, "half-half")** is not a special request — it's a standard menu option at nearly every Korean chicken chain, letting you get half plain/original and half of a flavored style in the same box, which is the easiest way to sample two styles without committing to a full order of either. Second, **chimaek (치맥)** — chicken plus maekju (beer) — is less a suggestion than the default pairing; ordering chicken without at least considering a beer alongside it will mark you as an outsider to anyone Korean sharing the table. Third, most orders come with **free pickled radish cubes (무, mu)** to cut the richness between bites — eat them throughout the meal, not just at the end, the way you'd use a palate cleanser.

Delivery is enormous in Korea, and all three big chains have strong app-based ordering, often faster and cheaper than a dine-in wait on a busy weekend night. If you're staying somewhere with delivery access, ordering chicken to your accommodation is a completely normal, non-touristy way to experience the category — arguably more authentic than eating at a storefront, since that's how most Koreans actually eat it themselves.

Sizing is the other thing worth knowing before you order. A single "whole chicken" order (한마리, han-mari) is genuinely built for two to three people once you add sides, not a solo meal — a common first-timer mistake is under-ordering for a group, then wondering why everyone's still hungry after the box arrives. If you're eating alone, most chains also offer smaller boneless or half-chicken options that don't force you into full-size portions just to try a flavor.

## Where to Eat vs. Where to Order

Storefront dine-in and delivery-only "ghost kitchens" both exist for all three big chains, and they're not always the same experience. A sit-down BBQ, Kyochon, or BHC location typically serves the food fresher off the fryer and often has a slightly wider menu than what shows up in the delivery app, including draft beer at chain-branded pubs like Goobne's. Delivery-focused locations, by contrast, are optimized purely for packaging and transit time — the chicken is still good, but it's had a few extra minutes to steam slightly inside its box, which changes the texture just enough that a purist would notice. If crunch matters most to you, dine in; if convenience matters most, delivery is built for exactly that.

## Price and Value: What You're Actually Paying For

Menu pricing across the big three tracks closely enough that price alone rarely decides which chain wins — a whole fried chicken from BBQ, Kyochon, or BHC generally lands in the same ₩20,000–25,000 band as of 2026, before delivery or service fees. What actually varies more is portion framing and add-on pricing: BHC's Bburinkle and other specialty flavors often carry a small premium over BBQ's or Kyochon's plain-fried baseline, since the seasoning and glaze production costs more than a simple fry.

Delivery minimums and fees matter more than the base chicken price for a lot of real-world ordering decisions. Most Korean chicken delivery comes with a delivery fee layered on top of the menu price, plus an app service fee depending on which platform you use, and minimums that sometimes push a solo order toward a combo you didn't originally want. Ordering with a group and splitting one large combo — chicken plus fries plus a drink — is consistently the better value than three people each ordering separately, the same math that applies to delivery food anywhere in the world.

Sides are where the chains actually differentiate on value. Cheese balls, tteokbokki add-ons, and coleslaw are common at all three, but the exact lineup and pricing shift often enough that it's worth checking the current app menu rather than assuming last year's combo pricing still applies.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the most popular Korean fried chicken brand?**
BBQ, Kyochon, and BHC are consistently named as Korea's "big three" fried chicken chains, each with a different signature style — olive oil frying, soy-garlic glaze, and cheese-powder coating respectively. Popularity varies by region and personal taste rather than one clear national favorite.

**Q: What is Bburinkle chicken?**
Bburinkle (뿌링클) is BHC's signature product: fried chicken tossed in a sweet-savory powder seasoning built around cheese and dried vegetables. It has no close Western equivalent and became a genuine pop-culture flavor phenomenon in Korea.

**Q: Is Korean fried chicken always spicy?**
No. Original/plain-fried and soy-garlic styles from all three big brands are not spicy. Spicy yangnyeom (양념) glazed styles exist at every chain for those who want heat, but they're a choice, not the default.

**Q: What does "반반" (ban-ban) mean when ordering chicken?**
Ban-ban means "half-half" — a standard menu option letting you order two different flavors in one box, usually plain/original on one side and a sauced style on the other. It's the easiest way to try two styles in a single order.

**Q: Is Goobne the same as BBQ, Kyochon, or BHC?**
No. Goobne markets itself specifically as oven-roasted rather than fried, making it a different cooking category from the fried "big three," and a common recommendation for travelers who want a lighter, non-fried option.

**Q: Can I order Korean fried chicken delivery as a tourist?**
Yes, if you're staying somewhere with a valid delivery address — Korea's delivery apps are extensive and fast, and ordering chicken for delivery is actually how most Koreans experience these brands day-to-day, not just a dine-in specialty.

## The Bottom Line

There's no single "best" Korean fried chicken brand — there's the honest, olive-oil classic from BBQ, the refined soy-garlic precision of Kyochon, and BHC's genre-bending cheese-powder Bburinkle, with Goobne standing off to the side as the oven-roasted alternative. Try the ban-ban option at whichever chain you find first, grab a beer, and you'll understand within one bite why chimaek isn't just a food category in Korea — it's practically a national pastime.
