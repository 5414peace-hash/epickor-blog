The tornado potato is one of those foods everybody assumes has always existed. A potato cut into one continuous spiral, threaded onto a stick, fried, dusted with flavored powder. You have seen it at a county fair in Texas, at a night market in Bangkok, at a festival in Germany. It reads as generic.

It is not generic. **회오리감자** was designed in Korea in 2006 by a specific person, first sold on a specific street on a specific day, and is protected by patents, designs and trademarks in six jurisdictions. The word 회오리 — "whirlwind" — is a registered trademark, which is why so many Korean vendors selling the identical object write **스프링감자** on their sign instead.

## It Started With a Machine for Peeling Persimmons

The origin is not a chef's idea. It is an engineering problem.

이봉구 had been selling **persimmon-peeling machines**, and the business was going badly. He had also worked as a cook. Those two facts collided into a question: could a machine of that kind cut a potato instead — not into slices, but into one unbroken helix that would stay connected on a skewer?

With help from a machinist friend, he built one. The result was a potato-forming machine that takes a whole potato and produces a continuous spring, which is a harder object to make than it looks: the cut has to be deep enough to open into a spiral under heat, and shallow enough that the potato does not fall apart on the stick.

On **27 May 2007** he opened in **Myeongdong** under the name **봉구네 통감자**. Korean television picked it up in 2008, and after that it stopped being one man's stall.

**The invention is the machine, not the recipe.** That distinction is the whole reason this post is not a recipe post. Anybody can season a fried potato. Almost nobody can cut one into a metre of unbroken spiral in a few seconds, which is why street vendors buy the machine rather than the technique.

## Why Korean Signs Say Two Different Things

Walk a Korean festival strip and you will pass two stalls selling what is visibly the same object, one labelled **회오리감자** and the next labelled **스프링감자** or **나선형감자**.

That is not regional variation. **회오리 is a registered trademark**, and the rights holder recommends exactly those generic alternatives — spring potato, spiral potato — to vendors who are not licensed. So the sign tells you something about the stall's relationship to the patent holder, and nothing whatsoever about the food.

Which is worth knowing for a reason that surprises people: **the inventor has largely not gone after street vendors.** A patent portfolio across six jurisdictions is expensive, and it has mostly been pointed at machine manufacture and franchise licensing rather than at the person running a cart at a school festival. The copies were allowed to spread, and the spread is what made it a national snack.

## It Runs on the Potatoes Nobody Else Wanted

![Bar chart of 회오리 company revenue from 700 million won in 2013 to a projected 12 billion won in 2017, alongside figures for roughly 150 supplying farms and 600 million won purchased from the Okcheon Daecheong agricultural cooperative in 2017.](/assets/images/posts/410/tornado-potato-farm-supply.jpg)
*EpicKor chart. Figures as reported in December 2017.*

This is the part that never makes it into English, and it is the best thing about the product.

A spiral cut does not want a big potato. It wants a **small, regular one** — and small, undersized tubers are precisely the grade that Korean farmers struggled to sell at a decent price into the table-potato market. The company's agricultural arm buys them by the ton: roughly **₩2 billion of potatoes a year from about 150 farms**, including **₩600 million in 2017 from the Daecheong agricultural cooperative in Okcheon alone**, whose potato shipment group had 33 member farms.

As the chairman of that shipment group put it, the buyer does not need large or premium potatoes — it needs a specific smaller size, and that takes pressure off the growers.

The company's own revenue over the same period tracks it: **₩0.7 billion in 2013, ₩2.7 billion in 2014, ₩5.2 billion in 2015, ₩7 billion in 2016**, and a projected **₩12 billion in 2017**.

So the snack that reads as disposable fair food is, in Korea, a small industrial supply chain that turned a grading problem into a product.

## Where to Actually Eat One in Korea

It is a **festival and street food**, not a restaurant food, so the honest answer is seasonal and situational rather than a list of addresses.

**Where it reliably appears:** university-area streets, night markets, seasonal festivals, cherry-blossom and fireworks crowds, amusement parks, and busy tourist strips including Myeongdong where it started. If there is a queue of people eating something on a stick, it is in the mix.

**Where to be careful:** highway rest stops. Korean coverage is blunt about this — a lot of rest-stop spiral potato is imported frozen product rather than cut fresh on site, and it goes soft. The thing you want is the one cut from a raw potato in front of you and fried to order. **If you cannot see a potato going into a machine, lower your expectations.**

**How it is served:** fried, then dusted at the counter from shakers of seasoning powder — cheese, onion, barbecue, honey butter, hot. The seasoning goes on after frying, so you can ask for less. Sausage versions exist, with a frankfurter threaded onto the same skewer under the potato.

**Eat it immediately.** The entire appeal is the contrast between crisp edges and the softer interior of the coil, and that window is a few minutes long. A spiral potato carried around for twenty minutes is a chewy potato.

## The Name Problem, Internationally

The official English brand is **Twist Potato**. Almost nobody uses it.

Japan settled on **トルネードポテト** (tornado potato) and スパイラルポテト (spiral potato). English-speaking fairs settled on tornado potato. It became genuinely popular as street food in India and Bangladesh. German and American festival vendors sell it with no reference to Korea at all.

That is the ordinary fate of a well-designed object: it travels faster than its story. But it does mean the search you would naturally type — "tornado potato" — leads to listicles and recipes and never once to 명동 in 2007.

## Honest Notes

**It is a fried potato.** Nothing here makes it healthy food, and the seasoning powder is where most of the salt lives. Ask for a light dusting if that matters to you.

**Frozen and vacuum-packed versions exist and are not the same experience.** The company itself developed vacuum and frozen packing for distribution, and it works well enough for logistics, but a pre-formed potato reheated at a rest stop is a different object from one cut to order. Korean commentary singles this out.

**What it costs, and the Myeongdong precedent.** In July 2023 Seoul's Jung-gu district
office rolled out a 판매가격표시제 — a mandatory price-display rule — on the Myeongdong street-food
stalls after a run of overcharging complaints. 회오리감자 came down from **₩5,000 to ₩4,000**, the
same cut applied to 군만두 and 핫바. That ₩4,000 Myeongdong figure is the most reliable public
number attached to this snack, and it is still the top of the market: a stall on a university
street or at a smaller local festival typically sits below it. Confirm at the stall — and note
that the same coverage recorded vendors taking **cash only**, which is unusual in Korea and still
worth carrying a few notes for.

**Away from Myeongdong there is no standard price.** This is street and festival food sold by independent vendors; a seasonal festival stall and a permanent shop in a student district will not charge the same, and festival pricing runs high. Look before you queue.

**Do not assume the stall is licensed.** Most are not, and it does not matter to your snack. The sign is a legal artifact, not a quality signal.

**The Korean-invention claim is about this specific product, not about spiral-cut food in general.** Cutting food into a helix is not new. What was invented in 2006 is a machine and a format — one continuous potato spring on a stick, fried and seasoned — and it is that package that spread worldwide.

## FAQ

**Q: Is tornado potato Korean?**
**A:** Yes. 회오리감자 was developed in Korea in 2006 by 이봉구 and first sold in Myeongdong, Seoul, on 27 May 2007. It is protected by registrations in Korea, the United States, the EU, China, Japan and the Philippines.

**Q: Why do some Korean signs say 스프링감자 instead?**
**A:** 회오리 is a registered trademark. Unlicensed vendors use generic terms — 스프링감자 or 나선형감자 — for the identical product.

**Q: What is it called in English officially?**
**A:** The brand's own English name is **Twist Potato**. The world calls it tornado potato or spiral potato.

**Q: Where do I find one in Seoul?**
**A:** Festival strips, night markets, university areas, amusement parks and tourist streets including Myeongdong. It is street food, so availability is seasonal and event-driven rather than fixed.

**Q: Is the rest-stop version worth it?**
**A:** Usually not. Korean coverage notes that much rest-stop spiral potato is pre-made frozen product that turns soggy. Buy the one you can watch being cut.
