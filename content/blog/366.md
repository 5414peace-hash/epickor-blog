**포카칩** has been the best-selling potato chip in Korea for over thirty years, and in the first half of last year it was the country's **second best-selling snack of any kind** at ₩54.4 billion — the fastest-growing product in the top ten.

Almost nothing has been written about it in English. You can buy it on Amazon and at every Korean grocer abroad, and what you will find online is a product listing.

So: which bag to pick, how it differs from the ridged one next to it, and the thing about the blue bag that nobody outside Korea knows.

## The Original Is Not the Original

This is the fact that reorders how you read the shelf.

**어니언맛 launched in July 1988.** At the time, potato chips barely existed as a category in Korea, and Orion led with onion — a seasoned chip, not a plain one.

**Original did not arrive until 1992**, and it was not called Original then either. It launched as **소금맛** — *salt flavour*. The English word "Salted" was added to the bag in **1996**, and only around **2006** was it relabelled **오리지널**.

So the product English speakers reach for as the default, assuming it is the base from which the others branched, is the **later** one, renamed twice, four years behind the flavour that actually established the brand.

That is not a trivia point. It changes the recommendation: in Korea, **어니언 is not a variant of Original — Original is the plain version of Onion.** If you are buying one bag to find out what Pocachip is, the green one is the more representative answer.

## The Bag Changes With the Season

Look again at the gold badge on the front: **100% 국산 햇감자**, one hundred percent Korean new potatoes.

It is true — for part of the year.

Korean potato harvest runs roughly **June to October**, and during those months Orion produces Pocachip from domestic new potatoes. Outside that window the domestic crop is gone, and production switches to imported potatoes, chiefly from the **United States and Australia**.

This is not concealed and it is not unusual for the industry, but it has a practical consequence that no English page mentions: **a bag bought in Korea in September and a bag bought in February are not made from the same potatoes.** Korean snack press treats the annual switch to 햇감자 as news, which tells you the difference is considered real.

The chips are also **생감자칩** — cut from whole potatoes rather than pressed from dehydrated flake, which is what separates Pocachip and Swing Chip from stacked chips like Pringles. And they are cut unusually thin, which is the entire textural identity: they shatter rather than crunch.

## Pocachip vs Swing Chip

These two sit next to each other in every Korean shop, they are made by the same company, and choosing between them is the actual decision a shopper faces.

![A bowl of Swing Chip — thick chips with deep parallel ridges.](/assets/images/posts/366/swingchip-chips-plate.jpg)

*스윙칩, the sister product. Same maker, same domestic-potato claim, completely different cut. Photo by 박찹쌀, CC BY-SA 2.0 KR, via Wikimedia Commons.*

<div class="table-scroll">

| | **포카칩** | **스윙칩** |
|---|---|---|
| Cut | **Thin and flat** | **Thick, with wave ridges (물결무늬)** |
| Texture | Shatters. Light, brittle | Substantial. A real crunch |
| Seasoning | Sits on a smooth surface, tastes cleaner | Catches in the ridges, tastes stronger |
| Best for | Eating a lot of, on its own | Dips, and heavily seasoned flavours |
| Signature flavour | Onion | 간장 (soy) and spicy lines |

</div>

The rule of thumb: **if you want to taste potato, buy Pocachip. If you want to taste seasoning, buy Swing Chip.** The ridges exist to hold powder, and Orion puts its more aggressive flavours — carbonara buldak, soy — on the ridged one for exactly that reason.

Together the two brands have passed **₩4 trillion in cumulative global sales**, and Orion's potato-snack business alone did **₩874 billion last year**, closing on a trillion won.

Limited editions rotate constantly — **라임모히또** (lime mojito), **불닭** (buldak), **표고버섯** (shiitake) have all had runs. Treat them the way you would treat any Korean limited edition: **buy it when you see it**, because it may not be there next month.

One honest note on Sweet Cheese. Korean "sweet cheese" seasoning is genuinely sweet, closer to a dessert note than the savoury cheese powder most Western palates expect. It was withdrawn once and came back by demand, so Korea clearly likes it — but it is the flavour most likely to surprise a first-time buyer in the wrong direction.

## It Beat Lay's in Vietnam

The part of the story that surprises Koreans as much as anyone.

Orion took Pocachip into Vietnam and has held **the number one potato chip market share there since 2017** — ahead of **Lay's**, the global leader. Orion runs localised products in Vietnam and China, and is expanding production lines at home and abroad on the back of it.

For context on how unusual that is: this is a Korean snack brand beating PepsiCo's flagship chip in a third country, sustained over most of a decade.

At home its position is less comfortable than it looks. **Nongshim's 포테토칩 has been closing the gap**, and Korean trade press in July 2026 framed the year as a fight over which potato chip takes the No.1 snack slot outright. The 2015 **허니버터칩** mania briefly convulsed the whole category and still did not displace Pocachip — but the current challenge is a direct competitor in the same format, which is different.

## Who Should Skip It

**Skip it if you want something you cannot get at home.** This is a very good thin potato chip, and it is a *potato chip*. Someone hoping for a distinctly Korean flavour experience will get more from [Orion's cheese chip](/blog/343) or from a snack with no Western equivalent at all.

**Skip Original if you only buy one bag.** It is the plainest thing in the range and the least representative. Buy Onion.

**Skip the big 137 g bag unless you are sharing.** Thin chips go soft quickly once opened, and Korean summer humidity is unkind to them. The 66 g is the sensible unit.

## FAQ

**Q: What is Pocachip?**
**A:** Korea's best-selling potato chip, made by **오리온 (Orion)** since 1988. It is a **생감자칩** — sliced from whole potatoes rather than pressed from flakes — cut very thin, so it shatters rather than crunches. The name is **포** from *potato* plus **카**, the Korean onomatopoeia for a crisp snap.

**Q: Which Pocachip flavour should I buy first?**
**A:** **어니언맛 (Onion, green bag).** It launched in 1988, four years before Original, and it is the flavour the brand was built on. Original is the plain salted version, not the base the others came from.

**Q: What is the difference between Pocachip and Swing Chip?**
**A:** Same company, different cut. **Pocachip is thin and flat**; **스윙칩 is thick with wave ridges**. Pocachip tastes more of potato, Swing Chip carries stronger seasoning because the ridges hold more powder.

**Q: Is Pocachip really made from Korean potatoes?**
**A:** During the Korean harvest, roughly **June to October**, yes — that is when the **100% 국산 햇감자** badge applies. Outside those months Orion uses imported potatoes, mainly from the United States and Australia. The same bag is not made from the same potatoes year-round.

**Q: How much does Pocachip cost in Korea?**
**A:** As of August 2026, **₩1,700** for a 66 g bag at convenience stores. Supermarket and online multipacks work out to roughly **₩1,100 per bag**. Convenience stores run 2+1 and 1+1 promotions on it very frequently.

**Q: Is Pocachip like Lay's or Pringles?**
**A:** Closer to Lay's — both are sliced from real potatoes. Pringles is pressed from dehydrated flake, which is a different product entirely. Pocachip is cut thinner than most Lay's, and in Vietnam it has outsold Lay's since 2017.

**Q: Why is it spelled Poca and not Poka?**
**A:** Romanisation is inconsistent on the bags themselves and across retailers, so you will see **Pocachip**, **Poca Chip**, and **Poka Chip** for the same product. The Korean is always **포카칩**.
