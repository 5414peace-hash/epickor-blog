Every Korean ramyun scene uses the same pot. Thin metal, two handles, a lid that becomes the plate. It boils absurdly fast, it costs almost nothing, and it is the reason instant noodles feel like a specific meal in Korea rather than a generic one.

It is also the thing people ask about most once they own one. **Is it safe?**

That question has a better answer in Korean than in English, because Korea has actually measured it — pot by pot, dish by dish, with numbers. ## The name is wrong, and that is the first clue

**양은 (洋銀)** means *Western silver* — nickel silver, an alloy of copper with 15–30% zinc and 10–20% nickel. It is silvery, it was used for tableware and instruments, and it is what these pots were originally made from.

**Modern 양은냄비 contain no 양은 at all.** They are aluminium. The material changed; the name did not.

**The colour gives it away, and it is the fastest way to identify one in a shop.** Nickel silver is silvery, as the name says. The pot Koreans actually picture is **gold** — that yellow tone is an anodised finish on aluminium, not a metal. If you are looking for the classic ramyun pot rather than a generic saucepan, look for the gold body with two matching side handles and a black lid knob.

This matters because almost every English explanation of the pot inherits the old name and then reasons from the wrong metal. Everything below — the boiling speed, the leaching, the care rules, the replacement interval — is a property of **thin aluminium**, not of a nickel alloy.

The surface is usually treated, and the treatment is what varies between a pot that is fine and a pot that is not.

## What Korea measured

![Chart: the Gyeonggi Institute of Health and Environment tested 56 aluminium pots sold in Korea by boiling citric acid at pH 3.5. By dish, kimchi jjigae leached 9.86 mg per kg, pickles 2.86, kimchi ramyun 2.34 and doenjang jjigae 1.64.](/assets/images/posts/438/aluminium-pot-leaching-measured.jpg)
*The study that answers the question, and does not exist in English. Chart by EpicKor.*

The **경기도보건환경연구원 (Gyeonggi-do Institute of Health and Environment)** bought **56 aluminium pots** on the Korean market and boiled a citric acid solution at **pH 3.5** in each one — a deliberately harsh acid test, more aggressive than most cooking.

**The 47 uncoated pots released an average of 23.90 mg/L of aluminium, and the worst single pot released 115.21 mg/L.**

**The nine coated pots released an average of 1.78 mg/L, worst case 8.72.**

That is roughly a **thirteenfold difference**, and it holds on both the average and the extreme. If you take one thing from this article to a shop, take that.

They then cooked real dishes and measured aluminium per kilogram of food:

- **김치찌개 kimchi jjigae — 9.86 mg/kg** (pH 4.6, 0.98% salt)
- **피클 pickles — 2.86 mg/kg** (pH 3.8, 0.38% salt)
- **김치라면 kimchi ramyun — 2.34 mg/kg** (pH 4.4, 0.55% salt)
- **된장찌개 doenjang jjigae — 1.64 mg/kg** (pH 5.7, 1.02% salt)

The ranking follows acidity, with salt amplifying it. It is not about the dish being Korean; it is about pH. The institute's own conclusion was measured rather than alarmed: **not at a level harmful to the body**, but do not use aluminium pots long-term for high-acid, high-salt cooking.

## Is that a lot? Korea publishes the other half of the sum

A leaching figure means nothing on its own. Here is the number to weigh it against.

Korea's **식품의약품안전처 (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety)** states that a **60kg adult can take in about 17 mg of aluminium a day** without health risk — a quantity it compares to a single grain of rice. It adds two facts that most scare-writing omits:

- **Only about 1% of ingested aluminium is absorbed.** The rest leaves in urine.
- **Korean dietary aluminium intake sits at about 19.5% of the WHO safety standard.**

Now do the arithmetic on the worst dish. Kimchi jjigae at **9.86 mg/kg** means a **300g serving carries roughly 3 mg** of aluminium from the pot. Against a 17 mg daily figure, that is about **a sixth of it**, from the single most aggressive dish in the study, cooked in an uncoated pot.

**A bowl of kimchi ramyun works out lighter but not trivially so.** A packet cooked in the standard 550ml of water lands around 600g of noodles and broth, which at 2.34 mg/kg is roughly **1.4 mg** — under a tenth of the daily figure, and that is if you drink all the broth.

So the honest answer to *is it safe* is: **yes, at the amounts measured, for the way people actually cook** — and the way to keep it that way is specific rather than vague. Buy coated. Do not stew acid in it for hours. Do not wreck the surface.

### The Alzheimer's question

This one deserves a direct sentence rather than a shrug, because it is the reason many people ask in the first place.

Korea's food-safety authority states plainly: **"현재, 국제적으로 알루미늄과 알츠하이머병과의 인과 관계가 명확히 밝혀진 바는 없습니다"** — internationally, no causal relationship between aluminium and Alzheimer's disease has been clearly established.

That is an accurate statement of where the evidence sits, and it is the position of the body that regulates this cookware in the country that uses it most. It is not a promise that nothing is ever knowable; it is that the link people worry about has not been shown.

## The surface is the whole product

![Chart in three parts: never use a metal scourer, because one pass with a steel scrubber takes the protective film off, so use a soft sponge and wooden utensils; do not store food in it, because salty acidic stew left sitting corrodes the pot, so transfer to a bowl as soon as cooking finishes; and when to throw it out — visible discolouration or scratches, with Korean guidance putting daily-use replacement at six months to a year.](/assets/images/posts/438/aluminium-pot-care-rules.jpg)
*Every number above assumes the film is intact. Chart by EpicKor.*

All of the reassuring arithmetic rests on one assumption: that the pot still has its protective surface. Korean guidance on this is unusually blunt.

**A steel scrubber removes the film in a single pass**, leaving bare aluminium in contact with your food. That converts a coated pot — the 1.78 mg/L category — into something closer to the uncoated one, and no amount of careful cooking undoes it.

So the rules are short:

1. **Soft sponge only.** Never a metal scourer, never a scouring powder.
2. **Wooden or silicone utensils**, not metal ones scraping the base.
3. **Do not leave stew sitting in it.** Salt and acid corrode the pot between meals, not just during cooking. Transfer to a bowl when you are done — which, conveniently, is how it is served anyway.
4. **Replace it when it discolours or visibly scratches.** Korean guidance puts the interval at roughly **six months to a year** with daily use.

Point 4 is the one Western kitchen instincts resist. These pots cost a few thousand won. They are consumables, not cookware you keep. Treating one as an heirloom is exactly how you end up cooking in a scratched pot for five years.

## Why it cooks ramyun better anyway

None of the above is an argument against the pot. It exists because it does one thing extremely well.

**Thin aluminium boils fast and loses heat fast.** That is the entire functional argument. Ramyun is a dish whose identity is speed — three minutes, no ceremony — and a thin pot reaches a rolling boil far quicker than a heavy one, then cools quickly enough that you can eat straight out of it. The lid becomes the plate for the noodles you lift out first.

This is also why the pot is visual shorthand in Korean film and television, a point our guide to [what Korean dramas are actually showing you at the dinner table](/blog/397) gets into: eating from the lid is the least-pretentious way to feed someone, so directors reach for it.

The physics that make it good are the same physics that make it thin, and thin is why the surface matters so much. Those are not two separate facts about the pot. They are one fact.

## Coated, uncoated, or stainless

Three real options, and the choice is not close once you know the numbers.

- **Coated aluminium** — the default recommendation. Keeps the fast boil, cuts migration by roughly thirteen times, and costs a few thousand won more. The trade-off is that the coating is destructible, which returns you to the care rules above.
- **Uncoated aluminium** — fine for water and plain noodles, which is genuinely most of its use. Not the pot for simmering kimchi jjigae for an hour.
- **Stainless steel** — no aluminium question at all, and no fast boil either. Heavier, slower, and it will not behave like the pot you saw on screen. It is a legitimate choice if the whole issue bothers you, and it is what many Korean households now use for actual stews while keeping the aluminium pot for noodles.

That last split is worth copying. In practice a lot of Korean kitchens keep both and use each for what it is good at — the aluminium pot for the three-minute job, a heavier pot or a [ttukbaegi](/blog/433) for anything that simmers.

## FAQ About Korean Ramen Pots

**Q: Is a Korean aluminium ramen pot safe to use?**

At the amounts Korea measured, yes. Uncoated pots released an average of 23.90 mg/L of aluminium under a harsh pH 3.5 acid test and coated ones 1.78 mg/L, while Korea's food-safety authority puts the daily figure a 60kg adult can take in at about 17 mg, of which roughly 1% is actually absorbed. Buy coated, avoid long acidic simmering, and keep the surface intact.

**Q: Which food pulls the most aluminium out of the pot?**

Kimchi jjigae, at 9.86 mg per kilogram of food, well ahead of pickles at 2.86, kimchi ramyun at 2.34 and doenjang jjigae at 1.64. The ranking follows acidity, amplified by salt.

**Q: Does aluminium cookware cause Alzheimer's disease?**

Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety states that no causal relationship between aluminium and Alzheimer's has been clearly established internationally. That is the position of the regulator in the country where these pots are most used.

**Q: Why is it called 양은냄비 if it is aluminium?**

Because it used to be nickel silver — 양은 literally means "Western silver". The metal was replaced with aluminium and the name stayed. Most English explanations inherit the old name and then describe the wrong material.

**Q: How long should a Korean ramen pot last?**

Korean guidance suggests replacing it at roughly six months to a year of daily use, and immediately if it discolours or you can see scratches. They are cheap on purpose; the surface is the part you are actually buying.

## Bottom Line

The pot is safe in the way most kitchen things are safe: within a range, under conditions, and not if you destroy it.

Korea did the work of establishing the range. **Uncoated 23.90 mg/L, coated 1.78, kimchi jjigae the worst dish at 9.86 mg/kg, against a daily figure of about 17 mg of which 1% is absorbed.** Those numbers say the answer is yes, and they also say exactly where the margin comes from.

**Buy the coated one.** Use a soft sponge. Do not leave stew in it overnight, and throw it out when it looks worn rather than when it fails. Then use it for what it is genuinely better at than any expensive pot you own: getting water to a rolling boil in about two minutes and putting dinner in front of you before you have decided what to watch.
