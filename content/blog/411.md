Korean custard cake — **카스타드** — is a soft yellow sponge cake with a pale custard cream injected into the middle, sold in a cardboard box of individually wrapped cakes, and it is in every Korean supermarket, convenience store and airport. It is also the single most confusing thing on the Korean snack shelf, because **two different companies sell it under the same name, in nearly the same box.**

There is a right answer to which one is 원조 — the original — and it is the reverse of what most people assume.

## The Copy Is the One That Advertises the Eggs

![The Orion Custard pack, a green and yellow box printed 오리온 카스타드 with a cut-away sponge cake.](/assets/images/posts/411/orion-custard-pack.jpg)
*오리온 카스타드. Lotte launched a custard cake in 1989 and Orion followed in 2004 — this is the later box, and the argument in this article is about what each one claims about its eggs. Image: Orion product listing.*

![Comparison of 롯데 카스타드 and 오리온 카스타드: Lotte launched in Korea November 1989 after Japan September 1986 with 24% egg content, Orion launched 2004 with a 계란을 듬뿍 package claim and 23.39% egg content, plus a panel noting Custard is the mirror image of the Choco Pie origin dispute.](/assets/images/posts/411/custard-lotte-vs-orion.jpg)
*EpicKor chart.*

**Lotte Custard came first.** Japan's Lotte launched it in **September 1986**; the Korean product followed in **November 1989**. Orion's version arrived in **2004** — fifteen years later.

And here is the detail that makes this worth writing down. Orion's packaging leans on the phrase **계란을 듬뿍** — "generously filled with egg." It is the product's stated selling point.

**Orion's egg content is 23.39%. Lotte's is 24%.**

The one that advertises the eggs has slightly fewer of them. It is a small gap and it will not decide your snack, but it is a nearly perfect illustration of how packaging claims work: the phrase is doing competitive positioning, not reporting a fact about the competitor.

## Why This Confuses Even Koreans

Because the other famous case runs the other way.

**초코파이는 오리온이 원조인데 롯데가 베꼈고, 카스타드는 그 반대다** — Orion invented Choco Pie in 1974 and Lotte copied it; Custard runs backwards. Two of the most recognisable snacks in Korea, and each company holds one original and one imitation.

So a Korean who confidently knows the Choco Pie story will often get Custard wrong by applying the same pattern. If you have ever been told "Orion makes the real one," that is where it comes from.

## The Holes Are Not a Defect

Turn a custard cake over and you will find small holes in the base. People assume they are a fault, or air pockets, or that the cake has been damaged in the box.

They are **injection points**. The cream is not baked in; it is pumped into a finished sponge through the underside, and the holes are where the nozzles went. A custard cake with no holes would mean the cream never got in.

This is the single most-asked question about the product in Korean, and it has never been answered anywhere in English.

## The 2021 Price Rise, and the Part That Was Not on a Label

![Chart of the September 2021 Lotte Confectionery price rise: the Custard six-pack went from 3,000 won to 3,500 won, while the large box went from 12 pieces to 10 pieces at an unchanged price.](/assets/images/posts/411/custard-2021-price-and-count.jpg)
*EpicKor chart.*

On **1 September 2021**, Lotte Confectionery raised prices across its snack range by an average of **12.2% per unit weight**. Custard was one of the named products: the six-pack went from **₩3,000 to ₩3,500**.

That was the visible half. The other half was that the **large box went from 12 pieces to 10, and the price did not change.**

Both are price increases. Only one of them appears on a shelf label, and it is the reason a Korean shopper who remembers this box as good value is remembering a box with two more cakes in it. The company's stated cause was sustained rises in oils, **egg liquid**, sugar and packaging — which, for a cake whose entire selling point is egg content, means the input that rose is the input the product is named after.

Korean listings today show the range at roughly ₩3,000–4,000 depending on box size and retailer. Convenience-store singles cost proportionally more, as always.

## Which One to Actually Buy

**On the numbers, Lotte.** It is the original, it has been in continuous production since 1989, and it has marginally more egg.

**On taste, Korean opinion genuinely splits.** The two are close enough that side-by-side comparisons are a recurring Korean internet pastime with no settled winner. Orion's is often described as slightly softer and sweeter; Lotte's as a touch more eggy. If you strongly prefer one, buy that one — the origin question is trivia, not a quality ruling.

**On availability abroad**, take whichever your Korean grocery stocks. They will not both be there.

## Where You Will Actually Meet It

카스타드 is not a snack Koreans go out to buy. It is a snack that arrives.

**It is a fixture of the Korean gift box.** Confectionery assortments given at Chuseok and Seollal, hospital-visit boxes, and the 종합선물세트 handed to children almost always contain custard cakes, because the box is bulky, looks generous and survives being carried around.

**It is standard 간식 provisioning.** Company pantries, church halls, study rooms, army care packages, and the snack drawer at a 학원 all run on it. If you spend a month in Korea in any institutional setting, someone will hand you one.

**Convenience stores carry the small box; supermarkets carry the large.** For a traveller the practical note is that the six-pack fits in a bag and the large box does not, and neither survives being crushed — this is a soft cake in a soft box, and it will arrive home flattened if you pack it under anything.

**It travels well as a gift, badly as a souvenir.** It is genuinely well liked, it is cheap, and it says nothing at all about Korea to somebody who has never been. Buy it because you want to eat it.

## Honest Notes

**Orion's version was recalled once, in 2024.** On **3 January 2024** Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety ordered a sale halt and recall of Orion Custard after **Staphylococcus aureus** was detected in a batch manufactured on **22 December 2023** at Orion's fourth Cheongju plant, with a use-by date of 21 June 2024. Roughly **4,700 units** were subject to recall.

That is one batch, caught by routine regulatory sampling and pulled — which is the system working rather than failing. But it happened, it is a matter of public record, and English-language coverage of this product does not mention it at all. Both products are on shelves normally today.

**The nitrogen complaint was real and was addressed.** Custard boxes were long mocked in Korea for how much of the packet was gas rather than cake — 질소를 샀더니 과자를 덤으로 줬다, "I bought nitrogen and they threw in a snack." Lotte made quality and packaging changes around 2020, alongside redesigns in 2010, 2020 and 2022. The box is still mostly air by volume, because a soft sponge cake needs it.

**It is a sweet, soft, mild snack, and that is the whole proposition.** There is no textural surprise, no heat, no fermented note. If you are shopping for something distinctively Korean to bring home, this is not that — it is the comfort item Koreans grew up with, which is a different and less exportable kind of appeal.

**Egg allergy: this is an egg product**, not an egg-flavoured one, at roughly a quarter of the cake by content in both brands.

**Do not judge by the box design.** Both companies have redesigned repeatedly, so the packaging you remember from a photograph may not be the packaging on the shelf, and a box that looks "older" is not the original brand.

## FAQ

**Q: Which Korean custard cake is the original?**
**A:** Lotte. 롯데 카스타드 launched in Korea in November 1989, following Japan's Lotte in September 1986. Orion's version arrived in 2004.

**Q: Is Orion's the one with more egg?**
**A:** No, despite the 계란을 듬뿍 claim on the box. Orion's egg content is 23.39%; Lotte's is 24%.

**Q: Why does the cake have holes in the bottom?**
**A:** They are injection points. The custard cream is pumped into a finished sponge from underneath, so the holes are a necessary part of manufacture, not damage.

**Q: How much does it cost in Korea?**
**A:** Around ₩3,500 for a six-pack since the September 2021 rise, with larger boxes roughly ₩3,000–4,000 depending on size and retailer as of August 2026.

**Q: Wasn't there a recall?**
**A:** Yes — Orion's version, on 3 January 2024, for Staphylococcus aureus in one batch made on 22 December 2023. About 4,700 units. Both brands are sold normally today.
