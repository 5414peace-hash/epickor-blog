The first thing to know about **Yeonnam-dong (연남동)** is that its famous park is a railway.

Not "used to be near a railway" — **is one**. Walk the path and you are walking on the old **Yongsan Line**, and the sleepers are still set into the pavement on both sides, deliberately left there. Seoul buried the line underground in the 2000s, the surface track sat abandoned, and an urban regeneration project turned the empty corridor into a park.

Koreans nicknamed the Yeonnam stretch **연트럴파크 (*Yeontral Park*)** — Yeonnam plus Central Park — because it became the longest and busiest section, with the same function a big city park has: people simply go there and sit.

**And that is the whole appeal.** Yeonnam-dong is ten minutes from Hongdae and feels nothing like it, because the thing at its centre is a long green strip rather than a nightlife street.

## How a Railway Became a Park

The sequence is worth knowing because it explains the neighbourhood's shape.

The **Yongsan Line** ran at street level through this part of western Seoul. In the 2000s the Yongsan–Gajwa section was **moved underground**, and what remained on the surface was a long, narrow, abandoned strip of railway land running through dense neighbourhoods.

Seoul turned it into a linear park. Construction ran in stages — **the Daeheung-dong section opened first in March 2012**, and the full route including Yeonhui-dong, Saechang-gogae, **the Yeonnam-dong section**, Wonhyo-ro, Sinsu-dong and Waugyo was **completed in 2016**.

The result is a park with an unusual geometry: **very long, quite narrow, and threaded directly between buildings**. You are never far from a shopfront, which is exactly why cafes and small shops colonised both edges of it.

**That geometry is the neighbourhood's engine.** A conventional park has an inside and an outside. This one is all edge.

## The Part Almost No English Guide Mentions

Before the park, Yeonnam-dong was known for something else entirely: **it is one of Seoul's historic Chinese-community neighbourhoods.**

A Chinese school operated in the area, and around it clustered the community it served. Along the railway, small unglamorous restaurants opened selling **Chinese home cooking and dumplings** — food aimed at residents rather than visitors — alongside **기사식당**, the cheap driver-canteen restaurants that cluster wherever working traffic passes.

That history is still edible. The Chinese food in Yeonnam-dong is not a trend import; **it predates the park by decades**, and some of it is closer to Chinese home cooking than to the Koreanised Chinese food most visitors meet elsewhere.

**Practical consequence:** if you only eat at the photogenic cafes fronting the park, you are eating the newest layer of the neighbourhood and missing the oldest one. Walk a block or two off the park.

## Why It Became Fashionable

![Low-rise shopfronts and small cafes on a Yeonnam-dong street.](/assets/images/posts/351/yeonnam-dong-shopfronts.jpg)

*Low buildings, small independent shopfronts. This scale is the difference from Hongdae. Photo by azer0, CC BY 2.0 KR.*

The transformation is recent and the mechanism is unremarkable, which is the interesting part.

Yeonnam-dong sits **beside Hongdae but not in it**. It was quieter, more residential, and **rents were relatively cheap**. Small workshops, independent cafes and one-person shops moved in for exactly that reason — the same drift that has reshaped fringe neighbourhoods in every large city.

Then the park opened, gave the district a spine and a reason to walk, and the drift accelerated.

**What survived is the scale.** The buildings are low, the shops are small, and almost nothing is a chain. That is why it reads as calm despite being busy.

## What to Actually Do Here

The honest answer is: not much, deliberately.

**Walk the park end to end in the Yeonnam section.** It takes far less time than you expect and it is the point of the visit.

**Sit on the grass.** This is a genuine local behaviour, not a tourist instruction. People bring coffee, food and blankets and stay for hours. Convenience stores nearby exist largely to supply this.

![Buskers performing beside the park in Yeonnam.](/assets/images/posts/351/yeonnam-busking.jpg)

*Busking beside the park. Sitting and watching is the main activity here. Photo by Explicit, CC BY-SA 4.0.*

**Pick a cafe by looking, not by list.** The density is high enough that walking until something appeals works better than navigating to a specific name — and small places here open and close often.

**Eat Chinese food at least once**, for the reason above.

**Then walk into Hongdae if you want the evening.** They are adjacent. The natural shape of a day is Yeonnam in daylight, Hongdae after dark.

## How to Walk It Without Getting It Wrong

The park is 6km end to end across all its sections, and visitors regularly make one of two mistakes: walking too much of it, or not finding it at all.

**You want the Yeonnam section, not the whole thing.** The full Gyeongui Line Forest Park runs through several districts, most of which are ordinary residential neighbourhoods with a pleasant green path. The **Yeonnam-dong stretch nearest Hongik University Station** is the part with the cafes, the crowds and the atmosphere. Walking further out is a nice walk and not what you came for.

**The park is easy to miss from the station.** Exit 3 puts you on an ordinary street; the park is a short walk and reads as a gap between buildings rather than an entrance. Head away from the main Hongdae drag and look for the tree line.

**Walk one direction, then cross and walk back.** The two sides of the strip have different shops, and the park is narrow enough that you can see both but not shop both. Doing one edge out and the other edge back covers it properly.

**Budget less time than you think for the park and more for the streets.** The green strip itself is a twenty-minute walk. The blocks on either side are where the afternoon actually goes.

## What It Costs

Yeonnam-dong is a cheap neighbourhood to spend an afternoon in, which is part of why people linger.

**The cheapest good version of this neighbourhood costs almost nothing.** Buy coffee and something to eat at a convenience store, sit on the grass, and watch. That is what a large share of the people around you are doing, and it is not a downgrade from the cafe version.

**The honest counter-recommendation: do not queue.** Some Yeonnam cafes run long waits at weekends. The neighbourhood's density means there is almost always an equally good place two minutes away with no line, and the queue is rarely buying you a better afternoon.

## When to Skip It

- **Skip it at night.** The park is unlit theatre after dark and the shops close early by Seoul standards. Go to Hongdae instead
- **Skip it in deep winter or heavy rain.** The entire attraction is an outdoor strip. There is no indoor version of this neighbourhood
- **Skip it if you want landmarks.** There is no monument, no tower, no museum. If your trip is built around sights, this is an afternoon you could spend elsewhere
- **Skip weekends if you dislike crowds.** The grass fills, and the narrow park concentrates people rather than dispersing them
- **Do not build the visit around one viral cafe.** Small independents here turn over quickly, and the neighbourhood is the attraction rather than any single address

## FAQ About Yeonnam-dong

**Q: What is Yeontral Park?**

**A:** A nickname for the Yeonnam-dong section of Gyeongui Line Forest Park, combining Yeonnam with New York's Central Park. It stuck because the Yeonnam stretch is the longest and busiest part, used the way a large city park is used.

**Q: Was it really a railway?**

**A:** Yes. The Yongsan Line ran at surface level here until the Yongsan–Gajwa section was moved underground in the 2000s. The abandoned surface corridor became the park, and the original sleepers were left set into the paving.

**Q: When did the park open?**

**A:** In stages. The Daeheung-dong section opened in March 2012, and the full route — including the Yeonnam-dong section, Yeonhui-dong, Saechang-gogae, Wonhyo-ro, Sinsu-dong and Waugyo — was completed in 2016.

**Q: How do I get to Yeonnam-dong?**

**A:** Hongik University Station on Line 2, the AREX airport line, or the Gyeongui-Jungang line. Take Exit 3 and walk a short distance to the park.

**Q: How is it different from Hongdae?**

**A:** Hongdae is loud, late and chain-heavy; Yeonnam is low-rise, quiet and centred on a park. They are adjacent, so the practical answer is to do Yeonnam in the afternoon and Hongdae in the evening.

**Q: Is there anything to eat besides cafes?**

**A:** Yes, and it is the older layer of the neighbourhood. Yeonnam-dong is one of Seoul's historic Chinese-community areas, with Chinese home-style restaurants and dumpling shops that predate the park by decades. Walk a block off the park to find them.

## The One Thing to Remember

**The park is the railway, and the neighbourhood grew along its edge.**

That single fact explains the shape of Yeonnam-dong — why it is linear rather than clustered, why the cafes face a strip of grass, why the whole district feels like one long walk rather than a destination you arrive at.

Go on an afternoon, walk the length of it, sit down somewhere, and eat Chinese food a block off the park.
