Choi Min-sik is one of the easiest Korean actors to recognize and one of the hardest to summarize. International audiences often meet him through *Oldboy*, where his performance feels like a wound walking through the frame. Korean audiences also know him through historical epics, crime films, prestige dramas, box-office records, and the 2024 occult hit *Exhuma*. He is not just a "famous actor." He is one of the faces people use when they talk about the force of Korean cinema.

What makes Choi different is not only intensity. Many actors can shout, suffer, or look severe. Choi's special skill is making a character feel inhabited from the inside: pride, shame, hunger, grief, vulgar humor, exhaustion, national memory, and moral contradiction can all appear in the same performance. That is why he can play a revenge figure in *Oldboy*, Admiral Yi Sun-sin in *The Admiral: Roaring Currents*, and a weary feng shui master in *Exhuma* without feeling like the same role repeated.

This guide explains Choi Min-sik's career for international viewers: why *Oldboy* mattered, why *Roaring Currents* became a Korean box-office landmark, why *Exhuma* proved his relevance to a new generation, and how to watch his films without reducing Korean cinema to violence or prestige cliches.

## Quick Answer: Why Is Choi Min-sik So Important?

Choi Min-sik is important because he connects several eras of Korean screen culture. He is globally associated with Park Chan-wook's *Oldboy*, one of the films that helped define the international reputation of modern Korean cinema. He also anchored domestic box-office history through *The Admiral: Roaring Currents*, a film widely known for its extraordinary Korean admissions record. In 2024, *Exhuma* brought him back to the center of a mass-audience phenomenon, crossing the 10-million-admissions milestone in South Korea.

His importance is also artistic. Choi's performances often carry **han**, a Korean emotional term commonly used for deep unresolved grief, resentment, and endurance, but he does not only play sadness. He can be funny, crude, warm, terrifying, patriotic, pathetic, and spiritually tired. That range lets his characters feel human even when the film around them is extreme.

For new viewers, the best way to understand him is to watch three lanes: *Oldboy* for international auteur cinema, *The Admiral: Roaring Currents* for national-history spectacle, and *Exhuma* for contemporary Korean genre cinema. Those three films do not cover everything, but they show why Choi can move between art-house legend, domestic blockbuster symbol, and modern horror-mystery anchor.

## The *Oldboy* Legacy: More Than the Hammer Scene

For many global viewers, Choi Min-sik begins with *Oldboy*. Park Chan-wook's 2003 film became an international reference point for Korean cinema: stylish, brutal, emotionally twisted, and formally confident. Choi's Oh Dae-su is not a clean hero. He is wounded, foolish, frightening, funny, and increasingly destroyed by forces he does not understand.

It is tempting to reduce *Oldboy* to the hallway fight, the live-octopus scene, or the final twist. Those moments are famous for a reason, but Choi's performance is the glue. He makes the film feel like more than a revenge machine. His body carries years of confinement. His face swings between animal hunger and childlike confusion. Even when the plot becomes operatic, he keeps the pain physical.

The live-octopus scene especially became part of Choi's legend because viewers understood it as evidence of commitment. But the deeper lesson is not shock value. It is that Korean cinema of that era often forced emotion through the body: eating, bleeding, running, screaming, kneeling, fighting, vomiting, and enduring. Choi was uniquely suited to that style because he could make excess feel grounded.

## Why Korean Audiences See More Than One Choi

International audiences sometimes freeze Choi inside the *Oldboy* image. Korean audiences are more likely to see a wider career. They know the theater-trained seriousness, the crime-film gravitas, the historical authority, the public familiarity, and the late-career willingness to re-enter popular genre work.

This matters because Korean film culture often values actors who can carry emotional national texture. A performer may be praised not only for technical acting but for having a face that seems to hold time: dictatorship memories, IMF-era pressure, family duty, class resentment, war memory, or generational fatigue. Choi's face is useful to Korean cinema because it can make personal pain feel historical.

At the same time, he is not only a solemn prestige figure. Part of his strength is that he can be ordinary, vulgar, and socially recognizable. He can look like a tired uncle, a dangerous gangster, a failed man, a national hero, or a specialist with too many ghosts behind his eyes. That flexibility is why directors can build very different films around him.

## *The Admiral: Roaring Currents* and the Weight of History

If *Oldboy* made Choi globally iconic, *The Admiral: Roaring Currents* made him central to one of Korea's biggest domestic movie memories. The film dramatizes Admiral Yi Sun-sin, one of Korea's most revered historical figures, and the Battle of Myeongnyang. For Korean viewers, Admiral Yi is not just a naval commander; he represents impossible resistance, strategic brilliance, and national survival against overwhelming odds.

Playing Admiral Yi is therefore not a normal historical role. The actor must carry reverence without becoming a statue. Choi's gravity helped the film turn military history into emotional spectacle. His voice, stillness, and burdened expression gave the audience a hero who seemed aware of both tactical danger and symbolic responsibility.

For international viewers, the key is to understand the cultural weight before judging the film only as action. The scale of its Korean reception came partly from national memory. The story let viewers experience victory, fear, sacrifice, and collective pride through a familiar historical figure. Choi's job was to make that figure feel human enough to follow and monumental enough to believe.

## *Exhuma*: The Veteran Actor in a New Occult Wave

In 2024, *Exhuma* showed that Choi Min-sik could still anchor a contemporary mass hit. The Korean Film Council's KoBiz database lists *Exhuma* as a 2024 South Korean mystery-horror film directed by Jang Jae-hyun, starring Choi Min-sik, Kim Go-eun, Yoo Hai-jin, and Lee Do-hyun. KoBiz lists total admissions above 11.9 million, and Yonhap reported that the film became the first 2024 release in Korea to pass 10 million admissions.

That success matters because *Exhuma* is not a simple nostalgia vehicle. It is a genre film about graves, shamans, geomancy, ancestry, wealth, land, and buried history. Choi plays a veteran geomancer, a role that depends less on explosive rage and more on authority, suspicion, fatigue, and ritual seriousness. He does not need to dominate every frame; he gives the film a grounded center while younger and ensemble characters bring different energies.

For viewers learning Korean cinema, *Exhuma* is a useful case because it shows how commercial Korean genre films can carry cultural material that may not be obvious in translation. Grave relocation, family lineage, shamanic ritual, feng shui, colonial memory, and land anxiety are not random spooky details. They are part of the emotional engine. Choi's presence helps the film feel rooted rather than gimmicky.

## How to Watch Choi Min-sik as a New Viewer

Start with the question you actually care about. If you want the global Korean-cinema canon, watch *Oldboy*, but prepare for violence, taboo material, and emotional discomfort. If you want a Korean historical blockbuster, watch *The Admiral: Roaring Currents* with some background on Admiral Yi Sun-sin. If you want a contemporary genre hit with cultural texture, watch *Exhuma*. If you want to understand his range, continue into crime, drama, and prestige roles afterward.

Do not watch only for "intensity." That is the easiest but shallowest reading. Watch how often he changes scale inside one scene. He may begin with quiet embarrassment, shift into threat, break into humor, and end with wounded pride. That ability to change emotional weather without making the transition feel mechanical is one reason his performances stay alive.

Also notice how Korean films use food, alcohol, rooms, alleys, cars, offices, ritual spaces, and family language around him. Choi's characters often exist inside social systems that are already pressurized. The drama is not simply "one angry man." It is often one person trapped in history, hierarchy, revenge, corruption, debt, war memory, or family obligation.

## Where Choi Fits in the Korean Cinema Boom

Korean cinema's global rise did not happen overnight with *Parasite* or streaming K-dramas. It was built through decades of directors, actors, festivals, domestic audiences, genre experimentation, and a film culture willing to mix commercial energy with social critique. Choi Min-sik belongs to the generation that made international viewers take Korean film seriously before the current streaming era.

His work with directors such as Park Chan-wook helped create a global image of Korean cinema as bold and unpredictable. His historical and commercial roles showed that domestic audiences wanted large-scale stories with emotional force. His later genre work shows how established actors can remain relevant when Korean film and streaming audiences shift toward horror, occult mystery, crime series, and hybrid genres.

For EpicKor readers, the larger point is simple: Choi is a gateway. If you understand why his performances matter, you will understand more about Korean storytelling itself: unresolved emotion, social pressure, family memory, class tension, ritual, food, violence, humor, and the strange way Korean films can move from absurdity to tragedy in one breath.

## FAQ

**Q: What is Choi Min-sik's most famous role internationally?**
A: His most famous international role is Oh Dae-su in Park Chan-wook's *Oldboy*. The film became a major global reference point for modern Korean cinema and remains the first Choi performance many overseas viewers encounter.

**Q: Why is *The Admiral: Roaring Currents* important in Korea?**
A: It became a massive domestic box-office phenomenon and dramatized Admiral Yi Sun-sin, one of Korea's most revered historical figures. Choi's performance helped carry both the action scale and the patriotic emotional weight.

**Q: Did *Exhuma* really pass 10 million admissions?**
A: Yes. Yonhap reported that *Exhuma* became the first 2024 release in Korea to surpass 10 million admissions, and KoBiz lists total admissions above 11.9 million.

**Q: Is Choi Min-sik only known for violent roles?**
A: No. Violence is part of some famous films, but his reputation comes from emotional range, historical authority, moral complexity, and the ability to make extreme stories feel human.

**Q: What should I watch first if I am new to Korean cinema?**
A: If you can handle dark material, start with *Oldboy*. If you prefer a more accessible historical spectacle, start with *The Admiral: Roaring Currents*. If you like horror mystery, start with *Exhuma*.

**Q: Why do people connect Choi Min-sik with Korean emotion like han?**
A: Many of his roles express unresolved grief, resentment, endurance, and social pressure. Viewers often read those emotions through the Korean concept of han, though his performances also include humor, pride, warmth, and absurdity.
