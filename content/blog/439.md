English guides to Korean sesame oil are taste tests. Somebody buys seven bottles, ranks them by aroma, and tells you which one won. That is useful, and it is also the entire genre.

None of them mention the thing a Korean shopper checks first, because it is written in Korean on the back of the bottle: **whether the product is 참기름 at all.**

In Korea, "sesame oil" and "sesame-flavoured oil" are **two different legal food types**. A bottle can sit on the shelf beside the real thing, look almost identical, smell strongly of sesame, and be corn oil with an aroma added. There is a word for that, it appears in the ingredient list, and once you know it the shelf stops being confusing.

Then there is the other half of the shelf: **the same volume of oil ranging from ₩3,500 to over ₩20,000**, which has nothing to do with quality tiers and everything to do with where the seed was grown.

## 향미유: the word that changes what you bought

Korea's food code defines **향미유 (hyangmiyu)** as edible oil — at least 50% of the product — blended with spices, flavourings, natural extracts or seasonings, for the purpose of adding aroma when cooking or processing food.

Read that again with a shopper's eyes. **Half the bottle can be something else entirely**, and the sesame character can come from added flavouring rather than from pressed seed.

The definition contains one detail that tells you how deliberate this boundary is: **cold-pressed and supercritical-extracted sesame and perilla oils are specifically excluded** from the 향미유 category. The law names the real processes and carves them out, which means the category exists precisely to hold everything that is not them.

So the check is mechanical:

**Turn the bottle over. Read 원재료명. If 향미유 is in there, it is not 100% sesame.**

You cannot do this by smell, and this is the part worth internalising. **A strong sesame aroma is not evidence of purity** — it is the exact property a flavouring is added to produce. Korean regulators developed a laboratory method in 2015 specifically because blended sesame oil could not be reliably identified any other way. If a national food-safety agency needed a lab test, your nose is not going to settle it in an aisle.

Korean consumer advice on spotting a fake, for what it is worth, runs the other direction from what people expect: the suspicious bottles are often the ones that smell *too* strong, or oddly artificial, and run thin rather than viscous.

## Why the same oil costs six times as much

![Chart on Korean sesame oil pricing: imported sesame at about 3,500 to 5,500 won for 500ml, roughly 700 to 1,100 won per 100ml; Korean-grown sesame at around 11,000 won for 200ml and past 20,000 won for 300ml, roughly 5,500 to 6,700 won per 100ml, six to seven times the imported price on origin alone; and the local mill, where you bring your own sesame and pay a pressing fee reported around 18,000 won, with about 6kg of seed yielding eight bottles.](/assets/images/posts/439/korean-sesame-oil-price-ladder.jpg)
*The spread on a Korean supermarket shelf, and the tier above it that never leaves the country. Chart by EpicKor.*

Stand in front of the oil section in a Korean supermarket and the prices look irrational. They are not. They are almost entirely a question of **where the sesame grew**.

- **Imported sesame**: roughly **₩3,500–5,500 for 500ml** — about **₩700–1,100 per 100ml**.
- **Korean-grown sesame (국산)**: around **₩11,000 for 200ml**, and **past ₩20,000 for 300ml** — about **₩5,500–6,700 per 100ml**.

That is **six to seven times the price for the same volume**, and the bottles can sit on the same shelf.

The reason is agricultural rather than culinary. **Korea's sesame self-sufficiency runs at roughly 8–12%.** Nearly nine-tenths of the sesame used in the country is imported, and domestic seed is priced accordingly. When a Korean recipe blog insists on 국산 참기름, it is not brand snobbery; it is a genuinely different and much scarcer input.

![A close-up of pale sesame seeds filling the frame, showing the flattened teardrop shape of each seed.](/assets/images/posts/439/sesame-seeds-close-up.jpg)
*The whole price question in one ingredient: Korea grows between eight and twelve per cent of the sesame it uses.*

For most cooking, imported oil from a reputable Korean brand is the sensible buy, and it is what is available abroad anyway. The domestic bottle is worth it when the oil is the flavour rather than a background note — a finishing drizzle, a dipping sauce, cold namul.

## The tier that never leaves Korea

There is a third option that no export catalogue contains, and it is the reason Korean families have opinions about sesame oil at all.

A **방앗간 (bangatgan)** is a neighbourhood mill. You bring your own sesame — often bought at a market where you picked the seed yourself — and they press it in front of you. Reported pressing fees run around **₩18,000**, and roughly **6kg of seed yields about eight bottles**.

![A small Korean neighbourhood mill on a village street, its blue sign reading 부창 떡방앗간 with a phone number, and hand-painted window text listing gochugaru, misutgaru and chamgireum.](/assets/images/posts/439/korean-bangatgan-oil-mill.jpg)
*A 방앗간 in Changnyeong. The window text lists 고추가루, 미숫가루 and 참기름 — chilli powder, grain powder, sesame oil. Photo by Visviva, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The oil that comes out of one of these is unfiltered, still warm, and does not keep as long as the supermarket version, which is exactly why it never became an export product. **It is not a premium SKU. It is a service.**

Understanding that this tier exists changes how you read Korean writing about sesame oil. When Korean sources talk about oil going stale, or about buying small bottles, they are often reasoning from fresh-pressed oil rather than from a sealed supermarket bottle with a long shelf life.

## 기름장: the sauce nobody can name

One of the most-searched things about Korean sesame oil in English is not about the oil. It is people trying to remember the name of **that small dish of oil and salt** that arrives with grilled pork.

It is called **기름장 (gireumjang)** — literally "oil sauce". Some people call it 소금장, salt sauce, but 기름장 is the ordinary word.

It is as simple as it looks: **sesame oil and salt at roughly 3:1**, sometimes with minced garlic, black pepper or a pinch of gochugaru. That is the whole recipe.

![Slices of pork belly cooking on a round Korean grill plate, a hand turning a piece with metal tongs, with lettuce and side dishes around the edge of the table.](/assets/images/posts/439/samgyeopsal-korean-grill.jpg)
*The table where the small dish of oil and salt appears. It has a name, and it is 기름장.*

What makes it worth understanding rather than just copying is **why it exists on a table that already has ssamjang.** Korean explanations are consistent on the point: ssamjang and salted sauces are assertive and cover the meat, while 기름장 is deliberately mild — it carries the fat and lets the pork taste like pork. Two sauces on one table because they do opposite jobs.

Our guide to [ssamjang, the sauce on the other side of the table](/blog/083) covers the assertive one, and [doenjang vs gochujang](/blog/373) sorts out the two pastes it is built from.

### Sesame or perilla in the dish?

Both are used, and Korean guidance splits them by cut:

- **참기름 (sesame)** — nutty, rounded, heavier. Pairs with **fatty cuts**: beef, samgyeopsal.
- **들기름 (perilla)** — sharper, greener, more aromatic. Pairs with **lean cuts**: neck, brisket.

If you have only had sesame oil, perilla is the one worth trying next; it is not a substitute so much as a different argument about what the meat needs.

## How to store it, and why Korean advice sounds paranoid

Sesame oil is a finishing oil. Its value is aroma, and aroma is the first thing to go.

- **Keep it away from heat and light.** Not next to the hob, however convenient that is.
- **Buy the size you will finish.** The 320ml bottle is not a worse deal than the 500ml if the 500ml goes flat halfway through.
- **Close it properly.** Oxygen does most of the damage.
- **Refrigeration is fine** and will not solidify it the way olive oil does.

Korean advice on this is stricter than Western advice, and the 방앗간 explains why: much of the domestic conversation is about **unfiltered fresh-pressed oil**, which genuinely does not last. A sealed supermarket bottle is more forgiving — but the aroma still fades, and aroma is the entire reason you bought it.

## FAQ About Korean Sesame Oil

**Q: What is the oil and salt sauce at Korean BBQ called?**

기름장 (gireumjang), literally "oil sauce". It is sesame oil and salt at roughly 3:1, sometimes with minced garlic or pepper. Some people call it 소금장. It exists alongside ssamjang because it is mild where ssamjang is assertive — it carries the fat instead of covering it.

**Q: How do I tell real Korean sesame oil from fake?**

Read 원재료명, the ingredient list on the back. If it contains 향미유, the product is a flavoured oil rather than pure sesame oil — Korea treats those as separate food types. You cannot judge it by smell; Korean regulators built a laboratory test in 2015 precisely because blended oil could not be identified otherwise.

**Q: Why is Korean sesame oil so expensive?**

Because of where the seed grew, not how it was made. Korea's sesame self-sufficiency is roughly 8–12%, so domestic seed is scarce. Imported-sesame oil runs about ₩700–1,100 per 100ml; Korean-grown runs ₩5,500–6,700 per 100ml — six to seven times more for the same volume.

**Q: What is a 방앗간?**

A neighbourhood mill. You bring your own sesame and pay a pressing fee — reported around ₩18,000, with about 6kg of seed making roughly eight bottles — and the oil is pressed in front of you. It is unfiltered and does not keep long, which is why this tier has no export equivalent.

**Q: Should I buy sesame oil or perilla oil?**

Both, eventually. Korean guidance pairs 참기름 with fatty cuts like samgyeopsal and beef, and 들기름 with leaner ones like neck or brisket. Perilla is sharper and greener; it is a different tool rather than a cheaper substitute.

## Bottom Line

The English-language question about Korean sesame oil is *which brand tastes best*. The Korean question is **what is actually in the bottle, and where did the seed come from** — and both are answered on the back label rather than by tasting.

**Read 원재료명. If 향미유 is listed, put it back.** Then decide on origin: imported for cooking, Korean-grown when the oil is the flavour, and a 방앗간 if you happen to be in Korea with a bag of sesame and an afternoon.

And when the small dish of oil and salt arrives with the pork, it is **기름장** — three ingredients, one job, and the reason there are two sauces on the table instead of one.
