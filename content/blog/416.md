**붕어빵** is Korea's great winter street pastry: a fish-shaped batter cake filled with sweet red bean, cooked in a hinged iron mould on a cart, handed over hot in a paper bag. It is seasonal, it is cash-only, and increasingly it is hard to find — which is why Koreans invented a word for living near one.

**참붕어빵** is what happens when a confectionery company decides that should not be a limitation.

Orion boxed the thing in 2011, deliberately launched it in **May**, sold **640 million packs** in Korea alone, and then sent it abroad under three completely different names — each one reaching for a different country's fish.

## 붕세권: Why a Boxed Bungeoppang Is Not a Downgrade

Korean has a family of words built on 역세권, "station catchment" — the desirable property of living within walking distance of a subway station. From it, half-jokingly, came **붕세권**: the catchment area of a **bungeoppang cart.**

The word exists because the carts have become scarce. They are seasonal, they are informal, the cost of red bean and flour has risen, and in any given winter Korean media runs a story about how few are left and how much a single fish now costs. There are crowd-sourced maps for finding them.

Against that, a boxed version that is available in July, at a fixed price, in every convenience store in the country, is not a sad substitute for the real thing. **It is solving the actual problem**, which is that the real thing is not there when you want it.

Orion's own framing has been exactly this from the start: a winter street snack turned into something you can eat 사시사철 — in all four seasons.

## Released in May, on Purpose

The launch is the part that gets retold in Korean coverage, because it looks like a mistake and was not.

참붕어빵 went on sale in **May 2011** — heading into summer, with a product whose entire reference point is a food Koreans eat in freezing weather. Korean write-ups call it a 역발상, a deliberate inversion.

It worked immediately. The product sold out **as fast as it could be shelved**, and the launch is remembered in Korea as a stock shortage rather than a launch.

The logic is the same as the 붕세권 point. In December you can buy the real thing. In May you cannot, and nobody else was selling one.

## 팥붕 or 슈붕: Korea's Oldest Snack Argument

*EpicKor chart.*

Ask Koreans what belongs inside a bungeoppang and you will start an argument that has been running for decades.

**팥붕** is red bean — the original, the traditionalist position. **슈붕** is 슈크림, custard cream — the modern one. People genuinely identify as **팥붕파** or **슈붕파**, cart signs specify which they sell, and the debate resurfaces every winter with the reliability of a public holiday.

If custard is the side you land on, Korea already sells that year-round and underground: [Deli Manjoo (델리만주)](/blog/071) is the same warm-mould-and-custard idea in a corn shape, made to order inside subway stations instead of on a winter cart.

Orion's approach to this national question was to refuse to answer it.

**The original 참붕어빵 (2011)** contains glutinous rice cake and chocolate cream — already a third position, since street bungeoppang contains neither.

**호떡 품은 참붕어빵 (2021)** — "hotteok inside" — borrows the filling of Korea's *other* great winter street food, the cinnamon-syrup pancake. It now accounts for roughly **24% of all 참붕어빵 sales**, which is a remarkable share for a variant.

**참붕어빵 우유슈크림맛** goes directly at the 슈붕파, with milk custard, and adds an argument neither cart can make: **180 mg of calcium per 29 g piece**, about a glass of milk, pitched at parents buying snacks for growing children.

That is three products, none of which is 팥붕.

## Four Countries, Four Fish

![Chart of the four market names: 참붕어빵 in Korea from May 2011, 小鱼糯糯 in China from 2019, Bống Bang in Vietnam from 2024 after the Tấm Cám folktale, and Bungo in Russia, with global sales past 40 billion won.](/assets/images/posts/416/cham-bungeoppang-four-names.jpg)
*EpicKor chart.*

This is the part that makes 참붕어빵 worth writing about rather than just eating.

Orion did not translate the name into other markets, and it did not romanise it either. **It replaced the fish.**

**China, 2019 — 小鱼糯糯 (xiǎo yú nuò nuò).** Local production began that year. The name means roughly "little fish, sticky-sticky," and **糯** is the character used for glutinous rice — so the Chinese name describes the texture the Korean name leaves implied. Cumulative sales there passed **₩40 billion** by early in the reporting year.

**Vietnam, 2024 — Bống Bang (봉방).** This is the elegant one. **Tấm Cám** is Vietnam's Cinderella story, and in it the heroine's companion and protector is a fish named **Bống**. The name also points at **Bống Bống Bang Bang**, an enormous Vietnamese pop hit built on that tale. So a Korean snack shaped like a Korean street pastry entered Vietnam named after a Vietnamese folktale fish and a song everyone already knew.

**Russia — Bungo (붕고).** No folklore here: the Korean word simply clipped into something a Russian speaker can say.

Four markets, four strategies. Korea kept the street-food reference, China described the filling, Vietnam borrowed its own mythology, and Russia just made the word pronounceable.

## The Box Counts Them in 마리

Korean uses different counter words for different kinds of thing: 개 for objects, 명 for people, 마리 for animals and fish.

The 참붕어빵 box does not say 12개. **It says 12마리 — twelve fish.**

It is a joke, and it is the single most Korean thing about the packaging. But it also tells you what the product is claiming to be: not twelve pieces of confectionery, but twelve of the thing you would have bought from a cart, if a cart had been there.

The rest of the box is more literal. **348 g, 1,452 kcal** — about **121 kcal per fish** — and **찹쌀떡 6.37%**, the declared glutinous-rice-cake content. The English strapline is **MOIST & CHEWY CAKE**, and *cake* is the accurate word: this is a soft sponge, not the crisp-edged batter of a street bungeoppang.

## It Has Quietly Become a Souvenir

Something happened to this product that Orion did not plan.

Word spread on social media outside Korea, and 참붕어빵 turned into what Korean coverage now calls a **한국여행 필수 기념품** — an essential Korea-travel souvenir. It is cheap, it is unmistakably fish-shaped, it survives a suitcase, and it is bought by the box for colleagues and family.

The data behind that is specific: in supermarkets in **Seoul Station and Myeongdong** — the districts where foreign visitors actually shop — Orion measured sales up **more than 20% year on year**, against a national rise of over 40% in the same January–February window.

And the company has responded in the most direct way possible. It is producing a **참붕어빵 코리아 에디션** for Seoul's main tourist districts, with the fish dressed in traditional costume — **선비** the scholar, **각시** the bride, **도령** the young gentleman.

**If you are reading this before a trip to Korea, that edition was made for you specifically.** Which is either charming or slightly unnerving, depending on your mood.

## Honest Notes

**It is not a bungeoppang and it does not pretend to be.** The street version is a thin crisp batter shell around hot red bean, eaten within a minute of leaving the mould. This is a soft, sweet, shelf-stable cake with rice cake and chocolate cream in it. Liking one tells you nothing about the other.

**Early versions were not good.** Korean commentary is candid that the original was greasy and heavily nitrogen-padded, and that it took years to become genuinely pleasant. If you tried it a decade ago and dismissed it, it is a different product now.

**121 kcal per fish adds up quickly.** They are small and they are individually wrapped, which is exactly the combination that makes people eat four.

**호떡 품은 is the one to try if you only try one variant.** A quarter of all sales is a strong verdict, and the cinnamon-syrup filling is further from what you can buy elsewhere than the custard version is.

**The Hanaro Mart exclusivity thing is real.** Some variants have launched into a single retail chain first, so a flavour existing does not mean the shop in front of you has it.

**참붕어빵 초코붕 is discontinued.** It replaced the red bean with chocolate cream. If you find it referenced online, it is no longer made.

## FAQ

**Q: Is 참붕어빵 the same as street bungeoppang?**
**A:** No. Street 붕어빵 is a hot, thin-shelled batter cake filled with red bean, made to order on a cart in winter. 참붕어빵 is a shelf-stable soft cake with glutinous rice cake and chocolate cream, sold year round.

**Q: Why was it launched in May?**
**A:** Deliberately. Korean coverage calls it a 역발상 — releasing a winter food heading into summer, when nobody else was selling one. It sold out as fast as it was shelved.

**Q: What does 12마리 mean on the box?**
**A:** Twelve *fish*. 마리 is the Korean counter for animals, not objects — the packaging counts the snacks as if they were live carp.

**Q: What is it called outside Korea?**
**A:** 小鱼糯糯 in China (local production from 2019), Bống Bang in Vietnam (from the Tấm Cám folktale, 2024), and Bungo in Russia. Global sales have passed ₩40 billion.

**Q: 팥붕 or 슈붕?**
**A:** Neither, in the original — it has chocolate cream. Orion later added a milk-custard version for the 슈붕파, with 180 mg of calcium per piece.
