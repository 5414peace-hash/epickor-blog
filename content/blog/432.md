Ask a Korean which ointment to put on a scrape and you will get one of two brand names: **후시딘 (Fucidin)** or **마데카솔 (Madecassol)**. Ask which is better and you will usually get a pause, because it is the wrong question — Koreans use them in **sequence**, not instead of each other.

That much is findable. What is not findable in English is the part that actually matters at the counter:

**"Madecassol" is not one product.** There is a green box that contains centella and nothing else, sold in Daiso for ₩5,000. There is a yellow-green box, same brand name across the front, that contains centella **plus an antibiotic** and is pharmacy-only. Their labels say opposite things: the green one tells you **not** to use it on an infected wound; the yellow one is specifically **for** infected wounds.

If you cannot read Korean, the front of the box will not tell you which one you are holding.

## Fucidin: the antibiotic half, with a time limit printed on it

**후시딘연고** is made by **Dongwha Pharm**, whose tubes carry "SINCE 1897" because the company is Korea's oldest pharmaceutical firm. The active ingredient is **sodium fusidate at 20mg per gram** — 2% fusidic acid, a genuine topical antibiotic. It is **일반의약품**: no prescription, but pharmacy-only.

![An orange and white tube of Fucidin ointment, 10g, from Dongwha Pharm, with the Korean product name 후시딘연고 and the company's SINCE 1897 mark.](/assets/images/posts/432/fucidin-ointment-dongwha.jpg)
*Fucidin ointment, 10g. The orange tube is the one Korean households keep in the drawer. Image: Dongwha Pharm official product page, used for product identification.*

The approved indications are specific and bacterial: impetigo, infectious eczematous dermatitis, acne vulgaris, folliculitis, boils, and — the one that covers a traveller's scraped knee — **secondary infection of burns, trauma, sutured wounds and grafts**, caused by staphylococci, streptococci, corynebacteria or clostridia.

**Two limits are printed in the official directions, and both are easy to ignore.**

**Do not spread it thickly**, and **limit treatment to about one week**. That is not a cautious blogger's addition; it is in the 용법·용량 section of the product information. Fusidic acid is absorbed well through skin, so the label also warns against applying it over large areas or for extended periods.

The reason is resistance. Korean regulatory documentation on this ingredient is unusually direct about it: confirm susceptibility, use the **shortest period clinically necessary**, and stop if a non-susceptible organism overgrows. Fucidin is in a lot of Korean bathroom cabinets and gets used on things that are not bacterial infections at all — athlete's foot, eczema, acne someone decided to squeeze — and that is exactly the pattern that breeds resistance.

**Practical version:** use it while the wound is dirty or looks infected. Then stop. It is not a general-purpose healing cream.

## Madecassol: two tubes, one name, opposite instructions

Here is the part that sends people home with the wrong tube.

![Side-by-side comparison: the green Madecassol Ointment box, a quasi-drug with centella only, sold at Daiso for ₩5,000 and labelled not for infected wounds; and the yellow-green Madecassol Care box, an OTC medicine with centella plus neomycin sulfate 3.5mg/g, pharmacy-only and labelled for early treatment of infected wounds.](/assets/images/posts/432/madecassol-vs-madecassol-care.jpg)
*Same brand across the front, different legal class, different ingredient, opposite instruction. Korean shoppers use box colour. Images: Dongkook Pharm official product pages.*

**마데카솔연고 — the green box.** Centella quantified extract, **10mg per gram** (4mg as asiaticoside), and nothing else. Its legal class is **의약외품**, a quasi-drug rather than a medicine, which is why it can sit on a Daiso shelf next to plasters instead of behind a pharmacy counter. The indication is modest and honestly worded: **"보조적 부분 치료"** — supplementary local treatment of wounds and skin ulcers. And its label says plainly not to use it on a **secondarily infected wound**.

**마데카솔케어 — the yellow-green box.** Same centella at 10mg/g, plus **neomycin sulfate at 3.5mg per gram**. That is an aminoglycoside antibiotic, and it moves the product into **일반의약품**, pharmacy-only. Its indication inverts the first one: early treatment of skin conditions **secondarily infected by neomycin-susceptible bacteria** — small lacerations, grazes, sutured wounds, superficial second-degree burns.

The Care version carries warnings the plain one does not:

- **Avoid prolonged continuous use**, because of kidney and hearing risks associated with the aminoglycoside class.
- **Sensitisation risk rises after about eight days** of use — neomycin is one of the more common contact allergens in topical products.
- Not for wounds on or near the eye, and not on the breast while breastfeeding.

There is also a **복합마데카솔** in the family which adds a corticosteroid to the antibiotic. If you are reaching for a tube in Korea without reading Korean, the safe default is the **green box**: the one that is only centella.

## The cica in your skincare and the ointment in the drawer are the same thing

If you have bought Korean skincare in the last few years you have met **cica** — cica creams, cica balms, cica pads, marketed for redness and a compromised skin barrier.

**Cica is centella asiatica.** The same plant extract, standardised to the same active compounds, that Dongkook Pharm has been putting in a wound ointment for decades. The K-beauty category did not invent the ingredient; it borrowed a pharmacy product's active and moved it into a cosmetic format.

This is worth knowing in both directions. It explains why cica products are pitched at *damaged* skin rather than at glow — the heritage is wound repair, not brightening. And it means that if what you actually want is centella at a therapeutic concentration on a specific scrape, the ₩5,000 tube in Daiso is a more direct route than a ₩30,000 jar of cream. ## Buying it without Korean

Find the green cross and the sign **약국 (yakguk)**, then:

- **Say the brand name.** "후시딘" and "마데카솔" both work as spoken words and are instantly understood. Brand names travel far better than ingredients here.
- **Show the wound if you can.** The pharmacist's first question is whether it is dirty or already closing, because that is exactly the fork this article describes.
- **If you want the plain centella one, say "마데카솔연고"** and add "케어 아니에요" ("not Care") if you want to be sure — or just buy the green box in Daiso.
- **Say if you have a neomycin or antibiotic allergy** before accepting Madecassol Care. This is the single question worth preparing.

For pharmacies, hospitals and what to do if the scrape turns into something worse, our [Korea healthcare guide for tourists](/blog/190) covers the system; the [Korea tourist shopping route](/blog/173) has where Daiso and Olive Young sit on a typical day.

**A boundary, stated plainly:** this describes what Korean product labels say and which shops may sell what. It is not medical advice. Deep wounds, animal bites, anything with spreading redness or fever, or a wound that is not improving needs a doctor rather than a shelf.

## FAQ About Korean Wound Ointments

**Q: Fucidin or Madecassol — which is better?**

Neither, because they do different jobs and Koreans use them in order. **Fucidin** is an antibiotic for a wound that is dirty or infected. **Madecassol** is centella for a wound that is closing, aimed at the scar. The usual sequence is Fucidin first, then switch.

**Q: What is the difference between Madecassol and Madecassol Care?**

**마데카솔연고** contains only centella extract (10mg/g) and is a quasi-drug you can buy outside a pharmacy. **마데카솔케어** contains the same centella **plus neomycin sulfate 3.5mg/g**, an antibiotic, which makes it a pharmacy-only medicine. Their labels are opposite: the plain one is not for infected wounds, the Care one is specifically for them.

**Q: Can I buy Korean wound ointment outside a pharmacy?**

Yes — the plain 마데카솔연고 is classed as **의약외품** (quasi-drug), so Daiso, marts and some convenience stores carry it. **8g costs ₩5,000 at Daiso.** Fucidin and Madecassol Care are 일반의약품 and pharmacy-only.

**Q: Is Madecassol the same as cica?**

Effectively yes. Madecassol's active is **centella asiatica** extract, which is what the K-beauty industry sells as "cica". The ointment came first; the cosmetic category borrowed the ingredient.

**Q: How long can I use Fucidin?**

Its own directions say to apply a thin layer and **limit treatment to about one week**. It is a topical antibiotic, and the product information is explicit about using the shortest necessary course to avoid resistance.

**Q: How much does Fucidin cost in Korea?**

About **₩6,300 to ₩10,000** for a 10g tube as of September 2026, depending on the pharmacy. Prices are set per shop, so the spread is real.

**Q: Is Fucidin available over the counter?**

In Korea, yes — it is **일반의약품**, which needs no prescription but must be bought in a pharmacy. In many other countries fusidic acid is prescription-only, which is why visitors often stock up here.

## Bottom Line

The Korean answer to a scraped knee is a two-step, and the steps are sold in different places.

Dirty wound: **후시딘**, from a pharmacy, thin layer, about a week and no longer. Closing wound and you care about the mark it leaves: **마데카솔**, and specifically the **green box** with centella and nothing else, which Daiso will sell you for ₩5,000 at ten at night.

The one thing worth memorising is that the brand name on the front is not the ingredient list. Two tubes say 마데카솔. Only one of them has an antibiotic in it, and it is not the one you probably meant to buy.
