There is a small brown bottle that has been sold in Korea since **1897**. It predates the Korean Empire's collapse, the colonial period, the division of the peninsula, and every company you associate with modern Korea. It is a digestive remedy called **활명수 (Hwalmyungsu)**, and Koreans drink it after eating too much.

**This month it goes on sale in the United States** — in supermarkets, pharmacies, Olive Young and on Amazon — under a new name, **K.O.D**, short for Korea Original Digestive.

And the bottle America is getting is not the bottle Koreans drink.

The Korean default is **까스활명수**, where 까스 means *gas*: it is carbonated, and it has been the country's go-to since 1967. The export product is the **non-carbonated 1897 liquid**, with L-aspartic acid and vitamin C added. Korea kept the fizzy one and sent the old one abroad, which is a strange and interesting decision worth understanding before you buy either.

## What is actually in the bottle

Hwalmyungsu is a **herbal medicine**, not a soda with a health claim attached. Dongwha Pharm's product information lists a set of traditional botanicals in each 75mL bottle, including:

- **현호색 (corydalis tuber)** — 180.0mg, the largest single component
- **진피 (dried citrus peel)** — 150.0mg
- **육계 (cinnamon bark)** — 30.0mg

plus further herbs in the same family of digestive botanicals. The approved indications are specific and unglamorous: **식욕감퇴** (loss of appetite), **위부팽만감** (stomach bloating), **소화불량** (indigestion), **과식** (overeating), **체함** (that particular Korean sense of food being stuck), **구역** and **구토** (nausea and vomiting).

The dose is one **75mL** bottle, **three times daily after meals**, for ages 15 and up, with reduced amounts for children. There is a dedicated children's version, **꼬마활명수액**, sold in small stick pouches.

**One caution matters more than the rest.** Corydalis is not recommended during pregnancy, and Korean pharmacists routinely flag this. It is the reason the pharmacy version is a medicine rather than a beverage, and the reason the next section matters.

## The bottle in the convenience store is not the same bottle

This is where visitors get caught, and it is the same trap Korea sets in several aisles at once.

**까스활명수-큐 is 일반의약품** — an over-the-counter medicine, which in Korea means **pharmacy-only**. A convenience store cannot legally sell it.

What convenience stores do sell are look-alikes with names built from the same syllables — **까스활**, **미인활** and others — which are classified as **의약외품**, quasi-drugs. They are lawful, they are not fakes, and they are not the same thing: the active content is lower, and the product is a different registration entirely.

## 1897, and why this bottle is in Korean history books

Hwalmyungsu is not old in the marketing sense. It is old in the archival sense.

In **1897**, a former royal court official named **Min Byeong-ho** and his son **Min Gang** opened **동화약방 (Dongwha Yakbang)** in Seosomun, Hanseong — the city now called Seoul. The product they launched, Hwalmyungsu, is recorded as **Korea's first modern medicine**. The name means, roughly, *the water that saves life*.

In **August 1910**, the company registered its **부채표** — the fan mark still printed on every box — as **trademark number 514**. The Hwalmyungsu name itself was registered that December.

Then the history gets less commercial. **Min Gang (1883–1931) was an independence activist**, and the money the bottle earned went into the independence movement. After the Provisional Government formed in Shanghai, **동화약방 served as its 서울 연통부** — the domestic liaison post in Seoul, used for passing information and funds. In **1919** the company opened a branch on Jongno near Tapgol Park, which was used as a distribution point for the Declaration of Independence, with takings paying for the printing.

There is one detail that reads like fiction and is well attested in Korean accounts: when Japanese surveillance made it too dangerous to send money to Shanghai, **Min Gang shipped the medicine itself**, so that funds could be raised by selling it on the ground in China.

In **1996**, Guinness recognised the company and product in **four categories**, including Korea's oldest manufacturer, oldest pharmaceutical firm, oldest registered trademark, and longest-running medicine.

None of that makes the drink work better. It does explain why a Korean pharmacy has stocked the same brown bottle for over a century, and why "129 years old" appears on the American packaging.

## 1967: the year carbonation won

For seventy years Hwalmyungsu was a still liquid. Then in **1967** Dongwha launched **까스활명수**, and the carbonated version became the one Koreans mean when they say the name.

![The green Gas Hwalmyungsu-Q box beside its brown bottle, marked SINCE 1897 and Indigestion Relief.](/assets/images/posts/434/gas-hwalmyungsu-q.jpg)
*까스활명수-큐 in the green box. This is the one on the pharmacy shelf and the one Koreans hand you after a heavy meal. Image: Dongwha Pharm official product page.*

The carbonation is not decoration. A burst of gas produces the belch that Korean digestive culture treats as the signal that the blockage — 체함 — has broken, and the sensation of relief arrives before any herb could plausibly act. Whether that is pharmacology or theatre is a fair question, and it is also beside the point: **it is what made the product a habit.**

That is what makes the export decision interesting.

## K.O.D: what is actually launching, and where

Dongwha released **Hwalmyungsu 1897 Liquid** domestically in **August 2026**, through pharmacies and duty-free shops, and is taking it to the **United States in September 2026** under the name **K.O.D — Korea Original Digestive**.

A company representative framed the significance precisely: Korean-made Hwalmyungsu **has been sold in Korean grocery stores abroad for years**, but this is the first product **designed for an overseas market**. That is the difference between a diaspora item and a launch.

**Why send the still one?** The company has not published its reasoning, so what follows is inference rather than fact, and worth reading as such. Two things are plainly true: a carbonated liquid is heavier, more fragile and more awkward to ship and shelf-stack than a still one, and the belch-as-relief convention that makes 까스활명수 satisfying in Korea does not exist as a shared expectation in an American supermarket. A still bottle with vitamin C added reads to a US shopper as a wellness shot, which is a category that already exists there. The fizz is the part that needs cultural translation, so it is the part that got left behind.

## Drinking it in Korea

**It is cheap, and the spread between pharmacies is real.** A single 75mL bottle of 까스활명수-큐 runs roughly **₩600 to ₩1,600** depending on the shop, and a ten-bottle box lands around **₩11,000**, on Korean pharmacy price-comparison listings checked for 2026. Korean pharmacies set their own prices, so the range is genuine rather than a discount — but at this level the difference is small enough that convenience should win.

Practical notes for a trip:

- **Buy it at a pharmacy (약국)**, not a convenience store, if you want the real thing. Say **"까스활명수"** and you will be handed the green box.
- **It is taken after a meal**, not during. The Korean use case is very specific: you overate, or something feels stuck, and you want it moving.
- **75mL is one dose.** It is a small bottle by design and it is not a beverage to sip.
- **It is bitter and sweet at once**, with a strong herbal finish. The carbonated version hides much of that; the still one does not.
- **Skip it if you are pregnant**, because of the corydalis, and ask the pharmacist if you are unsure about anything else you take.

## FAQ About Hwalmyungsu

**Q: What is Hwalmyungsu?**

A Korean herbal digestive liquid launched in **1897** by Dongwha Pharm, recorded as Korea's first modern medicine. It is taken after overeating or indigestion, in 75mL bottles, and the name translates roughly as "the water that saves life".

**Q: What is the difference between 활명수 and 까스활명수?**

**까스** means *gas*: 까스활명수, launched in **1967**, is the carbonated version, and it is what most Koreans mean by the name today. The original 활명수 is still (non-carbonated). The new US product, K.O.D, is based on the **non-carbonated** original.

**Q: Can I buy Hwalmyungsu in a convenience store?**

Not the real one. 까스활명수-큐 is **일반의약품**, an over-the-counter medicine that Korean law restricts to pharmacies. Convenience stores stock similarly named quasi-drug (**의약외품**) alternatives such as 까스활 and 미인활, which have lower active content and are a different product.

**Q: When is Hwalmyungsu available in the US?**

**September 2026**, as **K.O.D (Korea Original Digestive)**, in supermarkets, pharmacies, Olive Young and on Amazon. It launched in Korea in August 2026 through pharmacies and duty-free shops.

**Q: Is Hwalmyungsu safe in pregnancy?**

It contains **현호색 (corydalis)**, which is not recommended during pregnancy, and Korean pharmacists flag this routinely. Ask a pharmacist or doctor rather than assuming a 129-year-old product is automatically gentle.

**Q: Why is Hwalmyungsu in Korean history books?**

Its founders funded the independence movement with it. **동화약방** served as the Provisional Government's **Seoul liaison office (서울 연통부)**, its 1919 Jongno branch was used to distribute the Declaration of Independence, and when sending money to Shanghai became too dangerous, the founder's son shipped the medicine itself so it could be sold there to raise funds.

**Q: How much does Hwalmyungsu cost in Korea?**

Roughly **₩600–₩1,600** for a single 75mL bottle of 까스활명수-큐, and about **₩11,000** for a ten-bottle box, on Korean pharmacy price comparisons for 2026. Korean pharmacies price independently, so the spread between shops is real.

**Q: What does it taste like?**

Sweet and bitter together, with a strong herbal finish and, in the carbonated version, a sharp fizz. It is medicine that tastes like medicine. The still version tastes more so, which is worth knowing before the American bottle surprises anyone.

## Bottom Line

Most 129-year-old products survive as heritage. This one survived as a habit — a specific bottle for a specific feeling, kept behind a pharmacy counter, with a fan on the box and a story about the independence movement attached to it.

What is happening this month is that the story is being sold abroad for the first time as a product rather than as an import. If you are in Korea, buy the green box at a pharmacy and get the fizz. If you are in America in September, what will be on the shelf is the older, quieter version — the 1897 one, which Koreans mostly stopped choosing in 1967.
