Walk through **Euljiro (을지로)** at two in the afternoon and you will wonder why anyone told you to come. It is a working district: pallets of paper, compressors stacked on the pavement, LED strips and drill bits and industrial motors, men moving stock on handcarts, the sound of metal being cut.

Come back at seven in the evening and the same alley has red plastic stools down the middle of it, folding tables, and people drinking beer in front of the shutters that closed an hour ago.

**Both are Euljiro. That is the entire point of the place, and most guides only describe one of them.**

Koreans call the nightlife version **힙지로 (*Hipjiro*)** — a portmanteau of "hip" and "Euljiro". But the hip part only exists because the working part was there first, and understanding that connection is the difference between a good night here and a confusing one.

## Why There Are Bars in a Machine District

This is the causal link that English coverage almost always skips, and it explains everything about the atmosphere.

Euljiro was — and still partly is — **Korea's largest printing district**. Books, leaflets, business cards, packaging: an enormous share of what got printed in Korea passed through these blocks. Printing runs to deadlines, which means **night shifts**.

Workers coming off those shifts wanted a cheap, fast drink near where they worked. So the drinking spot grew where the labour was.

The origin point is documented: **Euljiro OB Bear (을지OB베어) opened in November 1980** as the first *nogari* beer house here. What followed became **노가리 골목 — Nogari Alley** — and Seoul designated it a **Seoul Future Heritage (서울미래유산) site in 2015**.

![A paper wholesaler in Euljiro's printing alley, stacks of paper stock filling the shopfront.](/assets/images/posts/349/euljiro-printing-alley-paper-wholesaler.jpg)

*A paper wholesaler in the printing alley. This industry is the reason the bars are here. Photo by Aaaatu, CC BY-SA 4.0.*

So the sequence is: **printing district → night shifts → cheap beer near the presses → decades later, that alley becomes the atmosphere young Seoulites travel across the city for.** The retro look is not styling. It is what a working district actually looks like.

## What Nogari Actually Is

**노가리 (*nogari*)** is dried juvenile pollack. It arrives in a small pile, often lightly grilled, sometimes with a gochujang-mayo dip, and it is chewy, salty and strongly savoury.

**It is not a meal.** It is engineered to be eaten slowly while drinking, which is why it costs about ₩1,000–1,500 and why nobody orders one each and stops.

The pairing has its own name — **노맥 (*nomaek*)**, nogari plus *maekju* (beer) — following the same construction as *chimaek* for chicken and beer.

**How to order without stress:**

- Sit down first. **Seating is outdoors, first-come, and nobody will seat you**
- Order **생맥주 (*saengmaekju*)**, draft beer, by the glass or pitcher
- Order nogari to share. One or two plates for a table of three or four
- Most shops have a small menu of other anju — 골뱅이 (sea snails), 계란말이 (rolled omelette), grilled items. Add one if you want something more substantial

**If you do not eat dried fish, this specific alley is not for you** — and that is fine, because Euljiro has far more than the nogari street. See below.

## What Else Is Actually There

Nogari Alley gets the photographs, but treating Euljiro as one alley wastes the trip.

![Euljiro tool and lighting shops with power tools, motors and LED strips displayed out onto the pavement.](/assets/images/posts/349/euljiro-tool-and-lighting-shops.jpg)

*The daytime district — tools, motors, transformers and lighting, spilling onto the pavement. Photo by Aaaatu, CC BY-SA 4.0.*

- **The tool and lighting streets.** Compressors, drills, LED strips, industrial transformers, shop signs for 조명백화점 (lighting department stores). Genuinely interesting to walk even if you buy nothing
- **The furniture street (을지로 가구거리)**, a long run of furniture and interior suppliers
- **The printing alleys**, where paper wholesalers and small presses still operate
- **The hidden bars and cafes.** This is the *Hipjiro* part: small, deliberately unmarked places, frequently on the **second floor or up a back staircase**, behind a door that looks like a workshop entrance

That last point is the practical one. **Many of Euljiro's best-known bars have no visible sign.** You find them by address and by looking up. If you are expecting a shopfront, you will walk past them.

## How to Actually Do a Night Here

A workable plan rather than a list of names, since individual shops open and close constantly.

**Arrive around 17:00–18:00 on a weekday.** You will get a seat outdoors, watch the alley fill, and see the transition from working district to drinking district happen in real time. That transition is the single most memorable thing about Euljiro and it is over by about 19:30.

**Walk the daytime streets first, while some are still open.** Fifteen minutes through the tool and lighting blocks before you sit down completely changes what the alley means when you do.

**Start in the alley, then go up.** Nogari and beer outdoors first, then find one of the upstairs bars for the second round. That order matches how the neighbourhood actually works.

**Bring cash.** Card is widely accepted, but the smallest and oldest stalls are the ones where cash is smoothest.

## How to Find a Bar With No Sign

The single most practical skill for Euljiro, and the thing that separates people who enjoy it from people who wander for an hour.

**Assume the entrance looks wrong.** Many of the well-regarded places are behind a door that reads as a workshop, a stairwell beside a shopfront, or a corridor between two wholesalers. There is often no English, no menu outside, and sometimes no name at all.

**Look up, not along.** Ground floors here belong to businesses that need loading access, so bars and cafes concentrate on the **second and third floors**. If you only scan at eye level you will miss most of them.

**Use the address, not the name.** Korean map apps navigate by address reliably; searching a bar's name in English often fails. Get the address first, walk to the building, then find the stairs.

**Signals that a door is a bar:** a small warm light above it, a doorbell or intercom, a hand-lettered paper sign, shoes or a doormat that does not belong to a workshop, or simply people going in wearing clothes nobody wears to move pallets.

**And a warning worth taking seriously:** because these are small rooms in old buildings, many hold ten to twenty people. **A full bar is full.** There is rarely a waiting list — you leave and try the next one, which is why having two or three addresses beats having one.

### The shutters are part of it

Look at the shutters themselves as you walk. Euljiro has been the site of shutter-art projects, and painted roller doors are a common sight along these blocks.

It is a neat physical summary of the whole neighbourhood: **the shutter is a working door by day and a canvas at night**, visible only during the hours the businesses are closed. You cannot see the art while the district is doing its actual job.

## When to Skip Euljiro

Being straight about this saves a wasted evening.

- **Skip it in the afternoon if you only have one visit.** Between roughly 10:00 and 16:00 this is a wholesale district doing business. Interesting to walk, not what you came for
- **Skip it in heavy rain or deep winter.** The alley experience is outdoor seating on plastic stools. Indoor bars exist, but the thing that makes Euljiro special does not happen under an umbrella
- **Skip it on a Friday night if you dislike crowds.** It is genuinely packed, and there is no queue system — people wait by standing near tables
- **Do not come looking for a specific viral bar.** Small unmarked places here open, move and close often, and building a night around one address is how people end up disappointed
- **This is not a family dinner destination.** It is an adult drinking street with outdoor seating in a working district

## The Redevelopment Question

Worth knowing, because it affects what you will find and because Koreans feel strongly about it.

Euljiro sits in the middle of long-running redevelopment planning. Rents rose after area redevelopment schemes advanced from around **2014**, and a further urban improvement project approved in **2018** put much of the Nogari Alley frontage at risk. Even Euljiro OB Bear, the 1980 original and a designated Seoul Future Heritage site, became a conflict case.

The tension is genuine: the same industrial texture that makes the neighbourhood atmospheric is old, low-rise, and sitting on extremely valuable central Seoul land.

**Practical implication for a visitor:** what is here now may not be here in a few years, and specific shops change hands more often than guidebooks update. **Go for the district, not for a checklist.**

## FAQ About Euljiro

**Q: What is Euljiro known for?**

**A:** Two things at once. By day it is a working district of printing houses, tool and lighting wholesalers, and metal workshops in central Seoul. By night, the same alleys fill with outdoor tables serving beer and dried pollack, which is why Koreans nicknamed it Hipjiro.

**Q: Which subway exit should I use?**

**A:** Euljiro 3-ga Station on Lines 2 and 3. Exit 4 leads toward Nogari Alley; Exit 10 is nearest Euljiro OB Bear, the original nogari beer house that opened in November 1980.

**Q: What is nogari and what does it cost?**

**A:** Dried juvenile pollack, served as a chewy salty snack to eat while drinking. Roughly ₩1,000–1,500 a plate, with draft beer around ₩3,000–4,000, though prices vary by shop.

**Q: When should I go?**

**A:** Early evening, ideally a weekday between 17:00 and 18:00, or right at opening on a weekend. You will get a seat and you will see the district change from work to drinking, which is the most distinctive thing about it.

**Q: Why are there bars in an industrial area?**

**A:** Because the industry came first. Euljiro was Korea's largest printing district, printing runs to deadlines on night shifts, and workers wanted a cheap drink near the presses when they finished. Nogari Alley grew from that, and Seoul designated it a Future Heritage site in 2015.

**Q: Is Euljiro being redeveloped?**

**A:** Parts of it, and it has been contested for years. Redevelopment plans advancing from around 2014 and a further project approved in 2018 have put much of the old frontage under pressure. Visit for the district rather than for a specific shop, since individual places change often.

## The One Thing to Remember

**Go at six in the evening, not at two in the afternoon.**

Euljiro's appeal is not a bar or a photograph. It is watching a district of workshops and paper wholesalers close its shutters and put tables out in front of them — the same street doing a completely different job an hour later.

The bars are there because the work was there. Walk the working streets first and the alley stops being retro decoration and starts being the actual history of the block.
