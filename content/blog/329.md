By the end of [Part 2](/blog/328), BTS had done almost everything a group can do on the Billboard charts: seven No. 1 singles, a UN podium, an organized global fanbase that could out-vote pop's biggest names. Then, at close to the peak of all of it, the group did something no comparable Western pop act has ever had to do. They stopped, one by one, to become soldiers.

This is not a metaphor and not a scandal. It's how South Korea's mandatory military service law works, and it applies to BTS the same way it applies to almost every other able-bodied Korean man.

## Why Global Superstars Had to Enlist

South Korea has required most able-bodied men to complete around 18 to 21 months of military service since the 1950s, a policy tied directly to the country's unresolved state of war with North Korea. There is no wealth exemption and, ordinarily, no fame exemption. As BTS's fame grew into the billions of dollars in economic impact, a real legislative debate opened over whether they should be treated differently. In December 2020, lawmakers passed an amendment — informally called the "BTS law" — that let popular musicians honored with the Order of Cultural Merit defer enlistment from age 28 to 30. That bought time, not exemption.

The debate didn't stop there. By 2022, with the deferment window closing, a genuine national argument broke out over whether BTS should be excused from service entirely, given their documented economic and diplomatic value. Public polling at the time found a majority — around 61 percent — in favor of exempting them, against roughly a third opposed.

The two sides of that argument are worth understanding on their own terms, because they explain why Korea took this seriously enough to debate in its National Assembly rather than settling it quietly. The case for exemption was straightforward: BTS's own documented economic impact ran into the tens of billions of dollars, and supporters argued that cultural diplomacy and soft power of that scale was itself a form of national service. The case against it, made most directly by South Korea's own Defense Ministry, was about fairness — that extending a special exemption to pop stars would be difficult to justify to every ordinary citizen, and every other athlete or public figure, who had already served without one. South Korea's mandatory service system runs on the principle that the obligation applies regardless of individual achievement, and officials were unwilling to be the ones who broke that principle for entertainers, however globally significant. In the end, no blanket exemption was granted, and more importantly, BTS did not spend their remaining leverage fighting for one. Jin, the oldest member, enlisted first, on December 13, 2022. J-Hope followed in April 2023. SUGA enlisted that September. RM, Jimin, V, and Jung Kook enlisted together in December 2023, completing the group's full transition into uniform.

Only days before Jin's enlistment, the group had been standing in the Oval Office. On May 31, 2022, BTS met with President Biden at the White House to discuss anti-Asian hate crimes and inclusion — a diplomatic-level meeting, not a promotional stop, that showed exactly how far the group's platform had grown. Weeks later, that platform went on pause. The same seven men who had just discussed policy with a sitting US president reported for duty like any other conscript.

## The Two-Year Question

For a group whose entire modern rise had been built on momentum — constant releases, constant fan engagement, an audience trained to expect near-continuous presence — a multi-year gap with all seven members simultaneously out of full-time promotion was a real risk, not a scheduling footnote. Comparable Western pop acts have never faced an equivalent mandatory pause at this scale. The honest open question, one that music trade press asked directly at the time, was whether an audience built on momentum would still be there on the other side.

By Korea.net's reporting, RM and V were discharged on June 10, 2025, Jimin and Jung Kook on June 11, and SUGA completed his service on June 21, 2025 — completing the reunion of all seven members. For the first time since late 2022, BTS existed again as a full group with no one in uniform.

## The Comeback Had to Answer the Question Immediately

It did, and it did so in public. On March 21, 2026, BTS held their first full-group concert since reuniting: "BTS The Comeback Live: Arirang," staged outdoors at Gwanghwamun Plaza in central Seoul. It wasn't a small venue booked cautiously to test demand. It was one of Seoul's most visible public squares, secured with the kind of crowd-control infrastructure — police lines, medical tents, marked pedestrian routes — that only genuinely mass-scale events require.

The music answered the question too. *Arirang*, BTS's fifth full-group studio album, followed, led by the single "Swim." On the Hot 100 chart dated April 4, 2026, "Swim" debuted at No. 1 — BTS's seventh Hot 100 chart-topper, arriving nearly three years after the members had scattered into separate military units. An audience that had been asked to simply wait, with no new full-group music and no touring, still moved fast enough to put a comeback single straight to No. 1. That is a much harder thing to manufacture than a first hit ever was.

A world tour supporting *Arirang* followed the album, running from April 2026 into 2027. On July 17, 2026, the tour played Stade de France outside Paris — a full stadium, all seven members visible on the round center stage under purple light, surrounded on every side by a crowd that filled the pitch and every tier above it.

## Two Days Later, MetLife Stadium

That Paris show was not a warm-up act for a separate story. It was the same tour, the same album cycle, and — two days later — the same seven men who walked onto the field at MetLife Stadium for the FIFA World Cup Final's first-ever halftime show, the scene [Part 1](/blog/327) of this series opened with. Madonna, Justin Bieber, and Shakira shared that stage with them. Post Malone had opened the day's festivities separately, at the pregame ceremony. BTS's slot lasted about two minutes.

Read against everything in this series, those two minutes stop being just a booking coup. They're the answer to two separate open questions the group had faced, back to back, in less than a decade: could seven trainees from a nearly bankrupt label ever break into an industry controlled by three giant companies — and could that same group, after a mandatory multi-year absence at the height of their career, still command a stage that size on the other side of it. Both times, across completely different kinds of pressure, the answer came out the same.

## What the Whole Arc Actually Adds Up To

The Hyundai Research Institute's oft-cited estimate — that BTS contributed close to $50 billion to South Korea's economy across their first decade, at points equivalent to roughly 0.3 percent of national GDP and comparable to a major national airline — gets repeated so often it risks becoming background noise. It's worth restating plainly, at the end of this series rather than the start: that figure describes the compounding effect of a small, broke label's one bet on a teenage rapper, sustained through public criticism, built without a marketing budget large enough to compete with the country's biggest entertainment companies, interrupted by mandatory military service that could easily have ended the whole thing quietly — and it didn't.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Why did BTS have to do military service if they're so famous?**

South Korea requires almost all able-bodied men to complete mandatory military service, with no standard exemption for wealth or fame. A 2020 law let musicians with the Order of Cultural Merit defer enlistment from 28 to 30, but that was a delay, not an exemption, and no blanket exemption was ultimately granted despite public debate.

**Q: Did BTS ever get an official exemption from military service?**

No. A 2020 law let them defer enlistment from age 28 to 30, and a real public debate followed in 2022 over full exemption (roughly 61 percent of one poll favored it), but South Korea's Defense Ministry held that exempting entertainers would be unfair to ordinary citizens and other public figures who had already served without one. No blanket exemption was ever granted, and all seven members enlisted.

**Q: When did each BTS member enlist?**

Jin enlisted first, December 13, 2022. J-Hope followed in April 2023, and SUGA enlisted that September. RM, Jimin, V, and Jung Kook enlisted together in December 2023.

**Q: When were all seven members reunited?**

By June 2025. Korea.net reported RM and V discharged June 10, Jimin and Jung Kook June 11, and SUGA completing his service June 21, 2025.

**Q: What was BTS's first concert after military service?**

"BTS The Comeback Live: Arirang," held at Gwanghwamun Plaza in Seoul on March 21, 2026, ahead of their fifth full-group album, *Arirang*.

**Q: Did BTS's audience actually come back after the hiatus?**

Yes, measurably. "Swim," the lead single from *Arirang*, debuted at No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100 (chart dated April 4, 2026) — BTS's seventh Hot 100 No. 1, arriving directly off a multi-year gap in full-group promotion.
