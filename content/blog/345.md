There is a bun in Korean convenience stores that outsells almost everything around it, and most visitors walk straight past it because it looks unremarkable in its plastic tray.

**Yonsei Cream Bread (연세우유 크림빵)** launched in January 2022 as a collaboration between the convenience store chain **CU** and **Yonsei Milk**, the dairy brand run out of Yonsei University. In **April 2026** it crossed **100 million units sold** — reaching that number faster than any product in CU's history. That works out to roughly **64,500 buns a day**, or about **45 every minute**, continuously, for four years and three months.

It now accounts for roughly **40.8% of CU's entire dessert revenue**. One product line, four out of every ten dessert won.

The reason is simple and slightly absurd: **about 80% of the bun's total weight is cream.** It is barely a bread product. It is a cream delivery system with a thin bread shell, and once you cut one open, the sales figures stop being surprising.

## Why It Is a CU Exclusive

This is the first thing to understand, because it determines where you have to walk.

Yonsei Cream Bread is not a general Korean bakery product you can pick up anywhere. It is a **collaboration product sold only through CU**, one of Korea's three largest convenience store chains. GS25, 7-Eleven, and Emart24 do not carry it, and neither do bakeries or supermarkets.

CU stores are marked with a purple and green sign and are extremely common — in any Seoul neighbourhood you are rarely more than a few minutes from one. But if you are standing in a GS25 wondering why you cannot find the famous cream bun, that is why.

The exclusivity is also the business logic. A product that pulls 40.8% of a chain's dessert revenue is a reason to choose that chain, and that is precisely what CU bought.

## What Is Actually Inside

![A packaged Yonsei red bean cream bread in its clear convenience store tray with the Korean label visible.](/assets/images/posts/345/yonsei-cream-bread-packaged.jpg)

*How you find it on the shelf — a clear tray, a Korean label, and a diagram of the cross-section. Photo by 이분의일디자인, CC BY 2.0 KR.*

The construction is the whole product.

A soft, mild, faintly sweet bread shell is split and packed with fresh cream until the cream, not the bread, is the dominant ingredient by weight. Korean coverage puts that share at **about 80%**. The bread is deliberately understated — it exists to hold the cream and to give you something to bite.

That ratio explains several things at once:

- **It is heavier than it looks.** Pick one up and it has real weight for its size.
- **It must be refrigerated.** This is a chilled product in the cold case, not a shelf-stable packaged bread.
- **It does not travel well.** Warm cream separates and the texture collapses. This is not a souvenir.
- **It is genuinely rich.** One is a dessert, not a snack. Two in a row is a mistake most people make exactly once.

The variety determines what else joins the cream. The red bean version adds a core of sweet red bean paste through the middle, which cuts the dairy with something earthier.

## The 39 Varieties, and Which One to Actually Buy

CU has released **39 varieties** since launch, adding roughly one new flavour every two months. That rotation is deliberate — it is what keeps a four-year-old product feeling like a new release, and it is why the lineup you find in July 2026 will not be the lineup a guide described last year.

Recent Korean sales rankings put the order as:

1. **Milk (우유)** — the original and still the best seller
2. **Chocolate (초코)**
3. **Matcha (말차)**
4. **Yellow cheese (황치즈)**
5. **Corn (옥수수)**

**If it is your first one, buy milk.** It is the version the product was built around, it is the cleanest expression of the cream-to-bread ratio, and it is the one that will be in stock. Treat the seasonal flavours as a second visit, not a first.

That yellow cheese entry at number four is worth noticing. **황치즈** — a sweet-savoury yellow cheese flavour — has been running through the entire Korean snack market rather than staying in one category. ## Eat It Frozen — This Is Not a Trick

Here is the part that no English-language page explains, and it changes the product completely.

Koreans freeze it. The habit is common enough to have its own shorthand, **얼먹** (*eol-meok*), a contraction of 얼려 먹기 — "freeze-and-eat." It is a standard enough search in Korea that it appears as an autocomplete suggestion on the product's own name.

What happens is straightforward. Freezing turns the fresh cream from soft and rich into something closer to **ice cream**, while the bread shell stays chewy rather than going hard. The result is a different dessert from the same package, and many Koreans prefer it that way — particularly in summer.

How to do it:

- **Freeze it in the packaging**, flat, for a few hours
- **Do not thaw it fully.** Take it out five to ten minutes early. Rock-solid is unpleasant; slightly softened at the edges is the target
- **It works best with the cream-forward varieties** — milk, chocolate — where the filling is the point

If you are staying somewhere with a freezer, buy two: eat one chilled, freeze one. That comparison, done back to back, is the actual experience of this product, and it costs about ₩6,000.

## When to Skip It

Being honest about this saves you money and a bad first impression.

- **Skip it if you do not like heavy dairy.** There is no version of this product that is light. The cream is the point, and 80% by weight is not a figure that leaves room for restraint.
- **Skip it as a souvenir, completely.** It is chilled, cream-filled, and short-dated. It will not survive a flight, and it will not survive an afternoon in a warm bag either.
- **Skip a tray that looks squashed or has separated cream visible at the seam.** These are handled products in soft packaging; a mistreated one is genuinely worse.
- **Skip buying four flavours at once on day one.** Richness compounds fast. Buy one, decide honestly whether you want a second, then expand.
- **If the CU near you is out of the milk variety, wait rather than substituting.** Stock rotates constantly and the seasonal flavours are not the same experience.

## Why This Product Explains Korean Convenience Stores

Step back and Yonsei Cream Bread is a fairly precise illustration of how Korean convenience stores actually work, which is different from how they work almost anywhere else.

**They are product developers, not just shelf space.** CU did not stock someone else's hit — it built one with a partner brand and kept it exclusive.

**Rotation is the business model.** Thirty-nine varieties in four years is not indecision, it is a deliberate engine for keeping a mature product in the news cycle and on social media.

**Dessert is a serious category.** A single product line taking 40.8% of dessert revenue only makes sense in a retail culture where people buy dessert at a convenience store as a normal daily act.

**Chilled fresh food is the differentiator.** The cold case — lunch boxes, sandwiches, cream buns — is what separates a Korean convenience store from a Western one, and it is why the fresh case is where you should be looking.

## FAQ About Yonsei Cream Bread

**Q: Where can I buy Yonsei Cream Bread?**

**A:** CU convenience stores only. It is an exclusive collaboration between CU and Yonsei Milk, so GS25, 7-Eleven, Emart24, supermarkets, and bakeries do not carry it. Look for the purple and green CU sign and check the refrigerated case.

**Q: How much does Yonsei Cream Bread cost?**

**A:** Roughly ₩2,600 to ₩3,000 as of July 2026, depending on the variety. Milk and red bean sit at the lower end, corn and limited-edition collaborations at the upper end. Check the shelf label, since the lineup rotates.

**Q: Why is it so popular in Korea?**

**A:** The construction. About 80% of the bun's weight is fresh cream rather than bread, which makes it far richer than it looks. It passed 100 million units sold in April 2026 — roughly 45 buns a minute since its January 2022 launch — and accounts for about 40.8% of CU's total dessert revenue.

**Q: Should I eat it frozen?**

**A:** Try it at least once. Freezing is a mainstream Korean habit for this product, known as 얼먹 (freeze-and-eat), because it turns the fresh cream into something close to ice cream while the bread stays chewy. Take it out of the freezer five to ten minutes before eating rather than letting it thaw completely.

**Q: How many flavours are there?**

**A:** Thirty-nine varieties have been released since launch, with roughly one new flavour every two months. Only a handful are on the shelf at any given time. Recent sales rankings put milk first, then chocolate, matcha, yellow cheese, and corn.

**Q: Can I bring it home as a souvenir?**

**A:** No. It is a chilled, cream-filled, short-dated product that does not export and will not survive a flight. If you want Korean sweets to take home, buy shelf-stable packaged snacks instead.

## The One Thing to Remember

If you take one fact from this guide: **it is CU-only, and about 80% of it is cream.**

Everything else follows. That ratio is why it is refrigerated, why it is heavier than it looks, why it does not travel, why freezing it turns it into a different dessert, and why one product line took four out of every ten dessert won at a national convenience store chain.

Buy the milk one, eat it cold, then freeze the second.
