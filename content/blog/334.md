Here's what surprises most people about **pet death in Korea**: by default, it's still legally classified the same as household trash. A companion animal's body is, unless you take a specific extra step, "생활폐기물" — general living waste — meant to go into a designated municipal garbage bag. That's not a loophole or an oversight. It's the actual legal starting point in a country where roughly 15 million people, nearly 30% of the population, now own a pet.

The gap between that legal default and how people actually feel about their pets is exactly why Korea's registered pet funeral industry has grown from just 7 businesses in 2013 to nearly 90 today. This isn't a story about quirky Korean customs. It's a real, fast-growing, now-regulated industry that exists precisely because the law hasn't caught up with how Koreans actually experience losing a pet.

## What's Actually Legal — and What Isn't

Korea began formally registering animal funeral businesses (동물장묘업) starting January 2008, under an Animal Protection Act amendment. A registered facility can offer any combination of: an animal-exclusive funeral hall, a crematorium, a drying facility, an alkaline hydrolysis facility, or an ossuary. Registration happens with the local city or county government.

**What's not legal is doing it yourself outside that system.** Burying a pet in a mountain, a public park, or even your own backyard — as opposed to using a registered facility or the municipal waste-bag route — carries real fines, reported in the range of roughly ₩50,000 to ₩1,000,000 depending on the violation. Self-cremation is explicitly described as prohibited. And if the pet was a registered dog (registration is mandatory for dogs in Korea, optional for cats), the owner has 30 days to report the death and deregister it, or face a fine up to ₩500,000.

There's also a real legal contradiction sitting underneath all of this: under **Article 98 of Korea's Civil Act**, animals are still classified as "things" — property, not sentient beings — regardless of how the culture around them has moved on. A 2021 Ministry of Justice proposal to change that has stalled in the National Assembly. Culturally, a pet is family. Legally, it's still an object. The funeral industry exists in the space between those two facts.

## From 7 Businesses to Nearly 90 in a Decade

The growth curve here is genuinely steep, and it's independently confirmed on Korea's live national animal-registry site: **7 registered animal funeral businesses in 2013, 44 by 2019, 57 by 2020, and 87 as of this year.** That's real infrastructure being built to meet real demand, not a niche curiosity.

The spending side backs this up. According to the 2025 Korea Pet Report from KB Financial Group's Management Research Institute, the average pet-funeral expenditure hit **₩463,000 in 2025 — up 22% from 2023.** Korea's registered dog and cat population reached **3.28 million in 2023** (dogs make up nearly all of that, since cat registration is optional), while the broader pet-owning population — 15.46 million people, 5.91 million households — has kept climbing alongside it.

## What Actually Happens at a Pet Funeral

The real process, consistent across multiple providers, looks like this: pickup and transport of the body (often available 24/7), an optional memorial or viewing ceremony, cremation — individual or communal, with individual costing noticeably more — and then a choice of what happens to the ashes.

Individual cremation for a small pet under 5kg typically starts around **₩200,000 to ₩250,000**. From there, owners can choose a simple urn, a biodegradable hanji (traditional Korean paper) ash pouch, or something more specific to Korea's pet funeral industry: **memorial stones** pressed from the cremated remains into small, polished, bead-like keepsakes — marketed under names like "Lucete" by providers such as Pet Forest, one of the country's established funeral brands, operating three locations since first registering in December 2016. Some providers also offer cremation jewelry, following the same idea as human memorial diamonds.

**A genuinely interesting sign of the industry maturing**: Skypet, a pet funeral brand, operates under **Boram Sangjo** — one of Korea's largest traditional human funeral conglomerates. When an established mutual-aid funeral company builds a pet-funeral division and it wins industry awards, that's a clear signal this has moved well past a scrappy startup category.

The cremation-method preference itself has also been shifting quickly. Where at-home burial used to be the default choice for many owners, formal individual or communal cremation has become the more common route in recent years, tracking the same direction as the business-count growth above — fewer backyard burials, more registered facilities actually being used the way the 2008 law intended.

## The Word Change That Explains Everything

The single best clue to how Korea's relationship with pets has shifted is a word swap: **반려동물** ("companion animal," literally "life-companion animal") has steadily replaced **애완동물** ("pet" — closer to "plaything animal") in official government usage since the 2000s. The term itself has a specifically documented origin: it was coined at a 1983 international symposium in Vienna, held by the Austrian Academy of Sciences to honor ethologist Konrad Lorenz's 80th birthday — meaning Korea imported not just a new word, but a whole framework for thinking about what an animal is to a household.

That shift shows up in real numbers, not just vocabulary. A 2025 survey by KB Financial Group's Management Research Institute (1,000 pet owners, ages 20-69) found that **83.2% of pet owners reported depression-like symptoms** after losing a pet, and **16.5% said the grief lasted over a year.** One pet owner, quoted under a pseudonym in Korea JoongAng Daily's coverage: *"I always expected a proper farewell… but it's beyond what I had imagined, this excessive weight of loss."* Park Eun-gyeong, director of a Pet Loss Counseling Center, put the mismatch plainly: *"Despite pets dying old, the bereavement is often felt the same as losing their babies,"* while also noting a real stigma still exists — *"There are still stereotypes that seeking psychological support for a pet is excessive."*

## The Mobile Cremation Gray Zone

Not everything about this industry is fully settled. A 2019 startup tried to launch **mobile cremation vehicles** — vans that could travel to a pet owner's home rather than requiring a trip to a fixed facility — and applied for a regulatory-sandbox exemption, since Korea's registration system was written around physical "facilities," not vehicles. The sandbox committee rejected the exemption that July, citing a failure to secure demonstration sites, and the responsible ministry described itself as still "continuously gathering opinions" on how to build a proper registration system for mobile services.

That means mobile pet cremation sits in a real regulatory gray zone even now, several years later. Despite that, at least one Seoul-based provider markets mobile cremation vehicles as an active service tied to a district-level discount program — a genuine tension between what's fully legally settled and what's already operating in practice. If you're a pet owner actually navigating this system, it's worth confirming a provider's registration status directly rather than assuming every "we come to you" option carries the same legal footing as a registered fixed facility.

## What Overseas Readers Should Actually Take From This

If you're not planning to use any of this yourself, the more interesting takeaway is what the industry's growth curve says about Korea more broadly. A country often associated abroad with fast urbanization and a famously demanding work culture has, in parallel, built out a fairly sophisticated regulatory and commercial infrastructure around an emotional need — pet grief — that a lot of other countries still handle informally or not at all. The 2008 registration law predates similar formal systems in a number of other countries, and the live government registry tracking every licensed provider is a level of official record-keeping that most pet-owning nations don't bother with for this specific industry.

There's also a genuinely useful practical lesson buried in the legal-versus-cultural gap described above: **whenever a culture visibly moves faster than its laws, a real commercial industry tends to grow directly in that gap.** Korea's pet funeral sector is a clean, well-documented example of exactly that pattern, and it's one reason the sector is being watched closely by market researchers — one industry report projects the *global* pet funeral services market to grow from roughly $2.11 billion in 2025 to $3.42 billion by 2030, with the Asia-Pacific region, Korea and Japan specifically named as cultural leaders, growing fastest of all.

## Why This Matters Beyond Korea

None of this is unique to Korea in feeling — grief over a pet is universal. What's specifically Korean is the speed and shape of the institutional response: a formal registration system since 2008, a live government registry tracking every licensed provider, a KB-Financial-scale survey putting hard numbers on the grief, and now an established funeral conglomerate treating pet death as a real business line rather than a niche.

The honest reading is that Korea's legal system (pets as property) and Korea's cultural reality (pets as family) are still catching up to each other, and the funeral industry's rapid growth — from 7 businesses to 87 in a little over a decade — is the visible evidence of that gap closing in real time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is it legal to bury a pet in your backyard in Korea?**
No. Burying pet remains outside a registered animal funeral facility or the designated municipal waste-bag system is not legal and can carry a fine, reported in a range of roughly ₩50,000 to ₩1,000,000 depending on the specific violation.

**Q: How much does a pet funeral cost in Korea?**
Individual cremation for a small pet typically starts around ₩200,000 to ₩250,000. The 2025 Korea Pet Report found the average total pet-funeral expenditure reached ₩463,000 in 2025, up 22% from 2023.

**Q: How many pet funeral businesses are registered in Korea?**
As of the most recent check of Korea's National Animal Protection Information System registry, 87 animal funeral businesses are registered nationwide — up from just 7 in 2013.

**Q: What happens to a pet's ashes in Korea?**
Owners can choose a standard urn, a biodegradable traditional hanji paper pouch, a columbarium placement at the facility, or a memorial keepsake stone pressed from the cremated remains — a service actively marketed by established Korean pet funeral providers.

**Q: Why is a pet's legal status in Korea confusing?**
Under Article 98 of Korea's Civil Act, animals are still legally classified as property ("things"), even though official terminology shifted decades ago from "pet" (애완동물) to "companion animal" (반려동물) to reflect how families actually think of them. A 2021 proposal to change the legal classification has stalled in the National Assembly.

## The Bottom Line

Korea's pet funeral industry didn't appear because of some unusual cultural quirk — it grew because millions of households started treating pets as family members faster than the law could keep up, and a real, regulated industry stepped into that gap. Seven registered businesses in 2013 becoming 87 today isn't a curiosity statistic. It's a decade of grief finally getting somewhere to go.
