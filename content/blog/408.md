Open the freezer in any Korean convenience store and one product is unmistakable: a flat, crisp wafer pressed into the shape of a **carp**, filled with vanilla ice cream and whole red bean.

It is **붕어싸만코** (*Bungeo Samanco*), Binggrae has made it since 1990, and the name is not a word. It is a **shortened sales pitch**: **싸고 많고** — *ssa-go man-ko*, "it's cheap and there's a lot of it." Say the two together fast and you get 싸만코.

That is a startlingly direct thing to call a product, and it is the most useful fact about it, because the whole design follows from it. Here is what is inside, why it is a fish, and what it costs in 2026.

## The Name Is a Price Claim, Not a Word

Korean brand names often do this — describe the product's virtue rather than name the thing — but few are as blunt.

**싸고** means "it's cheap." **많고** means "and there's a lot." Binggrae's own account is that the brand contracted the phrase 싸고 많고 into **싸만코**, which is the sort of naming that only works in a market where price is the headline feature and everybody knows it.

That was 1976, and the product then was **square**. A flat ice monaka — vanilla ice cream and red bean between two crisp wafers — with no fish anywhere near it.

**붕어**, the carp, arrived on **1 March 1990**, fourteen years later. Binggrae took the existing sandwich and pressed the wafer into the shape of a 붕어빵, Korea's winter street pastry, which meant the product suddenly carried a whole set of associations it had not earned: cold weather, street stalls, red bean, childhood.

It worked so well that the shape became the product. Ask a Korean about 싸만코 and most will describe a fish.

## What Is Actually In It

Three things, and the middle one is what separates it from every Western ice cream sandwich.

**The shell is a monaka wafer.** 모나카 is a crisp, almost dry wafer shell — the format comes from Japanese confectionery, where a monaka is traditionally a wafer sandwich around red bean paste. It is not a cone and not a soft cookie: it shatters, and it stays crisp because it is dry enough not to draw water out of the ice cream quickly.

**The filling is vanilla ice cream.** Plain, sweet, unremarkable on purpose. It is the neutral layer.

**The red bean is 통팥 — whole beans, not paste.** This is the detail that matters. A syrup of intact red beans runs through the ice cream rather than a smooth 앙금 filling, so you get texture and a slightly savoury, earthy note against the vanilla. Anyone who has decided they dislike red bean because of a dense paste-filled bun should try it in this form before deciding.

Put together it is closer to a Korean-Japanese dessert grammar than to an ice cream bar: crisp, cold, bean, restrained sweetness.

**떡붕어싸만코 is the one to look for.** Adding 떡 to a frozen product is a genuinely difficult thing to do — rice cake goes hard and unpleasant when frozen unless the formulation is right — and the fact that Binggrae ships one that stays chewy is the most technically interesting thing about the line.

## How to Find It in a Korean Freezer

Convenience-store ice cream in Korea lives in a chest freezer near the door, and it is not
sorted the way a Western freezer aisle is. Bars, cones, cups and 모나카 sandwiches are mixed
together in open bins, so you are scanning printed packets face-on rather than reading a shelf.

Three things make 붕어싸만코 easy to pick out. The packet is a **flat rectangle**, not a cone
sleeve or a bar wrapper, because the product inside is a sandwich. The artwork shows the fish
in profile with a red-bean-coloured seam along its belly. And the brand block reads 빙그레
above 붕어싸만코 in a heavy rounded face that has barely changed in decades.

If the shop has a 아이스크림 sign and rows of chest freezers rather than a single one by the
till, you are in a discount ice-cream shop, and the price is a different conversation
entirely — see below.

**One practical warning.** Because these bins are open and constantly opened further, product
near the top gets softer than product at the bottom. For a wafer item that matters more than
it does for a bar: dig. A packet from the bottom of the bin has spent less time near the warm
edge, and the monaka shell is the part that suffers first.

## What It Costs, and Why the Answer Is Three Numbers

![Chart of Samanco pricing across three Korean retail formats: discount ice-cream shop lowest, supermarket or 30-pack in the middle, convenience store highest at 2,200 won.](/assets/images/posts/408/samanco-price-by-shop.jpg)
*EpicKor chart.*

The convenience-store price rose to **₩2,200 in January 2023**, a 10% increase. That is the number you will pay standing in a GS25 at midnight, and it is the number that makes visitors raise an eyebrow at a product whose name literally claims to be cheap.

It is also the worst price in the country for it.

Korea has a retail format that barely exists elsewhere: the **아이스크림 할인점**, a shop that sells nothing but frozen confectionery at a steep discount, usually from chest freezers, usually with hand-written prices. The same fish costs a fraction there. Supermarkets and online 30-packs sit in between.

**So the honest answer to "how much is it" is: it depends entirely on which door you walk through**, and Koreans know this, which is why nobody buys ice cream at a convenience store on purpose. If you see a shop with 아이스크림 written large and a lot of freezers, that is the one.

## Honest Notes

**The name has aged badly, and that is interesting rather than damning.** A product called "cheap and plentiful" now lists at ₩2,200 in the most convenient channel. Korean coverage of ice-cream price rises reaches for this product precisely because the name makes the increase quotable.

**It is a wafer, so texture is time-sensitive.** Eat it soon after buying. A monaka shell that has been sitting half-thawed and refrozen in a shop freezer goes chewy, and a chewy monaka is a sad object.

**Red bean is not optional in the original.** If you dislike 팥 you want the 초코 크런치 version, not the classic — do not buy the classic hoping the bean is a background note. It is the point.

**Sales have come down from the peak.** Around ₩36 billion in 2012 and ₩34.5 billion by 2015, in a Korean frozen market that has since fragmented enormously. This is a heritage product holding a position, not a growth story.

## FAQ

**Q: What does Samanco mean?**
**A:** It is a contraction of **싸고 많고** — "cheap, and there's a lot of it." Binggrae's own account is that the sales phrase was shortened into the brand name. 붕어 in front simply means carp.

**Q: Is it a cone?**
**A:** No. It is an **아이스 모나카** — a crisp wafer sandwich in a fish shape. Taiyaki-style ice cream cones exist and are a different product.

**Q: What is the red filling?**
**A:** 통팥 syrup — whole red beans rather than smooth paste, running through vanilla ice cream.

**Q: Which version should I buy?**
**A:** 떡붕어싸만코 if you want the most interesting one; the rice cake stays chewy from frozen, which is harder to engineer than it sounds. The classic if you want the 1990 product.

**Q: Why is the price so different between shops?**
**A:** Korean frozen confectionery has no fixed retail price. Convenience stores list around ₩2,200; 아이스크림 할인점 discount shops sell the same item far below that.
