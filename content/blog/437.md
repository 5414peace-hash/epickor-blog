Search for a Korean exfoliating towel and the listings will hand you a system. Pink is gentle. Green is standard. Blue is aggressive. One Amazon listing puts it right in the product name: **"Blue — Strong"**.

It reads like a manufacturer's grading scale, so people buy on it.

**Korean sources do not have that scale.** In Korea the cloth came in green because green is what the first batch was, and the other colours arrived later as colours. The variable that actually decides whether this thing polishes your skin or injures it is not printed on the packet at all — **it is how long you sat in the water first.**

Here is the whole object: what it is, where it came from, what the colours actually indicate, and the frequency rule that Korean dermatologists are unusually blunt about.

## Where it came from, and why it is called Italy

The origin is unusually well documented for a household object, and it is not a tradition. It is an accident with a date.

In **1967**, a textile company called **한일직물 (Hanil Textile)**, run by **김원조 (Kim Won-jo)**, was operating in **초읍동, Busan** — the site of what is now Changgok Market. The company had imported a batch of **viscose rayon fabric from Italy** intended for clothing. It arrived far too coarse to wear, and it could not simply be written off.

Someone at the company suggested rubbing it on skin.

That is the entire invention. The name **이태리타월 — "Italy towel"** — refers to where the *fabric* came from, not to any Italian bathing habit. The cloth is a Korean product with an Italian material bill.

One footnote worth knowing, because you will run into it: **a lot of English-language material dates this to 1962** and credits a different person. Even the description on the Wikimedia photograph used further down this page says 1962. **Korean and English encyclopaedic entries now both give 1967 and Kim Won-jo**, and that is the attribution used here.

## The colour question, answered

![Chart comparing colour claims: English listings say pink is softest, blue coarsest, green standard, with one Amazon listing named "Blue — Strong"; Korean sources say green was simply the original with yellow, red, white and blue arriving later and no grade attached, and Korean retailers sell five-colour packs as colours; what actually changes the feel is soak time, weave and material blend, and pressure.](/assets/images/posts/437/italy-towel-colour-claim.jpg)
*The same cloth, described two different ways in two markets. Chart by EpicKor.*

This is the single most-searched thing about these towels in English, so it deserves a direct answer rather than a hedge.

**In English-language retail, colour is presented as a coarseness grade.** Pink for sensitive skin, green for normal, blue for heavy-duty. It is stated confidently and consistently enough that it has become common knowledge.

**In Korean sources, colour is not a grade.** The Korean-language description of the product is that green was the original and remains the most common, with yellow, red, white and occasionally blue appearing later. Korean retailers routinely sell **five-colour packs — red, green, pink, yellow, blue — as a colour assortment**, which is not how you would sell a set of graded abrasives.

So the honest guidance is this: **colour is cosmetic unless the particular seller explicitly states a grade.** If a listing says "Blue — Strong", believe that seller about that product. Do not carry the code across to a Korean shop, where a shelf of colours is just a shelf of colours.

**Green is not "medium".** Green is what Korea started with.

### What actually changes how it feels

Three things, in order of how much they matter:

1. **How long you soaked.** This is not a minor preparation step; it is the technique. A properly soaked stratum corneum comes away readily. An unsoaked one does not, so you press harder, and pressing harder is how people hurt themselves.
2. **The weave and material.** Korean listings specify **비스코스 100%** — pure viscose. Export versions sometimes blend in sisal hemp or nylon, which changes the bite far more than dye does.
3. **How hard you push.** The variable nobody sells you, and the one that decides the outcome.

## The four shapes, and what each is for

The flat rectangle is the classic, but Korean bathrooms usually hold two or three of these, because the flat cloth cannot reach everything.

- **The flat cloth (때수건)** — the original rectangle. Cheapest, most common, and what a Korean bathhouse hands out.
- **The mitt (장갑형)** — the same fabric sewn into a glove so it stays on your hand. This is the form most commonly exported, which is why English listings usually show a mitt when Korean ones show a cloth.
- **The long back towel (등밀이용)** — a strip you hold at both ends and saw across your own back. Daiso's is **비스코스 100%, about 88 × 24cm, ₩2,000** (checked on Daiso's own store, 2 September 2026).
- **The handled scrubber (핸들형)** — a plastic wand with a detachable scrub head, for the same problem when you would rather not saw.

## How to use it

![Chart in three vertical sections: soak first — sit in hot water or shower long enough that skin is genuinely softened, because scrubbing dry skin turns a wash into an injury; once a week at most — Korean dermatologists cap it there, and what comes off is the stratum corneum, so thin, atopic and sensitive skin should skip it and never use it on the face; the back needs a tool — a long 88 by 24cm towel or handled scrubber, both 2,000 won at Daiso, plain cloth 1,000 won.](/assets/images/posts/437/italy-towel-how-to-use.jpg)
*The soak is the technique. The frequency is the safety rule. Chart by EpicKor.*

1. **Soak.** Sit in hot water for ten to fifteen minutes, or take a long hot shower. Korean guidance is explicit that skin softened in water can be scrubbed smooth *without* being irritated. This step is the difference between the two outcomes.
2. **Scrub in short strokes, with light pressure.** Arms, legs, torso. You will see grey rolls come off. That is not dirt — see the next section.
3. **Skip the thin places.** Neck, chest, anywhere the skin is fine. And not the face.
4. **Rinse, then moisturise immediately.** You have just removed the layer that holds water in. Replacing it is not optional.

## How often — the part Korean dermatologists are blunt about

This is where a Korean source says something an English shopping guide will not.

**Korean dermatologists broadly oppose the practice**, and their objection is specific: what people call 때 — the grey rolls — is largely **각질층, the stratum corneum**, the outermost layer of skin. It is not accumulated grime. It is the barrier.

Their warning about over-scrubbing is concrete. Scrub hard or often and the barrier is damaged; the skin looks immediately smooth, then loses water rapidly through the microscopic scratches you just created, which are also entry points for bacteria and irritants.

**The published ceiling is once a week or less.** For thin-skinned areas, atopic skin and sensitive skin, the advice is to skip it entirely and use a gentle chemical exfoliant or simply hands.

That is the honest version, and it does not make the towel useless — it makes it a weekly tool rather than a daily one. Korea's own medical press is where that limit is stated most plainly, which is exactly why it is worth carrying across.

## Having it done for you: 세신

The other way to meet this cloth is to not hold it yourself.

**세신** is the professional scrub performed at Korean bathhouses by a **목욕관리사**, a trained attendant, on a vinyl-covered table. You soak first — the same rule, enforced by the queue — and then a stranger scrubs you thoroughly, efficiently and without ceremony.

What it costs, from Korean reporting: **most bathhouses land around ₩25,000**, with some under ₩20,000; **Gyeonggi Province tends to run ₩30,000–50,000**; large spa facilities sit higher. The recent wave of **1인 세신숍** — private single-person scrub studios — charges roughly **₩50,000–80,000 for a 50–60 minute basic course**, and well past ₩100,000 once massage or steam is added.

One thing worth knowing before you budget: **the women's side is frequently charged more than the men's side for the same service**, a gap Korean newspapers have covered and criticised. Prices are typically negotiated between the attendant and the bathhouse rather than set centrally, so confirm on the day rather than assuming.

## FAQ About Korean Exfoliating Towels

**Q: What do the colours of a Korean exfoliating towel mean?**

In Korea, nothing. Green was the original colour and remains the most common; yellow, red, white and blue came later, and Korean retailers sell mixed-colour packs as an assortment. The pink-soft, green-standard, blue-coarse scale is an English-market convention. If a specific seller states a grade, trust that seller for that product — but do not carry the code into a Korean shop.

**Q: Why is it called an Italy towel if it is Korean?**

Because the fabric was Italian. Hanil Textile in Busan imported viscose rayon from Italy in 1967 for clothing, found it far too rough to wear, and turned it into a bath cloth instead. The name records the material's origin, not a bathing tradition.

**Q: How often should I use one?**

Once a week at most. Korean dermatologists set that ceiling and many argue for less, because what comes off is the stratum corneum — the skin's outer barrier — rather than dirt. Atopic, sensitive and thin-skinned users should skip it.

**Q: Can I use a Korean exfoliating towel on my face?**

No. It is a body tool, and facial skin is exactly the thin skin the frequency warnings are about. Use a gentle facial exfoliant instead.

**Q: What is the difference between the mitt and the flat cloth?**

The fabric is the same; only the sewing differs. The flat rectangle is what Korean bathhouses hand out and what Korean shops mostly stock. The mitt stays on your hand, which is easier for a first-timer, and is the form most often exported — which is why English listings show mitts while Korean ones show cloths.

## Bottom Line

The colour code is the wrong question, honestly asked. It exists because English listings needed a way to differentiate identical cloths, and it stuck.

The Korean version of the same information is simpler and more useful: **the cloth is one thing, and how you use it is three things.** Soak properly, press lightly, and do it about once a week. Do that and this is a ₩1,000 object that does something a much more expensive product cannot. Do the opposite — dry skin, hard pressure, every morning — and you are sanding off the layer that keeps water in your body.

Buy green if you want the one Korea actually uses. Buy the long towel if the real reason you want one is your back. And keep the lotion within reach before you start.
