Open a packet of **Neoguri (너구리)** and something falls out that does not fall out of any other instant noodle: a stiff, dark green square about the size of a large postage stamp. It looks like packaging. A great many people outside Korea have picked it out and binned it.

It is **kelp (다시마)** — a whole piece of it, not a powder — and **Nongshim buys roughly 400 tonnes of it a year** to put one in each bag.

That single component explains almost everything about how this noodle is supposed to be cooked, and it is the reason `neoguri ramen hacks` is a live search with no proper answer written for it. This guide covers what the kelp is for, how Koreans actually cook the bag, the mild-versus-spicy question, the Chapaguri pairing that reached the Oscars, and a straight answer to the recall question people keep searching.

## The Kelp Is the Product

Here is the fact that reframes the whole noodle, and it comes from the Korean side of the record.

When Nongshim developed Neoguri in 1982, it wanted a seafood udon flavour that tasted like a broth rather than a seasoning sachet. The decision it made was unusual then and remains unusual now: **put a whole piece of kelp in the bag** rather than grinding it into the powder.

The kelp comes from **Wando (완도)**, an island county off Korea's south coast that is the country's kelp capital. That relationship has now run for **more than forty years**:

- Nongshim buys **around 400 tonnes of Wando kelp a year**, and announced a **450-tonne** purchase for the 2024 season.
- Between the 1982 launch and 2019 it bought roughly **15,000 tonnes**; cumulative purchasing has since passed about **17,000 tonnes** — the largest kelp buying programme of any Korean food company.

That is not a marketing line. It is a supply contract with a fishing region, renewed every harvest, for a component most instant-noodle brands would have replaced with extract decades ago.

**What the kelp is for:** it releases glutamates as the soup boils, which is the savoury backbone the seafood flavour sits on. It is doing the job that a stock would do in a cooked-from-scratch soup. Removing it does not just lose a garnish, it removes the depth the rest of the packet was formulated around.

**Eat it, or don't — but boil it.** Korean opinion genuinely splits on whether to eat the softened kelp at the end. Plenty of people do. Plenty push it to the side. Nobody takes it out before boiling.

## How Koreans Actually Cook It

These are the adjustments that come up constantly on the Korean web, and they are mostly about one thing: **Neoguri's noodle is thick**, so the rules that work for thin ramyun do not transfer.

### 1. Use less water than the packet says

The packet direction is written for a full, drinkable soup. Most Korean home cooks pull the water back by roughly a **half cup** to get a broth that clings to the fat noodle instead of sliding off it. If you have ever found Neoguri watery, this is why.

### 2. Give the noodles their full time, and no more

The udon-type noodle needs longer than a thin ramyun noodle to hydrate through — but it also goes soft and floury fast once it passes done. Set a timer rather than guessing. If you like it chewy, pull it about thirty seconds early and let the residual heat finish it in the bowl.

### 3. Put the kelp in at the start

In with the water, before the noodles. It needs the whole boil to give anything up.

### 4. Add an egg, but decide which kind

Two different results, and Koreans argue about both:

- **Beaten and stirred in** at the end — clouds the broth, makes it richer and softer.
- **Cracked in whole** and left alone — keeps the broth clear, gives you a yolk to break over the noodles.

For a seafood broth, whole is the more common choice, because a beaten egg mutes the anchovy-kelp edge that is the point of the soup.

### 5. The additions that suit this soup specifically

Neoguri's base is seafood, so it takes seafood additions better than a chili-based ramyun does. Frozen seafood mix, a handful of clams, or a few slices of fish cake (어묵) all belong. **Cheese does not** — it fights the kelp. Save cheese for Buldak.

A splash of something sharp at the end — a little vinegar, or kimchi brine — lifts the broth. This is the one "hack" that circulates in English as well, and it does work.

## Chapaguri: The Half of Neoguri That Went to the Oscars

The single most famous thing Neoguri does is not eaten on its own.

![Nongshim Chapagetti packet, the black-bean noodle that combines with Neoguri to make Chapaguri.](/assets/images/posts/359/chapagetti-nongshim.jpg)

*The other half. Chapagetti is a black-bean sauce noodle with no broth — the opposite construction to Neoguri. Product image: Nongshim.*

**Chapaguri (짜파구리)** is Chapagetti plus Neoguri, cooked together into one pan. It existed as a Korean home hack for years before *Parasite* put it on screen in 2019, where the English subtitles rendered it as **"ram-don."**

The construction, briefly:

1. Boil both noodle blocks **together** in one pot. Neoguri's is thicker, so it sets the timing.
2. **Drain nearly all the water**, keeping only a few tablespoons.
3. Add **Chapagetti's black-bean powder and oil**, plus **about half of Neoguri's soup powder**. All of Neoguri's sachet makes it too salty — this is the step people miss.
4. Stir off the heat until the sauce emulsifies and coats.
5. The film version adds seared sirloin. The home version does not.

The result is a dry, savoury, black-bean noodle with the seafood heat running underneath it. It is genuinely good, and it is the reason `chapagetti and neoguri` is a search term in English at all.

## "Is Neoguri Linked to Cancer?" — The Honest Answer

This gets searched, it deserves a straight answer, and English coverage either ignores it or reprints a 2021 headline without the resolution. Here is the sequence.

**In August 2021, Neoguri exported to Europe was recalled.** European testing found **ethylene oxide** in seasoning from batches produced on **27 January and 3 March 2021**, at **7.4 and 5.0 ppm** against an EU limit of **0.05 ppm**. Ethylene oxide is classified as a group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer. Ireland's Food Safety Authority separately recalled a batch of Shin Kimchi noodle soup, and Thailand's FDA froze shipments while it checked.

**Nongshim's position was that what was detected was 2-chloroethanol (2-CE), not ethylene oxide.** The two are related — 2-CE is a breakdown product — and this is where it becomes a regulatory disagreement rather than a purely factual one: **the EU and Taiwan treat the two as the same substance for limit purposes, while Korea and the United States treat them as different.** So the same laboratory result produces a violation in one jurisdiction and not in another.

**What this means practically:**

- It concerned **specific export batches from early 2021**, not the product line generally, and those batches were withdrawn.
- The limit exceeded was the EU's, which is among the strictest in the world and is set at a level where any detection is effectively a failure.
- The underlying dispute is about **how a residue is classified**, not about whether Nongshim added something.

**What we are not going to tell you** is that this is a non-issue or that it is a scandal. It is a five-year-old regulatory action on identified batches, with a live definitional disagreement between food authorities behind it. If you want current status for your own country, its food safety authority publishes recall notices — that is the source to check, not a food blog, and not this one.

## Halal, and Why People Ask

`neoguri ramen halal` is a common search, and the answer is not uniform across the range.

Nongshim operates halal-certified production for products destined for Muslim-majority markets, and separately sells non-certified versions elsewhere. Certification applies **per product and per production line**, not per brand. A packet bought in Malaysia and a packet bought in a US grocery are not automatically the same specification.

**The reliable method: read the actual packet in your hand.** A certified product carries the certifying body's mark on the packaging. If the pack in front of you does not have one, treat it as not certified, regardless of what a listing elsewhere says.

## FAQ About Neoguri

**Q: What is the green square in the Neoguri packet?**

**A:** A whole piece of dried kelp (다시마) from Wando. It is an ingredient, not packaging. Put it in the water at the start of the boil.

**Q: Should I eat the kelp?**

**A:** Your choice — Koreans genuinely disagree. What is not optional is boiling it, because that is where the soup's savoury depth comes from.

**Q: How spicy is Neoguri?**

**A:** The red bag is moderate and sits below Shin Ramyun. The green bag (순한맛) is very mild. Neoguri's identity is seafood broth rather than heat.

**Q: What is Chapaguri?**

**A:** Chapagetti and Neoguri cooked together and drained into a dry black-bean noodle. It appeared in *Parasite* subtitled as "ram-don." Use all of the Chapagetti sachet but only about half of Neoguri's, or it will be too salty.

**Q: Is Neoguri safe after the 2021 recall?**

**A:** The 2021 action covered identified export batches, which were withdrawn, and rested on a disagreement over whether 2-chloroethanol should be counted as ethylene oxide — the EU and Taiwan say yes, Korea and the US say no. For current status in your country, check your national food safety authority's recall notices.

**Q: Is Neoguri halal?**

**A:** Some production is halal-certified for Muslim-majority markets and some is not. Certification is per product and per line, so read the mark on the packet you are actually holding.

**Q: Why is it called "raccoon dog"?**

**A:** 너구리 is the Korean word for the raccoon dog, and Nongshim's long-running advertising line was "너구리 한 마리 몰고 가세요" — roughly, "take a raccoon home with you." The name is a mascot, not a description of the food.
