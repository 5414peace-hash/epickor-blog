Most English guides to Korea quote a price and leave it there. This one gives you the dates. **One of these increases has not happened yet — Paris Baguette's lands on 25 August 2026** — which means for a few more days there is something you can actually do about it.

## What Has Already Happened

**농심 (Nongshim)** moved first, raising major cup noodle lines by an average of **6%** and snacks by **5.5%** during August. Nongshim is the company behind Shin Ramyun, Chapagetti and Banana Kick, so this is the increase most likely to touch something you were going to buy anyway.

**풀무원 (Pulmuone)** raised 30 items across 7 product groups by an average of **6.7%** on **13 August**. Pulmuone is a tofu, noodle and chilled-foods company rather than a snack brand, so this one shows up in a supermarket basket rather than at a convenience store counter.

## What Changes on 25 August

**파리바게뜨 (Paris Baguette)** announced on 14 August that it would raise **127 products by an average of 5.0%** starting **25 August 2026** — 86 breads plus 41 cakes and desserts. It is the chain's first increase in **a year and six months**, the last one having been in February 2025.

Paris Baguette is the most visible bakery chain in Korea and one of the first Korean food brands most visitors interact with, usually at an airport or a station. If a bakery run is on your list and you are in Korea before the 25th, that is the window.

**삼립 (Samlip)** follows in **September**, raising about **50 breads by roughly 9%** — the steepest of the four increases. Samlip is the packaged-bread company whose products sit on convenience store shelves rather than in a bakery, so this is the one that changes the price of a bread you grab on the way somewhere.

## Why All Four at Once

The companies gave the same reasons, and the underlying numbers are public: **international wheat prices are up 5.8% and corn 3.6%**, on top of higher packaging and logistics costs.

That combination explains the pattern in the table. Bread makers move first and hardest because wheat is most of the input cost — hence Samlip's 9% and Paris Baguette's 127 items. Snack makers move less because a bag of crisps is more air, seasoning and packaging than grain.

**The outlook is the part worth internalising if you are planning a trip.** Korean industry coverage expects cost pressure to persist **into the first half of 2027**, because packaging prices lag the raw-material market on the way back down even when conditions ease. In other words, this is not a one-month blip you can simply wait out.

## Why Korean Price Rises Arrive in Waves

Here is the part that does not translate, and it is the reason four companies moved within three weeks of each other rather than at four unrelated moments.

Paris Baguette's own stated reason for this increase is worth reading closely. The company explained it as **readjusting prices it had previously lowered in order to align with the government's price-stability stance** after flour costs fell. That sentence describes a mechanism most visitors have never heard of.

Korean food prices are not set purely by the market. When consumer prices become politically sensitive, the government applies informal pressure on major food manufacturers to hold or roll back increases, and the companies generally comply — publicly, and framed as participation rather than compliance. It is not price control in the legal sense. There is no statute being enforced. But the effect is real, and Korean consumers understand the ritual perfectly well.

The consequence is that **suppressed increases do not disappear, they queue.** Cost pressure builds while prices are held flat, and when the political weather changes, the backlog releases at once. That is why Korean food price news arrives as a cluster of announcements in the same fortnight rather than as a steady drift, and why a Korean reader seeing four companies move in August 2026 reads it as one event rather than four coincidences.

For a visitor, the practical implication is counter-intuitive: **a long flat stretch in Korean food prices is not evidence of stability.** It may be evidence of a queue. Prices you find quoted from a quiet period can be further from current reality than the length of the gap suggests.

## What This Actually Means If You Are Visiting

Here is the honest version, because it cuts both ways.

**In absolute terms, none of this is much.** A 5% rise on a ₩2,400 castella is ₩100. A 6% rise on a cup noodle is roughly ₩100 to ₩150. If you are on a two-week trip, the entire set of August 2026 increases will cost you the price of one coffee across the whole holiday. Anyone telling you Korea has become expensive because of this month is selling you something.

**What it does change is the mental model.** Korea's reputation among travellers is built on a decade-old idea that convenience store food is trivially cheap. That is now half true. A convenience store meal is still good value against a restaurant, but it is no longer automatically the cheapest hot food available, and the ₩1,000 snack has effectively disappeared.

**And it changes where you buy.** Because the increases hit branded packaged goods hardest, the gap between a convenience store and a supermarket has widened. On the same product, buying the large bag at an E-Mart or Lotte Mart instead of the 67g bag at a GS25 can be **40% cheaper per gram**. That was always true; it is now worth acting on.

## FAQ About Korean Food Prices in 2026

**Q: How much did Korean food prices go up in August 2026?**

**A:** It depends on the company. Pulmuone raised 30 items by an average of 6.7% on 13 August, Nongshim raised cup noodles 6% and snacks 5.5% during the month, Paris Baguette raises 127 items by an average of 5.0% on 25 August, and Samlip raises about 50 breads by roughly 9% in September. There was no single across-the-board increase — four separate company decisions landed within weeks of each other.

**Q: Why are Korean food prices rising?**

**A:** International wheat prices are up 5.8% and corn 3.6%, and packaging and logistics costs have risen alongside them. Bread makers are affected most because grain is the bulk of their input cost, which is why Samlip's roughly 9% is the largest of the four.

**Q: Is it still cheap to eat at a Korean convenience store?**

**A:** Cheaper than a restaurant, yes. Trivially cheap, no longer. The ₩1,000 item has effectively disappeared, a standard snack bag is ₩1,700, and a convenience store lunch box now commonly sits around ₩6,000. It remains good value; it has stopped being the automatic cheapest option.

**Q: Should I buy Korean groceries before the price increases?**

**A:** Only for the Paris Baguette increase on 25 August, and only if you were going anyway. The absolute amounts are small — ₩100 to ₩200 on most items — so this is worth a few minutes of timing, not a change of plans.

**Q: Will Korean food prices keep rising in 2027?**

**A:** Korean industry coverage expects cost pressure to continue into the first half of 2027, because packaging costs come down more slowly than raw material prices even when the underlying market eases. Treat any price you read about Korea, including the ones on this page, as needing a date attached.

## The Bottom Line

Four companies, three weeks, one shared reason. The two increases that have not happened yet — Paris Baguette on **25 August** and Samlip in **September** — are the only ones you can still act on, and only one of them is worth acting on at all.

The larger point is the one worth carrying: prices in Korea now need a date next to them. A figure from a travel guide written two years ago is not slightly out of date, it is wrong. Every price on EpicKor carries the month it was checked, and this is why.
