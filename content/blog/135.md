## The Royal Origin of "Kim"
Kim (김, 金) was associated with Silla's ruling class. In pre-modern systems, royal names carried symbolic capital, so adoption pressure was naturally high.

## The Expansion Phase: How One Name Multiplied
As class barriers shifted in late Joseon, surnames became more widespread. Many families adopted high-status names, including Kim, Lee, and Park, to access social legitimacy.

Key drivers:
1. class transition,
2. administrative registration growth,
3. prestige-based naming incentives.

This is why Kim's share became unusually large.

## Bon-gwan: Why Not All Kims Are Family
The Bon-gwan system identifies ancestral origin (e.g., Gimhae Kim, Gyeongju Kim). Two people with surname Kim can belong to completely different historical lines.

## FAQ
### Are all people named Kim related?
No. Bon-gwan divisions mean many Kims are from different ancestral lines.

### What are the next most common surnames?
Lee and Park follow Kim, then others like Choi and Jung.

### Is asking Bon-gwan considered rude?
In casual settings it can feel personal, but in cultural or family-history conversations it can be acceptable.

### Why is this important for understanding Korea?
Surname systems reveal how class, governance, and identity evolved historically.

## Final Takeaway
The dominance of Kim is not random; it reflects Korean history's intersection of power, aspiration, and identity administration. Once you learn Bon-gwan logic, the "many Kims" question becomes one of the most fascinating doors into Korean civilization.
