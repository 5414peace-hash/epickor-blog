**Modern Korean drinking culture is built around shared food and conversation, but drinking alcohol is not a requirement.** A *hoesik* is a work-team meal, a *cha* is a round or next venue, *anju* is food served with drinks, and *somaek* mixes soju and beer. You can participate with water or a non-alcoholic drink, refuse a shot, skip later rounds and leave safely without failing a cultural test.

Older stories often describe compulsory company dinners, endless rounds and one-shot toasts as fixed Korean rules. Those practices still occur in some groups, but workplaces, generations and managers differ. A good host makes attendance and alcohol optional. A good guest communicates clearly, eats, joins conversation and does not pressure anyone else.

## What a Korean hoesik is in 2026

*Hoesik* literally centers on eating together. In a workplace it can be a team lunch, barbecue dinner, year-end gathering, welcome meal or project celebration. Alcohol may be present, but the useful purpose is social time outside the formal meeting room.

The old stereotype places the most senior manager at the center, with juniors attending because refusal feels risky. Today's reality is uneven. Some teams still hold hierarchical evening dinners; others use lunch, finish after one venue, ask employees what they prefer or rarely organize formal gatherings. Remote work, changing expectations and workplace-harassment awareness have made compulsory excess harder to defend.

Korea's Ministry of Employment and Labor explains that workplace harassment can involve using position or relationship to go beyond the proper scope of work and cause physical or mental suffering or worsen the work environment. Its [workplace-harassment guidance](https://www.moel.go.kr/news/cardinfo/view.do?bbs_seq=20230601346) does not declare every dinner invitation illegal, but it is a useful reminder: a manager's “friendly tradition” can feel very different to a subordinate who cannot safely say no.

*Cha* can also describe rounds in a private night out. A group may move from barbecue to a pub, karaoke room or late-night food. Each move should be a new decision. Agreeing to dinner does not create an obligation to attend every next venue.

## How rounds work without the marathon myth

The first round usually carries the meal and main group conversation. If there is a second round, the group may choose lighter food, beer, cocktails, tea, dessert or noraebang. A third round exists in popular culture, but it should not be presented as the normal finish line.

Ask before moving: “Are we going somewhere else?” and “Is it okay if I finish here?” A simple early notice is easier than disappearing. In a work setting, tell the organizer or a teammate you are leaving, thank the person who arranged the meal and say goodbye to your immediate group.

The healthiest modern rule is one decision per round. Check your energy, transport, medication, caregiving duties and next morning. Someone who leaves after dinner has completed dinner, not failed the night.

## Somaek has no medically safe “golden ratio”

Somaek combines soju and beer. Restaurants and friend groups invent names, mixing methods and preferred proportions, but there is no authoritative national recipe. A strong pour can hide behind carbonation and a small glass, making intake harder to track.

The World Health Organization's current [alcohol fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/alcohol) states that no form of alcohol consumption is risk-free. Risk varies with amount, frequency, health, age and context. That makes “the perfect 3:7 ratio” a taste claim, not health guidance.

If you choose alcohol, go slowly, know what was poured and alternate with non-alcoholic drinks. Do not mix someone's glass without asking. Never add alcohol secretly or treat a stronger glass as a prank. Pregnancy, driving, medication, recovery, religion, health and simple preference are all sufficient reasons not to drink—and nobody owes the table a reason.

## Polite Korean table etiquette without forced shots

When someone older or more senior pours for you, receiving the glass with two hands or supporting one wrist with the other is a familiar respectful gesture. When pouring, watch the glass and avoid overflowing it. During a group toast, lift your drink and make comfortable eye contact.

Turning slightly away from a senior while drinking appears in traditional etiquette explanations, but context matters. It is not necessary to perform an exaggerated side turn at a casual dinner. Follow the group's tone and prioritize not spilling on anyone.

“One shot” or *won-shot* is a request, not a command. You can sip. Leaving alcohol in the glass is not a moral offense. The better etiquette is mutual attention: do not refill someone who has declined, and do not ask why a glass contains cola, tea or zero-alcohol beer.

## How to decline alcohol clearly and politely

Short answers work: “I am not drinking tonight,” “I will have water,” or in Korean, *oneureun sul an masilgeyo* (오늘은 술 안 마실게요). You can also say *gwaenchanseumnida* (괜찮습니다, “I'm okay/no thank you”) while lightly covering the glass.

Do not invent a medical condition unless you want to disclose one. A complete host accepts the first answer. If a person keeps pouring, move the glass, ask a trusted colleague for support or leave the situation. At work, document repeated coercion and use an internal reporting channel or qualified labor advice when appropriate.

International visitors sometimes overperform because they fear being rude. Calm consistency is clearer than accepting several shots and then becoming unsafe. Join the meal through food, conversation and the toast itself.

## Anju is food, not protection from intoxication

Korean drinking venues commonly pair beverages with substantial dishes: grilled meat, fried chicken, jeon, fish cake soup, tofu with kimchi, dried seafood or spicy stew. Sharing anju slows the table and creates conversation, but food does not cancel alcohol or make it safe to drive.

Order for the group and check dietary needs before assuming a dish is suitable. Broths may contain meat or seafood, pancakes may include egg, and “vegetable” dishes may use fish sauce. At a pocha, confirm the price and portion before ordering.

## How workplace dinners are changing

There is no single “Korean company.” A multinational office, public agency, startup, factory, school and family business can have different expectations. Team age, manager behavior and industry matter more than a travel cliché.

Recent Korean research on Generation Z workplace gatherings identified multiple attitudes, including a group that experiences hoesik as burden and pressure. That does not mean all young workers hate group meals. It supports a more accurate conclusion: people want different forms, and voluntary attendance matters.

Better workplaces use lunch, set an end time, offer alcohol-free choices, avoid evaluating attendance and let employees leave without explanation. A restaurant meal can still build rapport when the employer removes the loyalty test.

## Safe return planning should happen before the first toast

Save your accommodation address in Korean, keep the phone charged and check the final subway before drinking. Seoul operates N-numbered late-night buses; the city's current [public transportation guide](https://english.seoul.go.kr/service/movement/public-transportation/) says late-night buses operate after midnight, but routes and last departures must be checked on the day.

Use a legitimate metered taxi or a recognized ride-hailing service. The official [Visit Seoul taxi guide](https://english.visitseoul.net/taxi) explains regular, deluxe, large and international taxis. Share the vehicle details with a friend when useful, sit where you feel safe and do not drive after drinking.

If someone is difficult to wake, breathing abnormally, repeatedly vomiting, confused or injured, treat it as a medical emergency rather than “sleeping it off.” Call 119 for ambulance or rescue. For crime or immediate police help call 112; the official [Seoul safety page](https://english.visitseoul.net/safety) lists both numbers.

## A practical plan for visitors and new employees

Before the event, ask whether it is lunch or dinner, who is paying, what time it ends and whether another venue is expected. Decide your alcohol limit—or zero—before the first pour. Eat, keep your own glass visible and arrange the return route.

During the meal, use respectful gestures without copying exaggerated drama etiquette. Talk to more than one person, help with shared food when comfortable, and do not make the youngest person manage every grill or pour. If the company pays, let the organizer handle the bill unless asked.

Afterward, leave when your plan says to leave. Send a short thank-you message if that fits the team. You do not need to explain why you skipped karaoke, and you should not post another person's drinking behavior.

## The practical answer

Korean drinking culture includes real rituals: shared anju, rounds, two-handed pouring and group toasts. None requires intoxication. Hoesik is changing because employees and responsible managers increasingly separate team connection from forced attendance and forced alcohol.

Join what feels safe, refuse clearly, respect other people's choices and plan transport before the night stretches. The most culturally fluent person at the table is not the one who drinks fastest; it is the one who notices boundaries.

## FAQ

**Q: Do I have to drink alcohol at a Korean hoesik?**

No. You can join with water, soda, tea or a zero-alcohol drink. A respectful workplace and host should not require alcohol or a personal explanation.

**Q: Is it rude to refuse a drink from a Korean boss?**

A brief, calm refusal is appropriate. Use two hands if receiving a non-alcoholic drink, join the toast and stay engaged in the meal without accepting unwanted alcohol.

**Q: Must I finish a Korean drink in one shot?**

No. “One shot” is sometimes said during a toast, but you may sip or not drink. Emptying the glass is not a universal etiquette rule.

**Q: What is the correct somaek ratio?**

There is no official ratio. Preferences vary, and stronger mixing makes intake harder to track. Somaek is optional and is not safer than drinking its ingredients separately.

**Q: How many rounds does a Korean night have?**

It can have one, two or more, but no fixed number is required. Treat every move to another venue as a new choice and leave after the main meal if that is your plan.

**Q: How do I get home safely after drinking in Seoul?**

Check the last subway, current N-bus route or legitimate taxi before the event. Keep the phone charged, share ride details when useful, never drive, and call 119 for a medical emergency.
