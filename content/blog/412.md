In early 2026 Korea went briefly insane for **두쫀쿠** — *Dubai jjondeuk cookie*, a homemade chewy cookie built to taste like the pistachio-and-kadayif chocolate bar that took over the internet. Then the ingredients ran out. Pistachio spread vanished from shelves, kadayif was hard to find, and Korean social media did what Korean social media does: it improvised.

What people reached for was a **찰떡파이** — a soft, coated mochi pie that has been sitting in Korean supermarkets since 1998.

It worked well enough that Lotte Wellfood shipped an official version. That product sold out on the company's own store in **one week**, moved roughly **one million boxes and ten million individual packs in its first month**, and pushed total 찰떡파이 sales up **290% year on year**.

And almost none of that is the interesting part.

## First, What Is Actually In It

A 찰떡파이 is chocolate over rice cake, and the box says so in four words: **달콤한 초코와 쫀득한 찰떡** — sweet chocolate and chewy rice cake.

Outside is the coating, declared on the label as two separate things: **초콜릿-S** and **준초콜릿**, "quasi-chocolate", the Korean regulatory category for a coating made with vegetable fat rather than cocoa butter alone. Inside is **찰떡** — glutinous rice cake, the same material as Korean 인절미, genuinely chewy rather than cake-soft.

If you have eaten a Choco Pie, this is the same silhouette with the marshmallow replaced by real rice cake, and the difference is enormous. Choco Pie is airy. This is dense and elastic, and it weighs more than it looks like it should.

**It also does not shed crumbs**, which is a small thing that turns out to explain a lot about where this snack became popular.

**The declared percentages are worth reading, because they are surprising.** The current box states **초콜릿-S 15.8%, 준초콜릿 12.5%, 찹쌀 2.7%** across 350 g. A 2020 label for the 375 g version gave 16.2% / 12.8% / **2.8%**.

So a product whose name is *glutinous rice cake pie* declares **under 3% glutinous rice**, against roughly 28% chocolate. The rice cake is a thin shell around a filling, not a slab — and the reason it can be that thin at all is the subject of the next section.

## The Hard Part Was Never the Chocolate

Anyone can coat a rice cake in chocolate. The problem is what happens two days later.

Cooked rice starch does not stay soft. It **retrogrades** — the starch molecules recrystallise, water migrates out, and the cake turns hard and dry. That is why fresh 떡 from a Korean rice-cake shop is a same-day food and why Koreans reflexively freeze it. A rice cake sealed in a wrapper for a supermarket shelf should be a brick within a week.

On **May 1999**, 삼진식품's chairman **박충호** registered a Korean patent for **"외피가 도포된 떡 및 그 제조방법"** — a coated rice cake and the method of making it. Its stated purpose was to prevent the staling of rice cake over time and to block microbial growth.

Around **2002** the company developed a further method that extended the rice cake's usable life from **ten weeks to five months**. It deliberately **did not patent that one.** It classified it as a trade secret.

That decision is the hinge of everything that follows.

## Lotte Sells It. A Company You Have Never Heard Of Makes It.

찰떡파이 was launched in **late 1998 by 삼진식품 (Samjin Food)**, a small Korean manufacturer, and it did not start life in supermarkets. It went after niches: **military bases and PC bangs** — places where people eat standing up, in a hurry, next to a keyboard. The no-crumbs property was not a footnote there.

In **2000** it did something a small company almost never does. In the Korean military PX market it **outsold Orion's Choco Pie** — the most famous snack in the country and, at that time, a semi-official currency of Korean army life — to become the top-selling single item, and was named the snack soldiers liked most.

In **September 2000** the company was certified as a technology-development venture firm by Korea's Small and Medium Business Administration. In **late 2001** it signed an **OEM supply contract with Lotte Confectionery**.

That contract is why this article exists. **Lotte's 명가 찰떡파이 and Samjin's own 찰떡파이 are made in the same factory, by the same company.** So is the version sold under other labels. When you buy the Lotte box, you are buying a small company's invention in a large company's packaging — which is completely normal in food manufacturing.

**And it is not hidden. It is printed on the box.** Korean food labels separate two roles: 제조원, the manufacturer, and 유통전문판매원, the company that distributes and sells it. A 명가 찰떡파이 label reads:

- **유통전문판매원: 롯데제과(주)**, Yeongdeungpo-gu, Seoul
- **제조원: 삼진식품**, Sudirae-gil 3, Cheongpyeong-myeon, Gapyeong-gun, Gyeonggi-do

Lotte's name is on the front in the largest type on the box. Samjin's name, and the address of the factory in Gapyeong where the thing is actually made, is in small print on the side. **Nothing is being concealed — it is simply that almost nobody turns the box over.**

## Then the Recipe Walked Out on a Laptop

In **June 2003**, the head of Samjin's R&D department resigned. He had overseen the raw materials, the blend ratios and the process control — that is, the trade secret the company had chosen not to patent.

He took the information out on a **personal laptop**. In **September 2004** he joined **청우식품**, a competitor. By **October 2005** 청우식품 had developed a similar snack using that information, and then did something bolder: it **registered the manufacturing method as its own patent.**

Chairman 박충호 filed to have that patent invalidated. Korea's Intellectual Property Trial and Appeal Board **rejected him.** He sued to overturn that decision at the Patent Court and won, and on **7 October 2011** the **Supreme Court confirmed it.** The panel's reasoning was blunt: 청우식품 *"contributed nothing to the invention in question,"* making it a case of a party with no right to the patent filing for and registering it — and therefore void.

박충호 was **78 years old** when the judgment came down.

## And Then Korea Started Building Dubai Cookies Out of It

![Chart of the 2026 Dubai-style Chaltteok Pie: sold out in one week, ten million packs and about one million boxes in the first month, total Chaltteok Pie sales up 290% year on year, brand revenue moving from 20 to a targeted 30 billion won, with the four-stage flow from the 두쫀쿠 trend to the product shipping.](/assets/images/posts/412/chaltteok-pie-dubai-2026.jpg)
*EpicKor chart. Figures as reported by Lotte Wellfood's development team in 2026.*

Twenty-eight years after that first box, the snack got the strangest promotion of its life.

**두쫀쿠** — 두바이 쫀득 쿠키, "Dubai chewy cookie" — was a home-baking craze built on pistachio cream and **카다이프** (kadayif, the shredded pastry that gives the Dubai chocolate bar its crunch). When the ingredients ran short, Korean social media started assembling it on top of a 찰떡파이 instead.

Lotte Wellfood has a name for this: **모디슈머** (*modisumer*), a consumer who writes the recipe. It also has machinery for catching it. The company runs a **트렌드 경보기** — a "trend alarm" that fires when a keyword spikes online — and a trend report called **민트** whose publication cycle was recently cut from quarterly to monthly, because, as the research lead put it, new trends now arrive every month.

The team went from a viral hashtag to mass production in about **three months**, which Korean confectionery coverage described as unusually fast. Two details from the development team are worth having:

**They made the mochi thinner.** 두쫀쿠 gets its texture from marshmallow, which is lighter than rice cake, so the 두바이 version runs a thinner 찰떡 layer than the standard product. Finding that thickness took hundreds of blend tests.

**They designed the box around a photograph.** The product's three layers — chocolate coating, mochi, cream — are shown as three colour bands, and the wrapper carries camels and palm trees. The marketer's stated reason was that colour-forward products do better on social media, so it is considered from the planning stage.

The result: sold out on Lotte's official store in a week, roughly **1 million boxes and 10 million packs in the first month**, and **찰떡파이 sales up 290% year on year** that month. The brand was doing about **₩20 billion a year**; the stated goal is now **₩30 billion**.

## What to Buy, and What It Costs

**The standard product is 명가 찰떡파이, 210 g, 12 pieces**, sold in supermarkets, online and in convenience stores in smaller counts. That is the one to start with — it is the original recipe and it is available all year.

**The Dubai-style version was widely reported at ₩7,980 at large marts in 2026.** Treat that as the mart shelf price rather than a fixed one: it began as a limited pre-launch run, sold out, and went into additional production, and limited editions in Korean confectionery move in and out of stock constantly.

**Check whether the box says 두바이st before you get excited.** Lotte expanded the 두바이st 피스타치오 line to six products across biscuits and frozen desserts, with more after that, so the pistachio wrapper on the shelf next to it may be a different snack entirely.

**Every other Korean confectioner did the same thing.** Crown released a 두바이스타일 쵸코하임 capped at 550,000 units, and Haitai released five pistachio versions of existing products including 홈런볼, 버터링 and 자유시간. If you want the pistachio thing, you have options; if you want the mochi thing, this is the one.

## Honest Notes

**The packaging has been mocked for fifteen years.** In June 2011 a Korean student's video comparing box volume to contents made 찰떡파이 a standing example of 과대포장 — over-packaging. The defence is real: rice cake is dense, so the box weighs more than a Choco Pie box of the same size, and the pieces need protection. It is still a lot of cardboard for what you eat.

**Mochi texture is genuinely divisive.** Korean commentary is candid that visitors from countries without a rice-cake tradition often do not enjoy the chew, describing it as gummy rather than pleasant. It became a word-of-mouth hit with Japanese visitors from the late 2000s — Japan has 餅 — and that is not a coincidence. Buy one packet before you buy a box for someone.

**Try it frozen.** Korean readers recommend a short stint in the freezer, the same trick people use with Choco Pie. The rice cake firms up and the coating snaps.

**It contains a sugar alcohol, and the box carries a warning about it.** The label declares **말티톨시럽 10.6%** — maltitol syrup — and prints the line **과량 섭취시 설사를 일으킬 수 있습니다**: excessive consumption may cause diarrhoea. That is standard for maltitol at this level and it is not a scare, but it is printed on a snack box for a reason, and a 10-pack is easy to work through.

**"Lotte invented it" is wrong, and so is "Lotte copied it."** Lotte is a distribution and branding customer of the company that invented it — a normal OEM relationship, disclosed on the label if you read the manufacturer line. The company that did try to take it lost at the Supreme Court.

**The 290% figure is one month against the same month a year earlier**, during a national craze, reported by the manufacturer. It is a real number and it is not a run rate.

## FAQ

**Q: What is 찰떡파이?**
**A:** A Korean snack: a chocolate coating over a soft glutinous rice cake centre. Lotte sells it as 명가 찰떡파이; the box currently on shelves is 350 g with 10 individually wrapped packs.

**Q: Is it the same as a Choco Pie?**
**A:** No. Same silhouette, but the middle layer is real rice cake instead of marshmallow, so it is denser, chewier and heavier.

**Q: Who actually makes it?**
**A:** 삼진식품 (Samjin Food), which launched it in late 1998 and patented the method in May 1999. Lotte has been an OEM customer since late 2001, so both companies' boxes come from the same factory.

**Q: What is the Dubai version?**
**A:** 명가 찰떡파이 두바이st 피스타치오맛, launched in 2026 after Koreans started using the ordinary pie as a base for homemade Dubai-style chewy cookies. It has a thinner mochi layer and pistachio. Widely reported at ₩7,980 at large marts.

**Q: Why did it become popular with soldiers?**
**A:** It went after the military PX early, it does not shed crumbs, and it is dense enough to be filling. In 2000 it outsold Orion's Choco Pie there to become the top-selling single item.
