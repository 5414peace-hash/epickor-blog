On the shelf of any Korean supermarket there is a long silver packet of pale, ridged, rectangular biscuits called **빠다코코낫**. Ask a Korean what it means and you will get a small pause, because the honest answer is that it means *butter coconut* — but it does not say butter coconut. It says something closer to **"ppada koconat."**

That is not a typo, and it is not a brand-name pun. It is what those two English words looked like in Korean in **1979**, before the loanword rules were tightened. The biscuit has simply refused to update.

And here is the part that makes it worth an article: **Orion once sold the same kind of biscuit under the modern, correct spelling — 버터코코넛 — and it is discontinued.** The one that got it wrong is 47 years old and has had two separate national revivals.

## The Spelling Is the Whole Story

![Comparison of 빠다코코낫 and 버터코코넛: Lotte's pre-standard spelling still sold after 47 years versus Orion's correctly spelled version, discontinued, with a panel explaining that both halves of the name are borrowed English frozen at the point they entered Korean.](/assets/images/posts/413/badacoconut-name-spelling.jpg)
*EpicKor chart.*

Korean writes borrowed words by rule, and the rules have changed. Modern standard Korean writes English *butter* as **버터** and *coconut* as **코코넛**. In 1979 the accepted rendering was different, and **빠다코코낫** was simply how you wrote it.

Then the rules moved and the product did not. Korean reference sources record an attempt to restandardise the name to **버터코코넛** during a push for correct orthography — and it was dropped, because by then 빠다코코낫 had stopped being a description and become a proper noun. Renaming it would have thrown away the only thing a 40-year-old biscuit has.

So the packet is now a small piece of preserved language: two English words, written the way Korea wrote them at a specific moment, kept alive commercially long after the spelling died everywhere else.

**Orion's version demonstrates the cost of being right.** Orion sold a 버터코코넛 using the current standard orthography. It is no longer made.

## What You Are Actually Eating

It is a **butter biscuit with coconut in it**, and the details are specific enough to check against the packet in your hand.

The shape is a **rectangle with scalloped edges** — Korean descriptions compare it to the perforated border of a postage stamp — and the face carries roughly **17 to 18 holes**, docked to stop the dough puffing in the oven.

The surface is glazed with **물엿**, starch syrup, which is why it looks faintly wet and catches the light. Mixed into the dough is **finely minced coconut flesh**, not just coconut flavouring, and the butter is laid on heavily.

Two consequences follow, and Korean commentary is blunt about both. It is **noticeably sweeter than Ace**, Korea's other national plain biscuit. And it **makes you thirsty** — Koreans rank it with 버터링 as one of the two great thirst-inducing biscuits, which is why the standing advice is to eat it with coffee rather than alone.

It is also a fixture of Korean military life, for the same practical reason as most snacks that end up there: it is dry, dense and does not fall apart.

## Korea Was the Fourth Stop, Not the First

![Chart of the biscuit's transmission route: Japan's coconut sablé in 1965, Nisshin Seika's butter coconut in 1966, Haitai's Korean version around 1969, Lotte's 빠다코코낫 in 1979, plus the Taiwanese and Indian equivalents.](/assets/images/posts/413/badacoconut-route.jpg)
*EpicKor chart.*

The name is borrowed English. The biscuit itself is borrowed too, and Korean reference sources are matter-of-fact about it.

The format traces to Japan. A **coconut sablé — ココナッツサブレ — appeared in 1965**, and the following year **닛신제과 (Nisshin Seika) launched バターココナツ**, "butter coconut." That is the name Korea took. The Japanese company expanded hard, opening plants in Taiwan and China, and was **liquidated in 2002**; the brand passed to a Singaporean owner and is now produced in China and exported back into Japan's hundred-yen shops.

In Korea, **Lotte was not even first.** Around **1969**, ten years before 빠다코코낫, 해태제과 made a **코코낫 빠다 비스켙** — the same two loanwords, in the other order.

The biscuit spread further than either. Taiwan sells it as **可口奶滋**, kept in production after Nabisco acquired Nisshin's local operation, and India has a near-identical biscuit called **Nice Time**.

So the object in your hand is a Japanese format with an English name, arrived in Korea second-hand, in a spelling that was already on its way out. **And it has outlived the company that named it.**

## 앙빠: How a Biscuit for Dads Got 20-Somethings Back

By the 2010s 빠다코코낫 had the demographic problem every legacy snack has: the people who loved it were the people who had always loved it. Korean commentary called it 아재 입맛 — a middle-aged man's taste.

Then **앙빠** happened, and it happened without Lotte.

Korea had been in the middle of an **앙버터** craze — 앙금 (sweetened red bean paste) plus a slab of cold butter, sandwiched in bread, sold in cafés everywhere. Someone swapped the bread for a 빠다코코낫. The biscuit's snap works better against cold butter than soft bread does, the size is right for a single dessert, and it keeps far longer. It started as a menu item in a handful of Seoul cafés, moved to social media as a recipe, and turned into thousands of posts.

The commercial result, reported in **August 2018**: 빠다코코낫 sales up **30% over three months** year on year, and the **large packs up more than 50%**.

Lotte's response is the interesting bit. The company caught the spike through an AI trend-analysis system it calls **엘시아 (LCIA)**, made 앙빠 content on its own channels, and then did something a manufacturer rarely does — **printed the 앙빠 recipe on the back of the packet.** A Lotte representative said the effect was to pull the eating demographic down toward women in their twenties and thirties.

**If that pattern sounds familiar, it should.** Eight years later the same company caught a different viral recipe with a different trend system and shipped [a Dubai-style Chaltteok Pie in about three months](/blog/412). Lotte has now done this twice, with two legacy products, and it is clearly no longer improvising.

## What It Costs, and How Koreans Actually Eat It

![Open packet of 빠다코코낫 showing the ridged rectangular butter biscuits stacked upright inside the foil sleeve.](/assets/images/posts/413/badacoconut-pack-commons.jpg)
*빠다코코낫 in the sleeve. Photo by zziny via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0 KR.*

The **100 g pack runs ₩1,700 at Emart24**, where a Korean convenience-store price tracker lists it as a recurring **2+1** promotion item — which brings the effective unit price to about **₩1,133**. Supermarkets carry larger boxes and a 78 g size exists as well. This has never been an expensive biscuit and it is not one now.

It has been reformulated more than once, and the current box declares different things from the one people remember.

In **2016** Lotte released a **new빠다코코낫** with the processed-butter content raised from **2.9% to 3.3%** and the coconut powder from **4.6% to 5.0%**, with the recommended retail price moving from **₩1,200 to ₩1,400**.

**The box on shelves now has moved again.** The 78 g pack states **395 kcal** and declares **가당볶음코코넛분말 6.7%** — sweetened roasted coconut powder, itself 69% dried coconut — and **버터 1%**, with a **프랑스산 버터** flash on the corner: French butter, called out as a selling point.

Two things to take from that. The coconut is no longer a flavouring percentage in the low single digits but a named 6.7% roasted-and-sweetened preparation. And the butter is now **real butter, declared at 1% and sourced abroad**, rather than the processed butter of the 2016 formula. If you remember this biscuit tasting different at some point, you are not imagining it.

**Three ways Koreans eat it:**

**With coffee, plain.** The default. The thirst issue is real and coffee solves it.

**As 앙빠.** Red bean paste and a cold slice of butter between two biscuits. The recipe is on the back of the packet.

**Dunked, briefly.** The starch-syrup glaze means it holds together longer in hot liquid than a dry biscuit would, but not indefinitely.

## Honest Notes

**It is genuinely sweet and genuinely rich, and that limits how many you can eat.** Korean reviewers describe getting sick of it quickly when eating it alone, which is unusual candour about a snack people are fond of. Buy the small pack first.

**The "correct" name would be 버터코코넛.** If you are learning Korean, do not take this packet as a spelling guide — it is a historical artefact, and writing 빠다 for butter in any other context will read as either dated or dialectal.

**It has almost no coconut aroma when cold and stale.** The coconut is minced flesh in the dough rather than a top note; a packet that has been open for a week loses most of it. Reseal it properly.

**The 2018 sales figures are the manufacturer's own** and cover a three-month window against the same period a year earlier, during a viral trend. They show a real effect, not a permanent level.

**Lotte has leaned on the brand outside biscuits** — an apparel collaboration with a Korean fashion label, and a 빠다코코낫 doughnut with Krispy Kreme. If you see the name on something that is not a biscuit, that is why.

## FAQ

**Q: What does 빠다코코낫 mean?**
**A:** Butter coconut. Both words are borrowed English, written in the loanword orthography that was standard when the biscuit launched in 1979. The modern spelling would be 버터코코넛.

**Q: Why is it spelled the old way?**
**A:** There was a push to restandardise it and it was abandoned, because the name had already become a proper noun. Orion's correctly spelled 버터코코넛 is discontinued.

**Q: Is it a Korean invention?**
**A:** No, and Korean sources say so plainly. Japan had a coconut sablé in 1965 and Nisshin Seika's バターココナツ in 1966; Haitai made a Korean version around 1969, a decade before Lotte's.

**Q: What is 앙빠?**
**A:** Red bean paste and cold butter sandwiched between two 빠다코코낫 — the 앙버터 café dessert with the bread swapped out. It drove a 30% sales rise in 2018 and the recipe is now printed on the packet.

**Q: How much is it?**
**A:** About ₩1,700 for the 100 g pack at Emart24, frequently on 2+1, per a Korean convenience-store price tracker in 2026.
