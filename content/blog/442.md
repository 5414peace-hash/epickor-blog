Two visitors can come home from Korea with opposite reports. One spent a week in Seoul and says nobody looked at them once. The other spent a week in a provincial city and says they were looked at constantly — on the bus, in the market, by children who stopped walking.

Both are telling the truth. **Whether Koreans stare at you depends almost entirely on where you are standing**, and a little on what you look like. This guide says where the looking happens and where it does not, why a country that just crossed the line into being 5% foreign still finds a foreign face worth a second look in most of its territory, what the look means, what it does not mean, and what to do when it lands on you.

## Where you are not looked at

Start with the places where the answer is simply no, because that is most of what a first-time visitor sees.

**Central Seoul does not look at you.** Myeongdong, Hongdae, Gangnam, Seongsu, Itaewon, the palaces, the big stations — these are districts where a foreign face is part of the furniture. Seoul had 450,000 foreign residents at the last count, Itaewon has been the foreigners' quarter since the American base arrived in the 1950s, and Hongdae on a weekend night is one of the most international crowds in Asia. Nobody has time to look, and nobody has a reason to.

The same goes for **university districts anywhere** — Sinchon, Daehak-ro, the areas around any campus in Busan or Daegu — and for **any place that sells to tourists**: Jeonju Hanok Village, Gyeongju's main sights, Haeundae. If you only ever stand in these places, you will conclude that the staring question is a myth — which is the conclusion most one-week Seoul visitors reach, and it is correct for the week they had.

## Where you are

Now leave those districts, and the picture changes within a few subway stops.

- **Traditional markets.** Namdaemun and Gwangjang in Seoul see tourists; the covered market in a provincial city does not. Vendors will look up, follow you with their eyes, and often say something to the next stall. This is the friendliest version of the stare. It ends in an extra piece of fruit more often than not.
- **Provincial cities, off the tourist line.** Daejeon, Cheongju, Jeonju outside the hanok village, Ulsan, Pohang, Changwon. Ordinary residential neighbourhoods, ordinary buses. You will be noticed.
- **Small towns and the countryside.** Here it is not a stare so much as a stop. A grandmother on a bench will watch you walk the whole length of the street. A farmer will pause the tractor. It is not unfriendly; it is *an event*.
- **The subway car, when it is full of older people.** A Seoul Line 1 train out toward Suwon or Uijeongbu at mid-morning is a different population from the same line at Hongdae at midnight. You will be looked at on one and not the other.
- **Children, anywhere.** Small children stare everywhere in the world. Korean small children stare and then say "hello!" and then hide behind a parent. Teenagers in groups stare and then dare each other to say hello. Both are the same thing, and both are fine.

## Why: Korea has been a 5% country for about a year

The forum explanation for the staring is that Korea is "97% Korean". That number is out of date, and the way it is out of date explains the pattern above.

**Foreign residents in Korea passed 5% of the population in 2025**, the threshold Korean policy and press, citing the OECD, use to call a country multicultural. The Ministry of the Interior counted 2.58 million foreign residents in its annual census-based figure (5.0%), and the Ministry of Justice's monthly count passed 2.83 million in October 2025, about 5.5% of the population — the highest ever, and up from 4.8% only a year earlier. Korea is now, on paper, a country of immigration.

Two things about *who* those people are, and *where*, keep the street feeling the way it does.

**First, they are concentrated.** A third of all foreign residents live in Gyeonggi province around Seoul, and the densest concentrations are industrial and commercial districts — Ansan (over 15% foreign), Siheung, and the southwest Seoul districts of Geumcheon, Yeongdeungpo and Guro (all over 12%). Most of the country's other cities and counties sit far below that. **The 5% is not spread across Korea; it is piled up in a few places**, and the tourist rarely goes to any of them.

**Second, most of them do not read as foreign at a glance.** The largest groups of foreign residents are Chinese nationals including ethnic Koreans from China, Vietnamese, and other East and Southeast Asians. The same is true of visitors: of the record **18.9 million tourists in 2025**, 5.5 million were Chinese, 3.7 million Japanese, 1.9 million Taiwanese, 0.6 million from Hong Kong — about 60% from places whose visitors do not stand out on a Korean street. Americans were 1.5 million, the fifth group.

So the honest arithmetic is this. Korea has plenty of foreigners now, but outside a handful of districts, a **visibly** foreign face — European, African, South Asian, Middle Eastern — remains something a resident of Andong or Mokpo might see a few times a month. A generation ago it was a few times a year. Their eyes have not caught up with the statistics, and it is unreasonable to expect them to have.

There is a longer history underneath, and Koreans know it. For most of the twentieth century the schools taught that Korea was a **단일민족**, a single-blooded people — a story built to hold the country together through occupation and war. In 2007 the UN's Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination formally told Korea that the idea was getting in the way of a multi-ethnic present, and the textbooks began to change. The people over sixty who look at you longest were educated before that. The students who do not look at you at all were educated after.

## What the look means, and what it does not

**What it means, most of the time:** *you are unusual here, and I am not used to pretending otherwise.* Korean public manners do not include the Western rule that you must not be caught looking. There is no taboo on watching a stranger; there is a taboo on bothering one. So the look is open, and it stops short of contact, which is precisely what makes it feel so loud to someone raised where the rule is the reverse.

It helps to know what Koreans themselves say about strangers. Korea's own 2025 values survey found that **84% of Koreans would not trust a stranger they had just met** — a Korean stranger — and 77% said the same of foreign residents. Distance from anyone outside their circle is the default, and we wrote a whole guide to [why Koreans can seem cold to foreigners](/blog/440) because of it. The stare is that same distance with curiosity added. It is not a step toward you; it is also not a step away.

**What it does not mean:** that you are unwelcome. Korea recorded its highest-ever visitor numbers in 2025 and its towns compete for them; the stare on the bus and the free tangerine at the market come from the same person, and the second follows the first more often than not.

**And the part that is not fine.** A minority of foreigners in Korea meet something worse than a look: a bar in Itaewon or Hongdae that refuses non-Koreans at the door, a taxi that pulls away, a landlord who will not rent, a comment. In the 2025 government survey, **43.7% of foreign residents said they had experienced discrimination**, most often on the basis of nationality. In the National Human Rights Commission's 2020 survey of migrants, 68.4% said racial discrimination exists in Korea, and the grounds they named most were Korean-language ability (62.3%), not being Korean (59.7%) and country of origin (56.8%). Residents from Africa, South and Southeast Asia consistently describe more of it than white Westerners do, and this article should not paper over that. A stare is not discrimination. A refused door is — and Korea, unusually for a rich democracy, still has **no general anti-discrimination law**; bills have been filed in every National Assembly since 2007 and none has passed. What exists is the National Human Rights Commission, which takes complaints on **1331**, and the 다누리 helpline (**1577-1366**, 24 hours, 13 languages) for migrants and multicultural families.

## What to do with it

The visitors who enjoy provincial Korea are the ones who learned to answer the look rather than absorb it.

1. **Meet it.** Eye contact, a small nod, a smile. Most stares are waiting for permission to become something else. Given it, they turn into a grin, a thumbs-up, or a question.
2. **Say 안녕하세요.** *Annyeonghaseyo*, said badly, is the single most effective tool a foreigner has in a Korean market. The person who was staring will laugh, answer, and very often start feeding you.
3. **Say where you are from.** "미국에서 왔어요" (*miguk-eseo wasseoyo* — I'm from America), or just the country name. It answers the question the stare was asking.
4. **Let children have it.** Wave. Say hello. They will scream and run and come back with a friend. This is the best five minutes of most people's trip.
5. **Do not answer a stare with a stare.** It reads as a challenge in Korea just as it does anywhere. Look away, look back, smile.
6. **Expect it on the bus, not in the cafe.** If the looking gets tiring, the fix is geographic. Any place where the average age drops under thirty-five — a university street, a chain cafe, a cinema — is a place where nobody looks. And if you need directions in one of those places, [ask the person next to you in English](/blog/441); they will answer.
7. **Know the difference between a look and a door.** If you are refused service because of your face, that is not a cultural misunderstanding and you do not have to absorb it. Leave, and if it matters to you, report it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Why do Koreans stare at foreigners?**

Because outside central Seoul and a few industrial districts, a visibly foreign face is still uncommon, and Korean public manners have no rule against looking at a stranger — only against bothering one. Foreign residents passed 5% of Korea's population only in 2025, and they are concentrated in a handful of areas most visitors never see. The look is curiosity, not hostility, and it is strongest among older people, children, and in markets and small towns.

**Q: Do Koreans stare in Seoul?**

In central Seoul, almost never. Myeongdong, Hongdae, Gangnam, Itaewon, Seongsu and the university districts see so many foreigners that nobody registers one. The staring reports come from provincial cities, the countryside, traditional markets, and the outer ends of subway lines where the passengers are older.

**Q: Is staring in Korea rude?**

Not by Korean standards. The Western rule that you must not be caught looking does not exist in Korea; the rule that exists is that you must not intrude. So the stare is open and stops short of contact. Meeting it with a nod or a hello almost always turns it into a smile.

**Q: Do Koreans stare more at Black people?**

Yes, honestly. The look is the same kind of look, and there is more of it, along with more of the worse things — refused doors, comments. Residents from Africa, South and Southeast Asia consistently describe more discrimination than white Westerners, and in the Human Rights Commission's 2020 survey migrants named country of origin as one of the top three grounds. Most of it is still curiosity; some of it is not. Korea has no general anti-discrimination law, but the National Human Rights Commission takes complaints on 1331.

**Q: What should I do when Koreans stare at me?**

Meet it. A nod, a smile, and 안녕하세요 (*annyeonghaseyo*) will turn most stares into a conversation or a gift. Do not stare back. If it tires you, move to a younger neighbourhood — a university street or a chain cafe — where nobody looks.

**Q: How many foreigners live in Korea?**

About 2.83 million people on long-term stays as of October 2025, roughly 5.5% of the population and the highest ever, by the Ministry of Justice's count. A third live in Gyeonggi province; the densest districts are Ansan and southwest Seoul. Most are Chinese, Vietnamese and other Asian nationals, which is why the streets still feel less international than the number suggests.

## Bottom Line

Korea has become a country of immigration on paper — 5% foreign, and rising every month — while most of its territory still experiences a visibly foreign face as an event. The stare is the gap between those two facts, and it is closing about as fast as you would expect: **the students do not look, and the grandmothers do.**

So the answer to the question is geographic. In Seoul, nobody will look at you. Everywhere else, some people will, and the right response is to look back kindly and say hello, because on the other side of that stare is usually someone about to hand you a tangerine.
