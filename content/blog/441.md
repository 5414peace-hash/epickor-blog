The forum answer to "do Koreans speak English?" has not changed in fifteen years: *not really, learn some Korean, download a translator.* It was true once. In 2026 it is mostly wrong, and it is wrong in a specific, useful way.

**Stop a Korean in their twenties or thirties on a Seoul street and ask a direct question in English, and you will almost always get an answer.** Not a perfect one, sometimes a shy one, but a real one, often with the person walking you to the corner to make sure. Ask a shopkeeper in his sixties the same thing and you may get an apologetic wave.

That is the whole picture: **it is a question of who you ask, not whether Koreans can.** The line runs by age, and it has moved a long way in the last ten years. This guide explains where the line is, why it sits there, and how to put yourself on the right side of it — on the street, in the subway, in a taxi, and outside Seoul.

## What actually happens when you ask

Here is the experiment, run more times than we can count in Seoul.

Stand outside a subway exit in **Hongdae, Sinchon, Gangnam or Seongsu** with your phone open and ask the nearest person in their twenties, in plain English, "Excuse me — which way to Gyeongbokgung?" What you get back, nine times out of ten, is one of three things:

1. **A direct answer in English**, sometimes hesitant, usually correct.
2. **"Ah — a little"**, followed by an answer that is considerably more than a little.
3. **A walk.** They gesture *follow me* and take you to the corner, or the platform, or the door, because explaining it felt harder than doing it.

What you almost never get from that age group is a refusal. The refusals — the apologetic wave, the "no English, sorry", the pointing at someone else — come from a different group: shopkeepers and taxi drivers in their fifties and sixties, market vendors, security guards, the older staff in a neighbourhood restaurant. They are not being unfriendly. They genuinely did not learn to speak it, and they know it.

So the forum advice is half right. Koreans over fifty mostly do not speak English. Koreans under thirty-five mostly do, at least enough to get you where you are going. **The mistake is treating the country as one group.** For a few of the reasons why Koreans of any age can seem reserved with strangers at first, see our guide to [why Koreans seem cold to foreigners](/blog/440) — it is not about language.

## Why the line runs by age

The generational split is not a stereotype. It has a date.

**English became a compulsory subject in Korean primary schools in March 1997**, starting in the third grade, at age nine. Before that, Korean children met English at thirteen, in middle school, and what they met was grammar and translation: a textbook, a blackboard, and a teacher who had learned it the same way. Nobody spoke. The exam did not test speaking, so the classroom did not teach it.

Do the arithmetic and the line falls in a predictable place. A child in third grade in 1997 was born around 1988. **In 2026 that person is 38.** Everyone younger started English at nine with a curriculum that, from the start, included listening and speaking; everyone older started at thirteen with one that did not. That is the age line you meet on the street, and it explains why "under 35" is a safe rule and "under 45" is a coin toss.

Three other things pushed the same generation further:

- **They grew up with English on the screen.** YouTube, Netflix, Marvel, and the entire online life of anyone born after 1995 runs partly in English. Their parents' did not.
- **They travel.** Koreans made a record **29.55 million overseas trips in 2025**, and the biggest single group was people in their thirties — 5.33 million trips. Ordering coffee in Da Nang, Osaka and Los Angeles is a language class of its own.
- **They went to the academy.** Private tutoring reaches about three quarters of Korean schoolchildren, and English is one of the two subjects it is spent on. Most twenty-somethings have had far more contact hours with English than most Europeans.

The one honest caveat comes from the international tests. On the EF English Proficiency Index for 2025, Korea sits at 48th globally, and among the four skills its **speaking** score is the lowest — reading 540, listening 518, writing 509, speaking 489. Read that the wrong way and it says Koreans cannot speak. Read it the right way and it says what every visitor notices: **a young Korean understands almost everything you say and is a little slower to reply**, because the reply is the part that risks a mistake. That gap is confidence, not competence, and the way you ask can close most of it.

## How to ask so it works

Because the hesitation is about confidence, most of what helps is on your side of the conversation.

- **Do not open with "Do you speak English?"** It asks the person to grade themselves before they know what you want, and a modest Korean will say no to that question and then answer your real one in English. Ask the real one.
- **Ask something short and concrete.** "Which exit for Gyeongbokgung?" beats "I'm trying to get to the palace, is it far, should I take the subway or walk?" One question, one answer.
- **Hold up the map.** A phone showing Naver Map with the destination pinned turns a language problem into a pointing problem. Our guide to [Naver Map, Kakao T and why Google Maps still lags](/blog/222) covers getting the app set up before you land.
- **Use the Korean name of the place.** "Gyeongbokgung" lands; "the big palace" does not. "Gangnam-yeok" (역 = station) beats "Gangnam Station". The [Korean subway names and etiquette guide](/blog/174) has the ones you will use most.
- **Pick your person.** A student with a backpack, the person behind a cafe counter, the part-timer at a convenience store, anyone in a department store or a hotel lobby. These are the people under 35 who deal with the public all day.
- **Give them a second.** The pause after your question is not confusion. It is someone assembling an English sentence they have never said out loud. Fill it and you will get "a little" again.
- **Say thank you in Korean.** 감사합니다 (*gamsahamnida*) is the one word that reliably changes the temperature of the exchange, and it is the only one you need. If you want to go further, our [honest guide to learning Korean](/blog/068) says how far a week of effort actually gets you.

What not to do is the mirror image: do not slow-shout, do not switch to the sort of pidgin English people use on tourists, and do not read a refusal from an older person as rudeness. They would help if they could. Ask the next person, who is probably twenty years younger and already looking over. (If the older person is looking at you rather than away, that is curiosity, not refusal — our guide to [why Koreans stare](/blog/442) explains where it happens and what to do with it.)

The subway is the point worth repeating. Seoul's system has been layered with English for two decades: station numbers on every platform, exits numbered so that "exit 7" means the same thing to everyone, four-language announcements, and an app, 또타지하철, that runs in Korean, English, Chinese and Japanese. **You do not need a single Korean speaker to get across the city.** The people come in when you leave the station.

## Outside Seoul

The age line holds everywhere in Korea. What changes outside the capital is **who is standing on the street**.

Busan, Daegu, Daejeon and Incheon are cities full of universities and the young people who go to them; ask the same way and you get the same result, and on the EF index Seoul (550) and those cities (520–536) are close. **Suwon, oddly, scores lower (493)** — a reminder that a satellite city full of factories is a different population from a university district, even thirty minutes from Seoul.

In a small town or the countryside, the arithmetic turns against you: the twenty-somethings have moved to the cities, and the person running the guesthouse, the bus counter and the pharmacy is sixty. Three things carry you there:

1. **The address in Korean, on your phone, large.** Every Korean address has a Korean form; screenshot it from Naver Map before you go anywhere without signal.
2. **Papago's conversation mode**, phone held between you and the other person. It is markedly better than Google Translate at Korean, and older Koreans are used to being handed a phone — it happens to them with their grandchildren.
3. **The 1330 line.** Call it, say what you need, and hand the phone over. The interpreter talks to the pharmacist; the pharmacist talks back; you get the answer. It is the single most under-used tool a visitor has.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do Koreans speak English?**

Younger Koreans do. Anyone born from about 1988 onwards — under 38 in 2026 — started English at age nine when it became compulsory in primary school in 1997, and grew up with it on screens and on holidays. Ask a Korean in their twenties or thirties a direct question on a city street and you will almost always get an answer. Koreans over fifty largely learned it from grammar books and will usually tell you honestly that they cannot.

**Q: Can I travel in Korea with only English?**

Yes, comfortably in Seoul and the big cities. The subway, signage, ticket machines, Kakao T taxis and card payments need no Korean at all, and the 1330 tourist line will interpret for you 24 hours a day. In small towns you will lean on Papago, a written Korean address and 1330, because the young people who speak English have moved to the cities.

**Q: Why do Koreans say "a little" and then speak good English?**

Because "a little" is the polite Korean answer to any question about your own ability, and because the pause before replying is someone assembling a sentence they rarely say out loud. Korea's speaking score on the EF index is its lowest of the four skills, which describes confidence more than competence. Ask your real question instead of asking whether they speak English, and give them a second.

**Q: Is Papago better than Google Translate for Korean?**

For Korean, yes, clearly. Papago is built by Naver for Korean–English and handles Korean grammar, honorifics and menus better. Use camera mode on menus and signs and conversation mode with a phone held between you and the other person. Keep Google Translate for languages Papago does not cover.

**Q: What is the 1330 number?**

The Korea Tourism Organization's tourist information and interpretation line. Dial 1330 from any Korean phone, or +82-2-1330 from abroad; English, Japanese and Chinese run 24 hours, and Russian, Vietnamese, Thai and Malay/Indonesian are also offered. It is free apart from the call, has a text-chat service, and will interpret three-way between you and a driver, a shop or a pharmacy.

**Q: Do taxi drivers speak English?**

Mostly not — taxi drivers skew older. You do not need them to: book with Kakao T and the destination appears to the driver in Korean, or show the address in Korean on your phone. If something goes wrong mid-ride, call 1330 and hand the phone over.

## Bottom Line

"Do Koreans speak English?" is the wrong question, because Korea is not one group. **Draw a line at about 35 and the answer is yes on one side and mostly no on the other**, and the line has a date on it: March 1997, when nine-year-olds started English and their parents' generation did not.

So ask the person under 35. Ask the real question, short, with the map up. Give them a second, and say 감사합니다 at the end. And for everything else — the subway, the taxi, the pharmacy in a small town — Korea has already built the English in; you only need to know that 1330 exists.
