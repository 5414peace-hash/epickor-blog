Ask which Korean instant noodle is the most famous and the answer is Shin Ramyun. Ask which one Koreans **actually buy most often**, and the answer changes.

In a national consumer survey published in 2020, the bagged ramyun Koreans reported buying most frequently was **Jin Ramen (진라면) at 26.4%**. On future purchase intent it also came first — **24% against Shin Ramyun's 20%.**

Jin Ramen is the bag foreigners walk past. It is also the one behind two things visitors notice without explaining: why the mild version turns up in Korean households with small children, and why the free self-serve ramyun at Korean restaurants is *always* this brand and never the famous one.

## Mild vs Spicy: The Soup Powder Settles It

Open both bags and empty them out. The noodle block is the same. The dried vegetable flake is nearly the same. **The soup powder is not.**

*순한맛 (mild). The powder is a pale, sandy tan. Photo by Mobius6, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

That colour shift is the entire difference, and it tells you what kind of difference it is. **These are not two recipes. They are one recipe with the chili dialled up.** The savoury base underneath — a beefy, slightly sweet, everyday broth — is shared.

This matters when you choose. With Ansungtangmyun versus Shin Ramyun the two soups are built on genuinely different foundations, soybean paste against chili, so heat is the wrong axis to compare them on. Jin Ramen is the opposite case: **heat really is the whole axis**, so picking a grade is a straightforward decision rather than a flavour gamble.

## There Is a Third Grade, and It Barely Leaves Korea

English coverage of Jin Ramen describes two options. Korea has three.

**약간매운맛** — *slightly spicy* — sits between mild and spicy, and it is a normal stocked product, not a novelty. As of August 2026 the slightly-spicy cup is listed on Korean convenience-store promotion sheets at the same **₩1,100** as the mild and spicy cups, in the same 3+1 and 2+1 deals.

It is also, in Korean search behaviour, **the most-searched Jin Ramen variant of the three.**

Its absence from English write-ups explains a small mystery: people describe Jin Ramen spicy as milder than expected, or mild as blander than expected, because the grade that would have suited them was never presented as an option. If you are shopping in Korea and neither extreme sounds right, ask for **약간매운맛**.

## Why the Free Ramyun in Korean Restaurants Is Always Jin Ramen

Eat at enough Korean all-you-can-eat grills, budget buffets or self-serve noodle bars and you notice something. Many of them put out free ramyun — a rack of packets, a hot-water station, help yourself.

**It is nearly always Jin Ramen.** Not Shin Ramyun, which is the more famous brand and would arguably be a better draw. Not Neoguri. Jin Ramen.

The reason is a pricing decision Ottogi made and then held for over a decade.

**Ottogi froze Jin Ramen's shipment price in April 2008 and did not raise it again until July 2021 — thirteen years and four months.** Through that period Korean food inflation ran on regardless and competitors raised prices repeatedly. Ottogi's stated reason was easing the burden on ordinary households, and it earned the company a nickname that is still in use: **갓뚜기**, a portmanteau of *God* and *Ottogi*.

When the freeze finally broke in July 2021, Jin Ramen went from **₩684 to ₩770** — a 12.6% rise, and Ottogi's first in thirteen years.

**The commercial consequence is the thing nobody writes down.** A price held flat for thirteen years made Jin Ramen the cheapest mainstream bag on the Korean shelf — not cheap in a budget-brand way, but the lowest-priced of the major manufacturers' flagship products. Ottogi also sells it in **catering bulk**, in 30-pack cases of 120 g bags aimed at food-service buyers.

So for a restaurant giving ramyun away, the arithmetic decides itself. An unlimited-refill business is a cost-control business. **The free ramyun is Jin Ramen because Jin Ramen is the cheapest bag a Korean caterer can put out that customers will still be happy to see.**

That is also why the brand's ubiquity is easy to underestimate. A lot of Koreans' Jin Ramen consumption never shows up in a retail number at all — it happens at a table where somebody else bought it.

## Why "Jin," and What the Purple Bags Are

**진 (jin)** is from the Chinese character 眞, meaning *true* or *genuine*. It is a quality claim, not a flavour word — the same instinct as calling something "the real thing."

The brand's origin is less tidy. Jin Ramen descends from **진곱배기**, made by **Cheongbo Food** from 1983. When Cheongbo went bankrupt in 1987, **Ottogi acquired the business**, and Jin Ramen launched in March 1988. It has carried both mild and spicy since that first day.

![The official Ottogi pack shot of Jin Ramen spicy, a yellow bag with the character 진 in red.](/assets/images/posts/361/jin-ramen-spicy-ottogi.jpg)

*The spicy bag: yellow ground, red 진, and 매운맛 in the corner. Product image: Ottogi.*

**And the purple question.** `jin ramen purple` and `jin ramen violet` are common English searches, and the answer is not a flavour.

In February 2023 Ottogi ran a **BT21 collaboration edition** with purple packaging across single bags and multipacks, carrying BT21 characters. BT21 is the character line created with the members of BTS — and BTS has a member named **Jin**, which is the same syllable as the noodle. The name collision is the whole reason the collaboration existed and the whole reason English speakers search for it.

The purple run also included the slightly-spicy grade, which pre-sold **1.5 times the prepared quantity** on Coupang in May before a formal September launch.

**So if you are hunting a purple bag: it is a limited collaboration edition, not a permanent heat level.** The permanent line is yellow.

## FAQ About Jin Ramen

**Q: What is the difference between Jin Ramen mild and spicy?**

**A:** The chili level, and essentially nothing else. Same noodle, same savoury base — the soup powder goes from pale tan to orange-red. Heat really is the whole axis here.

**Q: Which is the blue bag and which is the red?**

**A:** Both bags are yellow. The accent colour is the tell: blue means 순한맛 (mild), red means 매운맛 (spicy).

**Q: What is the purple Jin Ramen?**

**A:** A BT21 collaboration edition from February 2023, not a permanent flavour. BT21 is the character line made with BTS, whose member Jin shares the noodle's name. The regular product is yellow.

**Q: Is Jin Ramen spicier than Shin Ramyun?**

**A:** No. Jin Ramen spicy is moderate and sits below Shin Ramyun, which measured 3,400 SHU in 2022. Jin Ramen mild is not a spicy food at all.

**Q: Can children eat Jin Ramen mild?**

**A:** Korean households with small children buy it for exactly that reason, and Korean five-year-olds eat it. It is the standard choice when someone at the table cannot take heat.

**Q: Why do Korean restaurants give away Jin Ramen specifically?**

**A:** Cost. Ottogi held its price flat from April 2008 to July 2021, which left Jin Ramen the cheapest of the major makers' flagship bags, and it sells in catering bulk. For an unlimited-refill restaurant that decides it.

**Q: Is Jin Ramen more popular than Shin Ramyun in Korea?**

**A:** By purchase frequency, yes — a 2020 national survey put Jin Ramen first at 26.4%, and ahead on future purchase intent at 24% against 20%. Shin Ramyun remains far more famous internationally.

**Q: What is 약간매운맛?**

**A:** "Slightly spicy," the middle grade between mild and spicy. It is a normal stocked product in Korea at the same price as the other two, and it rarely appears on overseas shelves.
