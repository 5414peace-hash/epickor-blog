Some perfectly normal gifts mean something else in Korea. 🎁

Nobody is going to be offended by a foreigner, and plenty of Koreans do not observe these at all. But they are cheap to know, and the safer version is usually just as good a present.

✕ Shoes, for someone you are dating
→ The old superstition says the person walks away from you. Give an accessory, a dessert, or a shared experience instead.

✕ A name written in red ink
→ This one is worth actually following. Red names carry a death association and it is widely enough held that it reads as unpleasant rather than quirky. Any other colour.

✕ Knives or anything sharp, as a housewarming gift
→ Cutting tools can symbolise cutting the relationship. A kitchen towel, tea, or something consumable lands the same and carries nothing.

✕ A very expensive gift
→ Korean gift-giving runs on rough reciprocity, so a present far above what the relationship calls for obliges the other person to match it. Modest and well presented beats large and awkward.

✕ Handing it over one-handed
→ Use both hands if the setting is formal or the person is older than you — and take it back with both hands too. It is the single easiest piece of Korean etiquette to get visibly right.

And one that catches people out: a first refusal is not always a no. A soft "아니에요, 괜찮아요" can be politeness rather than a decision. Offer once more, warmly, then let it go. Do not push.
