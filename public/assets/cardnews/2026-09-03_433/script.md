**Main:** Clay against stone
**Sub:** **뚝배기** is fired clay and goes to stew, soup and steamed egg. **돌솥** is stone — granite or agalmatolite — and its job is rice, above all dolsot bibimbap. Different material, different food.

**Main:** It is a heat battery,\nnot a pan
**Sub:** Thick clay is slower to heat than steel — first-time owners think theirs is faulty — then holds a simmer for minutes off the burner. **Korean cooks pull it early on purpose**, and serve it in the pot, because moving it to a bowl throws the point away.

name_ko: 뚝배기
name_en: ttukbaegi — fired clay

**Main:** No dish detergent. Ever.
**Sub:** Korean writing calls the pores **숨구멍**, breathing holes. Detergent goes in, rinsing does not fully pull it back out, and a twenty-minute simmer draws it into the food. **Use detergent and you end up eating it.**

**Main:** Rice water, coarse salt,\nbaking soda
**Sub:** The cloudy water from rinsing rice: **its starch surrounds grease so it lifts and floats off.** A real mechanism, not folklore. Season a new pot the same way — simmer 쌀뜨물 gently for 10–20 minutes and let it cool in the pot. Koreans call it **길들이기**, taming it.

## Card 06
mode: stamp
kicker: THE TWO WAYS PEOPLE RUIN ONE
image: sources/obunjagi-ttukbaegi.jpg
fit: cover
image_position: center 50%
image_opacity: 0.86
image_label: Obunjagi ttukbaegi — Jeju abalone stew with shrimp and clams in a black earthenware pot

**Main:** Thermal shock cracks it.\nInduction ignores it.
**Sub:** Let it cool before it meets water — cold water on a hot pot is what splits these. And clay has **no magnetic layer**, so it sits on an induction hob doing nothing. Induction versions exist, but they are not pure clay. **If the box does not say induction, it is not.**

**Main:** Rice to the stone.\nStew to the clay.
**Sub:** The crisped rice at the bottom of a dolsot is the whole reason that bowl exists. **If you are buying one pot, buy the ttukbaegi** — it covers jjigae, guk, steamed egg and braises, which is most of what a Korean kitchen cooks.
