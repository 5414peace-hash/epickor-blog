Koreans never put dish soap in this pot. There is a material reason. 🍲

The 뚝배기 (ttukbaegi) is fired clay, and fired clay is porous. Korean writing calls the pores 숨구멍 — "breathing holes." Detergent goes into them, rinsing does not fully pull it back out, and the next time you simmer a stew for twenty minutes you draw it out again into the food.

Korean household guidance is blunt about it: use detergent and you end up eating it.

🥄 WHAT KOREANS USE INSTEAD
▪️ 쌀뜨물 — the cloudy water from rinsing rice. The starch surrounds grease so it lifts and floats away. A real mechanism, not folklore.
▪️ Coarse salt, scrubbed with a brush rather than a sponge that has met detergent.
▪️ Baking soda, simmered in water for stubborn residue.

🔥 IT IS A HEAT BATTERY, NOT A PAN
Thick clay is slower to heat than steel — first-time owners genuinely think theirs is faulty — and then holds a simmer for minutes after the flame is off. That is why Korean cooks pull a jjigae a touch early on purpose, and why the stew arrives at your table in the cooking pot. Moving it to a bowl throws the whole point away.

🪨 뚝배기 vs 돌솥, WHICH IS WHICH
▪️ 뚝배기 — fired clay. Jjigae, guk, steamed egg, braises.
▪️ 돌솥 — stone, usually granite or agalmatolite. Rice, above all dolsot bibimbap. The crisped rice at the bottom is the entire reason that bowl exists.

⚠️ THE TWO WAYS PEOPLE RUIN ONE
▪️ Cold water on a hot pot. Thermal shock is what cracks these — let it cool first.
▪️ Buying one for an induction hob. Clay has no magnetic layer, so it sits there doing nothing. Induction-compatible versions exist but they are not pure clay, and the box has to say so explicitly. If a listing does not mention induction, assume it does not.

🆕 SEASON A NEW ONE FIRST: 길들이기
Rinse in plain water, fill with 쌀뜨물, bring it up on a low flame, simmer 10–20 minutes, let it cool in the pot. Koreans call it "taming it." It closes the pores so the pot absorbs less and lasts longer.
