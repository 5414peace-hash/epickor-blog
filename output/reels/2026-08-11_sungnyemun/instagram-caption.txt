Everyone photographs this gate as 600 years old. The wood was cut in 2013. 🔥

On 10 February 2008 a 69-year-old man walked up to Sungnyemun with paint thinner and a lighter. He was furious about a land compensation payout. It burned for five hours, live on national television, and the country watched the roof come down.

The part that still stings: he had done it before. He set fire to a palace hall in 2006, got a suspended sentence, walked out — and told police he chose this gate next because the security was easy.

Putting it back took five years, about ₩27.67 billion and roughly 35,000 people.

One detail hiding in plain sight: of Seoul's great gates, only Sungnyemun's signboard is written vertically (崇禮門, top to bottom). The traditional explanation is geomancy — 禮 belongs to fire in the five elements, so the name was hung like a rising flame to press down the fire energy of Gwanaksan. Fire fought with fire. Then the gate burned anyway, and in 2008 the plaque itself fell and broke apart; it was restored in July 2009.

And the label your guidebook still uses? "National Treasure No. 1" stopped existing in November 2021, when Korea scrapped heritage designation numbers. The ranking was never Korean — it came off the colonial-era list. It's not number one. It's just Sungnyemun.
